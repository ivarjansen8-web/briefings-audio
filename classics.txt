Montaigne and the Art of Not Being Sure.

In 1571, at the age of 38, Michel de Montaigne gave up his post as a magistrate in Bordeaux and withdrew to a round tower on his family estate. He had a library built on the top floor, painted sayings from the classics onto its ceiling beams, and set out to study the one subject he thought he could actually get at: himself. France around him was being torn apart by the Wars of Religion, as Catholics and Protestants killed each other in the name of certainty. What Montaigne wrote in that tower became the Essays (first published 1580), and with them he invented a new form of writing.

The core idea.

The French word essai means a trial or an attempt. Montaigne's essays don't argue their way to firm conclusions. They try out a thought, follow it where it wanders, contradict it, and admit what they don't know. Titles like "Of Idleness," "Of Smells," and "Of Experience" sit beside "That to Philosophize Is to Learn to Die." In 1576 he had a medal struck with a pair of balanced scales and his motto: Que sais-je?, "What do I know?"

This is skepticism, but a gentle and practical kind. Montaigne isn't saying that nothing can be known. He's saying that people are changeable and inconsistent, that our habits feel like natural law only because they're ours, and that someone who truly sees this will be slower to condemn and much slower to kill. Even his cat got a hearing.

"When I play with my cat, who knows if I am not a pastime to her more than she is to me?"

An example.

In "Of Cannibals," Montaigne writes about the Tupinambá people of Brazil, some of whom he met in Rouen. Europeans called them savages. Montaigne turns the charge around. Yes, they ate their dead enemies, he concedes, but Europeans tortured living people on the rack and burned their neighbours alive over points of doctrine, and called it piety. His verdict: "Each man calls barbarism whatever is not his own practice." Coming from a 16th-century nobleman, it was a startling thing to say.

Why it still matters.

Montaigne is the patron saint of anyone who has ever thought, "I might be wrong about this." In an age of online certainty and instant outrage, his habit of testing his own opinions before defending them looks less like weakness and more like a discipline worth practising. Psychologists now talk about "intellectual humility," the willingness to recognise the limits of what you know, and link it to better judgment and less polarised thinking. Montaigne got there four centuries earlier, by watching himself honestly.

There's a personal lesson too. Montaigne thought that ordinary life, with its meals, moods, friendships and kidney stones, was worth paying attention to. "Every man," he wrote, "bears the whole form of the human condition." You don't need to be extraordinary to be worth understanding.

A good starting point: Sarah Bakewell's How to Live: A Life of Montaigne in One Question and Twenty Attempts at an Answer*, then the essays themselves in Donald Frame's or M. A. Screech's translation. Start with "Of Cannibals" and "Of Experience."*
