Thucydides and the Melian Dialogue: When Power Meets Justice.

Thucydides was an Athenian general who failed. In 424 BC he arrived too late to save the city of Amphipolis, and Athens exiled him for it. He spent the next twenty years watching the Peloponnesian War between Athens and Sparta from the outside, and writing its history. He wanted it to be, in his words, "a possession for all time" rather than a story to please the crowd. The most famous passage in it isn't a battle. It's a conversation.

The core idea.

In 416 BC an Athenian fleet sailed to Melos, a small island that wanted to stay neutral. Before attacking, the Athenian envoys sat down with the Melian leaders, and Thucydides reconstructs their exchange as a dramatic dialogue. The Athenians refuse to talk about right and wrong at all. Justice, they say, only comes into it between equals.

"The strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must."

The Melians try every argument they have. Fairness is in your interest too, they say, because one day you may fall and need it. The gods will protect the innocent. Sparta will come to help us. The Athenians brush each one aside. Hope is an expensive luxury for the weak, they reply, and as for the gods: "of men we know, and of the gods we believe, that by a law of their nature they rule wherever they can."

The Melians refused to surrender. Athens besieged the island, killed the men of military age, and sold the women and children into slavery. Thucydides adds no comment. He simply goes on to his next chapter, where the same confident Athenians launch their invasion of Sicily. Within two years it ended in total disaster.

Why it still matters.

The dialogue is the founding text of what political scientists call realism: the view that states act on power and self-interest, and that talk of justice between them is mostly decoration. Thomas Hobbes translated Thucydides into English before he wrote Leviathan, and diplomats and generals still study the passage, from war colleges to foreign ministries. Every time a large country tells a small neighbour that it has no real choice, the Melian Dialogue is being replayed.

But it's a mistake to read Thucydides as simply siding with Athens. By placing Melos right before Sicily, he invites a darker reading. A power that stops believing limits apply to it also loses the ability to see its own limits. The Melians' warning, that the strong may one day need the restraint they now dismiss, turns out to be the more realistic argument after all.

The lesson also works on a smaller scale. In offices and families, whoever holds the power is often tempted to treat "I can" as if it meant "I should." Thucydides suggests the people who reason that way are usually the last to notice when the tide turns.

A good starting point: Book 5, chapters 84 to 116 of The History of the Peloponnesian War*, in the Rex Warner (Penguin) or Richard Crawley translation, or* The Landmark Thucydides*, edited by Robert Strassler, which adds helpful maps. The dialogue takes about twenty minutes to read.*
