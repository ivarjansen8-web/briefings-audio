Montaigne and the Courage to Say "What Do I Know?"

In 1571, at thirty-seven, Michel de Montaigne retired from his career as a magistrate in Bordeaux to his family château. France was tearing itself apart in the Wars of Religion, with Catholics and Protestants each certain that God was on their side. Montaigne, a Catholic who stayed loyal to the crown, watched neighbours turn on one another over certainty. In his tower library, with Latin maxims carved into the ceiling beams, he began writing something no one had quite written before: the Essays, from the French essayer, "to try" or "to test."

The core idea is a disciplined humility. Montaigne drew on the ancient Greek skeptics, especially Sextus Empiricus, whose works had recently been rediscovered in Europe. His motto, struck on a medal, was Que sais-je? "What do I know?" This was not a shrug. For Montaigne, doubt was a tool for living well. If our senses deceive us, our reason is bent by habit and passion, and customs that seem natural in one country look barbaric in the next, then confident certainty is mostly a failure of imagination. In his essay on cannibals, he noted that Europeans condemned the eating of dead enemies while torturing living people in the name of religion, and he observed that "each man calls barbarism whatever is not his own practice."

The method matters as much as the conclusion. Montaigne turned his skepticism inward and made himself his subject. "I am myself the matter of my book," he told the reader in his opening note. He recorded his kidney stones, his poor memory, his changing moods, his friendship with the late Étienne de La Boétie, and his contradictions. He found himself inconsistent, and rather than hide it, he treated it as the honest truth about being human: we are, he wrote, "made of bits and pieces," never a single fixed self.

A concrete example: Montaigne sat in judgment on neighbours accused of witchcraft and, unlike many contemporaries, argued that it was "putting a very high price on one's conjectures" to burn a person alive for them. His doubt about his own certainty had a real moral edge. It made him lenient, curious, and slow to cruelty.

Why it matters now: we live in a time of loud, fast certainty, with feeds that reward the confident take and punish the qualified one. Psychologists call the tendency to overrate our own knowledge the illusion of explanatory depth, and Montaigne anticipated it by four centuries. His practice is simple and portable. Before defending a belief, ask how you actually came to hold it, and notice that someone raised elsewhere would likely believe otherwise. That is not a recipe for having no convictions. It is a recipe for holding them with open hands, which is often what keeps disagreement from becoming violence.

If you read one essay, try "Of Experience," his last. It ends with the quiet conclusion that the greatest thing is to know how to belong to oneself, and to live one's own life well.
