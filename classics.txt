Confucius and the Rectification of Names.

Around 500 BCE, China was breaking apart. The Zhou dynasty's authority had decayed into a patchwork of warring states, and the philosopher Kong Fuzi, known in the West as Confucius, spent years wandering between courts hoping to find a ruler who would listen. His sayings, gathered by students in the Analects, are less a system than a long reflection on one question: how do you rebuild a trustworthy society when its institutions have lost their meaning?

The core idea.

In one passage, a ruler's minister asks what Confucius would do first if given charge of government. His answer is surprising. He would "rectify the names" (zhengming). When names are not correct, he explains, speech does not accord with things; when speech does not accord with things, affairs cannot be carried out; and when affairs fail, the people "do not know where to put hand or foot."

The point is that words like ruler, minister, father and son carry obligations. A ruler who does not rule with care is a ruler in name only, and everyone who deals with him is then living inside a small lie. Confucius puts it in a compact line elsewhere: "Let the ruler be a ruler, the minister a minister, the father a father, the son a son." Social trust, for him, depends on titles and conduct matching.

An example.

Imagine a company whose "safety officer" is never allowed to stop production, or a "public servant" who serves only himself. Everyone knows the title is hollow, so everyone learns to read official language as decoration. Soon nobody can coordinate, because the words that are supposed to bind people no longer bind anything. That slow corrosion is what Confucius feared, and he thought no law could repair it until the words and the reality were brought back together.

Why it still matters.

The idea is strikingly modern. George Orwell worried about the same thing in "Politics and the English Language," when he argued that vague, dishonest words make dishonest thinking easier. You can see it in euphemism, in job titles that describe nothing, and in political slogans that say the opposite of what is done. Confucius gives a more constructive angle: the repair starts with honesty about roles. Ask what a title, a promise or a role actually requires of you, and then try to live up to the word.

A good starting point: Book 13 of the Analects*, which contains the passage on names, in the translation by Edward Slingerland or Annping Chin.*
