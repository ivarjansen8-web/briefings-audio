Thucydides and the Melian Dialogue: The Strong Do What They Can.

In 424 BC an Athenian general named Thucydides arrived too late to save the city of Amphipolis from the Spartans. Athens punished him with twenty years of exile. He used the time to write the History of the Peloponnesian War, the long struggle between Athens and Sparta, and he set out to explain not just what happened but why people and states behave as they do. He hoped, he said, that his book would be "a possession for all time". It has turned out that way, largely because of one short, chilling scene.

The core idea.

In 416 BC an Athenian fleet anchored off Melos, a small island that wanted to stay neutral in the war. Before attacking, the Athenian envoys sat down with the Melian leaders, and Thucydides reconstructs their conversation as a debate.

The Athenians refuse to dress things up. They won't pretend they have a just cause, and they ask the Melians not to waste time arguing about fairness either. Justice, they say, only comes into play between equals.

"Right, as the world goes, is only in question between equals in power, while the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must." (trans. Richard Crawley)

The Melians try every argument they have. Destroying a neutral island will turn other states against Athens. The gods favour the just. Sparta will surely come to help its kin. The Athenians answer each one coldly: the gods and men alike rule wherever they can, Sparta looks after its own interests, and hope is a luxury that only the strong can afford. The Melians choose freedom over surrender. Athens besieges the city, kills the men and sells the women and children into slavery.

Thucydides offers no comment. He simply moves on to the next year, when a confident Athens launches a vast invasion of Sicily that ends in catastrophe. Readers have been left to draw the connection themselves: a power that believes it answers to nothing may be heading for a fall.

Why it still matters.

The Melian Dialogue is the founding text of "realism" in international relations, the school of thought, associated with Hans Morgenthau and Kenneth Waltz, which holds that states act on power and interest rather than morality. It is assigned in military academies and diplomatic schools worldwide. The political scientist Graham Allison has drawn on the same history for the idea of the "Thucydides Trap": the risk of war when a rising power threatens an established one.

Yet the dialogue cuts both ways. It can be read as a hard-headed warning to small states not to count on the kindness of strangers, or as a portrait of how power corrupts the people who hold it. That tension is why it endures. Whenever a large country pressures a small one, or anyone with leverage says "let's be realistic", the Athenians and the Melians are still arguing.

A good starting point: Book 5, chapters 84 to 116 of the History*, which you can read in twenty minutes, in Crawley's classic translation or in* The Landmark Thucydides edited by Robert Strassler, which comes with excellent maps.
