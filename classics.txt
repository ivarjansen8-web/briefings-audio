Hannah Arendt and the Banality of Evil.

In 1961, the political theorist Hannah Arendt travelled to Jerusalem to cover the trial of Adolf Eichmann for The New Yorker. Eichmann had been a senior SS official who organised the deportation of Jews to the death camps. Arendt was herself a German-Jewish refugee who had fled the Nazis, studied under Heidegger and Jaspers, and spent years thinking about how totalitarianism was possible. She expected to meet a monster. What she found unsettled her more.

The man in the glass booth was not a raging fanatic or a sadist. He was a diligent bureaucrat, fond of clichés, anxious about his career, and strikingly unable to see things from another person's point of view. Her 1963 book Eichmann in Jerusalem gave the phenomenon a name that has been argued over ever since: "the banality of evil."

The phrase is often misunderstood. Arendt did not claim that the Holocaust was banal, or that Eichmann was innocent. She argued that the person behind enormous crimes need not be demonic. What marked Eichmann, in her account, was thoughtlessness: a failure to stop and ask what he was actually doing. He spoke in stock phrases and official language, and he took refuge in following orders and doing his job well. Language that sounds respectable can shield us from the reality of our actions.

A concrete moment from the book: Eichmann, awaiting execution, offered conventional pious remarks, and Arendt noted that even in the face of death he reached for ready-made phrases rather than any thought of his own. For her, this was the point. Evil on a vast scale had been made possible less by burning hatred than by ordinary people who stopped thinking and exercising judgment.

For Arendt, thinking is a moral act. It means holding an inner conversation with oneself, imagining how others experience the world, and refusing to hide behind roles or slogans. A person who never does this can be drawn into almost anything, provided the surrounding system makes it routine.

Why does this still matter? Arendt's idea is a warning about the everyday machinery of institutions: the clerk who processes the form, the employee who follows a policy they know is harmful, the citizen who repeats a talking point without examining it. Modern psychology echoes her intuition. Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments, run in the same years, showed how readily ordinary people comply when responsibility is diffused. The takeaway is not that we are all villains, but that conscience needs exercise. Pausing to ask "what am I really doing?" is a small habit, and in Arendt's view it is one of the last defences we have.

Suggested reading: Arendt's Eichmann in Jerusalem (1963) and her essay "Thinking and Moral Considerations."
