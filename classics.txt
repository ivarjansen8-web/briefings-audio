Festinger and the Prophecy That Failed: Cognitive Dissonance.

In the autumn of 1954, a Chicago-area woman named Dorothy Martin announced that beings from the planet Clarion had sent her a warning. A great flood would destroy much of the world before dawn on 21 December, and a flying saucer would arrive at midnight to rescue the faithful. Her followers quit their jobs, gave away their possessions and waited. Among them, posing as believers, were three social psychologists led by Leon Festinger, then a young professor at the University of Minnesota. They wanted to see what happens to people when reality flatly contradicts what they believe.

The core idea.

Midnight came and went. No saucer appeared, and no flood followed. What happened next was the surprise. The group did not quietly give up. After hours of distress, Martin received a new message: their faith had been so strong that God had spared the world. The group, which had previously avoided publicity, started calling newspapers and preaching to anyone who would listen.

Festinger called the underlying force cognitive dissonance: the mental discomfort we feel when we hold two ideas that clash, such as "I gave up everything for this" and "this was false." We are driven to reduce that discomfort, and the easiest way is often not to change the belief but to change how we see the facts, or to recruit others so that the belief feels confirmed. He set out the case in When Prophecy Fails (1956), which opens.

"A man with a conviction is a hard man to change. Tell him you disagree and he turns away. Show him facts or figures and he questions your sources. Appeal to logic and he fails to see your point."

He then tested the idea in the lab. In a famous 1959 experiment with James Carlsmith, students did an hour of mind-numbing tasks, then were paid either 1 dollarsor 20 dollarsto tell the next participant it had been fun. Afterwards, the 1 dollarsgroup rated the task as more enjoyable. The 20 dollarsstudents had a good reason for lying. The 1 dollarsstudents didn't, so they quietly adjusted their opinion to match what they'd said.

Why it still matters.

Dissonance is everywhere once you look for it. It's why we defend a car we overpaid for, why hazing makes people more loyal to a club, and why a smoker finds reasons to doubt the health statistics. In politics, it helps explain why evidence against a cause can sometimes harden support instead of weakening it, since admitting the error would mean admitting the cost of having believed.

The original study deserves some caution: later historians have questioned how much the undercover observers themselves shaped the group's behaviour. But the theory has become one of the most influential in social psychology. Its practical lesson is uncomfortable and useful: the more you have invested in a belief, the less you should trust your own sense of how strong the evidence for it is.

A good starting point: Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson's Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me), a lively modern tour of dissonance, then When Prophecy Fails itself.
