Here is your classics report for Sunday, 8 November: Simone Weil and the radical generosity of attention.

In a world engineered to capture your eyes, one of the twentieth century's most uncompromising thinkers argued that the highest thing you can give another person is simply your attention.

Simone Weil lived from 1909 to 1943. She was a French philosopher, factory worker, wartime volunteer and mystic who died at thirty-four, partly because she refused to eat more than the rations her countrymen in occupied France were receiving. Trained at the elite École Normale Supérieure, she left the classroom to work on assembly lines, convinced that you cannot think honestly about suffering from a distance. Her notebooks and essays, collected after her death in books such as Gravity and Grace and Waiting for God, are fragmentary, fierce and strangely luminous.

One of her most striking ideas comes from a short essay on school studies. Weil argues that the real purpose of studying, even geometry or Latin grammar, is not the knowledge itself but the training of attention. Attention, for her, is not straining or effort. It is a kind of suspended thought: an open, receptive mind that lets the object enter as it truly is, rather than as our assumptions shape it. A student who struggles with a maths problem and fails to solve it has still gained something, because the effort strengthens the very faculty that later makes prayer, love and justice possible.

From this she draws a startling conclusion about compassion. In a letter to the writer Joë Bousquet she wrote: "Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity." To ask a suffering person what they are going through, and truly listen, without rushing to fix, judge or relate it to yourself, is to recognise them as a full human being rather than a case or a category. Someone in affliction does not need our advice or pity so much as the sense that we are really looking at them.

The idea fits our moment almost uncomfortably well. We live in an economy that treats attention as something to be harvested, and many of us feel our concentration fragmenting. Weil suggests the cost is more than lost productivity. If attention is the root of both learning and love, a distracted culture risks becoming a less compassionate one. Psychologists who study active listening and therapeutic presence echo her point that being fully heard changes people.

The takeaway is modest and demanding. Today, choose one conversation and give it your whole mind: phone away, no rehearsing your reply. Notice how hard it is, and notice what the other person does when they feel truly seen.

As Weil put it: "Absolutely unmixed attention is prayer."
