Here is your classics report for Sunday, 8 November.

Simone Weil and the radical generosity of attention. In a world engineered to capture your eyes, one of the twentieth century's most uncompromising thinkers argued that the highest thing you can give another person is simply your attention.

Who she was. Simone Weil, who lived from 1909 to 1943, was a French philosopher, factory worker, wartime volunteer and mystic. She died at thirty-four, partly because she refused to eat more than the rations she believed her countrymen in occupied France were receiving. Trained at the elite École Normale Supérieure, she left the classroom to work on assembly lines, convinced that you cannot think honestly about human suffering from a distance. Her notebooks and essays, gathered after her death in works such as Gravity and Grace and Waiting for God, are fragmentary, fierce and strangely luminous.

The idea. In a short essay on the right use of school studies, Weil argues that the real purpose of studying, even geometry or Latin grammar, is not the knowledge itself but the training of attention. Attention, in her sense, is not straining or muscular effort. It is a suspension of thought: a readiness to receive, leaving your mind empty and open, so that the object can enter it as it truly is, rather than as your preconceptions shape it. A student who struggles over a math problem and fails to solve it has still gained something, she says, because the effort of attending strengthens the very faculty that later makes prayer, love and justice possible.

Compassion. From this she draws a startling conclusion. In a letter to the writer Joë Bousquet she writes: "Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity." To ask a suffering person, "What are you going through?", and truly listen, without rushing to fix, judge or relate it to yourself, is to recognise them as a full human being rather than a case or a category. Someone in affliction does not need our advice or pity so much as the sense that we are looking at them, really looking, and seeing them as they are.

Why it matters now. We live in an economy that treats attention as a commodity to be harvested, and many of us feel our concentration fragmenting. Weil's insight suggests the cost is more than lost productivity. If attention is the root of both learning and love, then a distracted culture risks becoming a less compassionate one. Psychologists studying active listening, mindfulness and therapeutic presence echo her claim that being fully heard changes people.

The takeaway. Today, choose one conversation and give it your whole mind: phone away, no rehearsing your reply. Notice how hard it is, and notice what the other person does when they feel truly seen.

As Weil put it: "Absolutely unmixed attention is prayer."
