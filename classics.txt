Festinger and Cognitive Dissonance: When Prophecy Fails.

In late 1954 a Chicago woman named Dorothy Martin announced that beings from the planet Clarion had sent her a warning: on 21 December a great flood would destroy much of the world, but true believers would be rescued by a flying saucer. A small group gathered around her. Some quit their jobs, gave away their possessions and left their spouses. Among the faithful were also a few newcomers who weren't believers at all. They were researchers sent by the social psychologist Leon Festinger, who wanted to answer a simple question: what happens to people when the prophecy fails?

The core idea.

The answer, published in When Prophecy Fails (1956), surprised almost everyone. Midnight came and went with no saucer. Early the next morning Martin received a new message: the group's faith had been so strong that God had spared the Earth. The believers didn't drift away in embarrassment. Instead, a group that had previously avoided publicity began phoning newspapers and actively looking for converts.

Festinger's explanation became one of the most influential ideas in psychology: cognitive dissonance. Holding two thoughts that clash, such as "I gave up everything for this belief" and "the belief was false", produces real mental discomfort, and we are strongly motivated to make it go away. The easiest fix is rarely to admit we were wrong, especially when we have invested heavily. It is often easier to reinterpret the facts and recruit others to agree, because if more people believe it, it must be true. The book opens with a line that still rings true.

"A man with a conviction is a hard man to change. Tell him you disagree and he turns away. Show him facts or figures and he questions your sources. Appeal to logic and he fails to see your point."

In 1959 Festinger and James Carlsmith tested the idea in the lab. Students spent an hour on mind-numbing tasks, such as turning pegs on a board, and were then paid either 1 dollarsor 20 dollarsto tell the next participant it had been fun. Afterwards, those paid only 1 dollarsrated the task as more enjoyable. The 20 dollarsgroup had a good reason for lying. The 1 dollarsgroup didn't, so they quietly adjusted their memory of the task instead.

Why it still matters.

Dissonance helps explain why investors hold on to losing stocks, why people defend a bad purchase most fiercely just after making it, and why hazing makes club members more loyal rather than less. It also explains why political arguments so rarely change minds: evidence that threatens someone's identity tends to feel like an attack rather than information.

Festinger's lesson works in both directions. When someone digs in against the facts, they are often protecting a past investment, not being stupid, so leaving them a dignified way to change their mind does more good than piling on proof. And when you feel that sudden urge to explain away an inconvenient fact, it is worth asking what you would have to admit if you didn't.

A good starting point: When Prophecy Fails by Festinger, Riecken and Schachter, which reads like a strange true-crime story, or Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson's Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me), a lively modern tour of dissonance in everyday life.
