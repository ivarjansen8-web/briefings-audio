Tolstoy's Ivan Ilyich and the Terror of the Ordinary Life.

Leo Tolstoy had already written War and Peace and Anna Karenina when, in his fifties, he went through a spiritual crisis that left him doubting the worth of his own fame and comfort. The Death of Ivan Ilyich (1886), a short novella, is the sharpest product of that crisis. It is a book about a man who does everything right and discovers, too late, that he may have lived wrongly.

Ivan Ilyich is a Russian judge, capable, agreeable and careful. He climbs the career ladder, marries suitably, furnishes a fashionable apartment and plays cards with the right people. Tolstoy's famous verdict on him comes in one line: "Ivan Ilyich's life had been most simple and most ordinary and therefore most terrible." While hanging curtains, he falls and bruises his side. The pain refuses to go away, doctors speak in pompous evasions, and he slowly understands that he is dying.

The core idea is not the fear of death itself but what death exposes. Ivan Ilyich has a syllogism from his school logic textbook: Caius is a man, men are mortal, therefore Caius is mortal. It had always been true of Caius, never of him, Ivan, with his particular feelings and habits. Facing his own end, he is forced to ask whether his life, so correct and so respectable, was real at all. Everyone around him, including his wife and colleagues, treats his illness as an inconvenience and maintains the polite fiction that he will recover. His deepest suffering is this lie, and the loneliness it creates. Only the peasant servant Gerasim, who simply accepts that his master is dying and cheerfully holds up his legs to ease the pain, offers him real comfort.

At the end, Ivan Ilyich lets go of his self-justification, feels pity for his family instead of resentment, and in the final lines the fear of death dissolves: "Death is finished," he says to himself. "It is no more!"

Why does this matter now? Psychologists who study end-of-life regret and existential therapy, such as Irvin Yalom, repeat Tolstoy's insight: people tend to regret conformity more than failure. The book also works as a mild warning for a culture of résumés and curated feeds, where the measure of a good life is easily outsourced to what looks impressive. A practical way to use Tolstoy is to ask, occasionally and without panic, which parts of your routine you chose and which you simply inherited. You do not need a fatal illness to run the experiment, which is exactly his point.

The novella takes about two hours to read and is widely available in the Maude translation.
