The Banality of Evil: Hannah Arendt in Jerusalem.

In 1961, the philosopher Hannah Arendt, a German-Jewish refugee who had fled the Nazis and rebuilt her life in New York, travelled to Jerusalem to watch the trial of Adolf Eichmann. Eichmann had organised the logistics of deporting millions of Jews to the death camps. Arendt expected to meet a monster. Her reports for The New Yorker, later collected as Eichmann in Jerusalem (1963), described something far more unsettling.

The core idea.

What Arendt saw in the glass booth was an ordinary, careerist bureaucrat. He spoke in clichés and recycled stock phrases, and he seemed unable to see the situation from anyone else's point of view. He was not driven by burning hatred or demonic ideology. He wanted to do his job well and be promoted. She called this "the banality of evil." She did not mean that the crimes were small. She meant that their source was shallow: a failure to think.

For Arendt, thinking is not intelligence or cleverness. It is the habit of stopping to hold an inner dialogue with yourself, asking what you are actually doing and whether you could live with it. Eichmann, she argued, had let thinking be replaced by procedure, slogans and obedience. When the state declared the Final Solution to be the law, he treated that as the end of the matter. She later put the point in a line from The Life of the Mind.

"The sad truth is that most evil is done by people who never make up their minds to be good or evil."

The context.

Her argument was controversial from the start. Many readers felt she was excusing Eichmann or minimising the Nazi project. Historians have since debated whether she took his courtroom performance too much at face value, and she was perhaps too quick to dismiss his real antisemitism. But her central question has outlasted these disputes: how do ordinary people take part in atrocity without feeling like villains?

Why it still matters.

Arendt's insight is that moral catastrophe does not always require fanatics. It can run on people who follow the process, hit the target, say "that's not my department," and hide behind a job title. You can see echoes of this in any large institution where responsibility is spread so thin that nobody feels it: a company covering up a defect, a system processing people as cases, a crowd repeating a talking point because everyone else is.

Her remedy is modest and personal. It is not heroism, just the discipline of pausing to think, of asking "what am I actually doing here?" before accepting the answer "what I was told."

A good starting point: Arendt's essay "Personal Responsibility Under Dictatorship," which develops these ideas in a short, readable form.
