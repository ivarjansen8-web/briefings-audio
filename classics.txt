Tolstoy's Ivan Ilyich and the Life Lived Correctly.

By the late 1870s, Leo Tolstoy had written War and Peace and Anna Karenina and was famous, rich and respected. He was also close to suicide. The question that tormented him, as he described it in his Confession, was simple: what meaning does my life have that death will not destroy? Out of that crisis came a short novel, The Death of Ivan Ilyich (1886). It runs to about sixty pages and may be the most unsettling thing he ever wrote.

The story.

Ivan Ilyich Golovin is a successful judge in St Petersburg. He has done everything right. He studied law, climbed the ranks, married suitably, furnished his apartment in the fashionable style and played whist with the right people. Tolstoy sums him up in one of literature's great opening verdicts.

"Ivan Ilyich's life had been most simple and most ordinary and therefore most terrible."

One day, while hanging curtains, he bumps his side. A dull pain begins, and grows. Doctors give him confident diagnoses that explain nothing. His wife and colleagues treat his illness as an inconvenience and keep up the polite fiction that he will get better. Only Gerasim, a young peasant servant, is honest with him. Gerasim holds Ivan's legs up on his shoulders through the night to ease the pain, without complaint, because, as he says simply, we will all die someday.

The core idea.

As he dies, Ivan is forced to ask a question he has spent his whole life avoiding: "What if my whole life has really been wrong?" His life wasn't wicked. It was correct. He had lived by what was proper and pleasant, what his social circle approved of, and in doing so he had avoided anything real: love, honesty, the fact of death itself. The lie that everyone tells him about his illness is the same lie he told himself for forty-five years.

Tolstoy's point is that a conventional life can be a way of not living at all. In the final pages, Ivan stops defending his past, feels pity for his family, and finds that his fear of death is gone. "In place of death there was light."

Why it still matters.

The novella has had a long second life. Heidegger cited it when he wrote about how people hide from death in the chatter of "what one does." Doctors and nurses read it in medical training to learn how isolating false cheerfulness can be for a dying patient. Palliative care nurse Bronnie Ware's widely shared list of the regrets of the dying begins with Ivan's regret exactly: "I wish I'd had the courage to live a life true to myself, not the life others expected of me."

The question doesn't need a deathbed. It is worth asking on an ordinary Friday: which parts of my life do I choose, and which parts do I just do because they're correct?

A good starting point: the novella itself, in the Maude or Pevear and Volokhonsky translation. It can be read in a single evening.
