Montaigne and "Que sais-je?": The Art of Suspended Judgment.

In 1571, at the age of 38, a French magistrate named Michel de Montaigne gave up his public career and retired to a round tower on his family estate near Bordeaux. Outside, France was tearing itself apart in the Wars of Religion between Catholics and Protestants; the following year, thousands of Protestants would be murdered in the St Bartholomew's Day massacre. Inside his library, Montaigne painted sayings from ancient sceptics onto the ceiling beams and began writing short pieces about whatever occupied his mind: friendship, cruelty, sleep, smells, cannibals, his kidney stones. He called them essais, meaning "attempts" or "trials", and in doing so invented a new literary form. The first edition appeared in 1580, and he kept revising and adding to it until his death in 1592.

The core idea.

Around 1576 Montaigne had a medal struck with a pair of balanced scales and a motto in French: Que sais-je? "What do I know?" It sums up his outlook. Montaigne wasn't claiming that nothing can be known. He was practising what the ancient sceptics called suspending judgment: noticing how often our certainties are just habit, mood or local custom, and holding them more loosely as a result.

His favourite subject for this experiment was himself. "I am myself the matter of my book," he warns the reader in the preface. By watching his own mind closely, he found it changeable, contradictory and easily swayed, and concluded that everyone else's probably was too. Even his cat became a lesson in perspective.

"When I play with my cat, who knows if I am not a pastime to her more than she is to me?"

His most famous application of this habit is the essay "Of Cannibals". Europeans of his day were horrified by reports of the Tupinambá people of Brazil, who ate their dead enemies. Montaigne agreed that was barbaric, but pointed out that his own countrymen tortured and burned living people over points of theology, which seemed to him far worse. "Each man," he wrote, "calls barbarism whatever is not his own practice."

Why it still matters.

Montaigne's direct heirs include Shakespeare, who borrowed from "Of Cannibals" for The Tempest, as well as Descartes, Emerson, Virginia Woolf and every columnist or blogger who has turned an ordinary afternoon into a reflection on how to live.

His deeper legacy is a temperament. Psychologists now study "intellectual humility", the willingness to recognise that one's views might be wrong, and find it linked to better reasoning and less hostility towards opponents. Montaigne arrived at the same idea in the middle of a civil war, surrounded by people who were completely sure they were right and prepared to kill for it. In an age of polarised feeds and instant verdicts, "What do I know?" remains one of the most useful questions you can ask before you speak.

A good starting point: Sarah Bakewell's How to Live: A Life of Montaigne in One Question and Twenty Attempts at an Answer*, then the essays themselves in Donald Frame's or M. A. Screech's translation. Start with "Of Cannibals", "Of Friendship" and "Of Experience".*
