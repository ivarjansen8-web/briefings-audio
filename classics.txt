Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich: The Lie Everyone Agrees to Tell.

By the late 1870s Leo Tolstoy had everything a writer could want. War and Peace and Anna Karenina had made him famous, he had a large estate and a big family, and he was in good health. Yet he was close to suicide. He described the crisis in A Confession: if death erases everything, what is the point of any of it? He emerged with a new, stripped-down Christian faith and a distrust of comfortable, conventional lives. In 1886 he put the whole struggle into a novella of barely sixty pages.

The core idea.

Ivan Ilyich Golovin is a respectable judge in St Petersburg. He has a sensible career, a suitable marriage and a tastefully decorated apartment. Tolstoy's verdict comes in one of literature's most cutting sentences.

"Ivan Ilyich's life had been most simple and most ordinary and therefore most terrible."

While hanging curtains, Ivan slips and bangs his side. The small injury turns into a mysterious, fatal illness. The real horror of the story, though, is not the pain. It is the lie. His wife, his colleagues and his doctors all pretend he is just unwell and will soon recover, because the truth would be awkward and would interrupt their card games. Ivan joins in, and he is lonelier for it.

He remembers a syllogism from his school logic book: "Caius is a man, men are mortal, therefore Caius is mortal." It had always seemed perfectly correct about Caius. It had never seemed to apply to him, little Vanya, who loved the smell of his mother's leather ball. Knowing that people die and believing that you will die turn out to be very different things.

The only person who doesn't pretend is Gerasim, a young peasant servant. He cheerfully holds his master's legs up through the night to ease the pain and says simply that everyone dies, so why shouldn't he help? In his final hours Ivan finally admits that his life was "not the right thing", feels pity for his family instead of resentment, and the fear lifts. "In place of death there was light."

Why it still matters.

The novella has become a set text in medical schools. The surgeon Atul Gawande opens his book Being Mortal (2014) with Ivan Ilyich, arguing that modern medicine often repeats the family's lie by offering one more treatment instead of an honest conversation about what a dying patient actually wants. Psychologists see the same pattern too: Ernest Becker's The Denial of Death and the research it inspired suggest that much ordinary behaviour, from status-chasing to tribal loyalty, is partly a way of keeping our own mortality at arm's length.

Tolstoy's question isn't really about dying. It is whether the life you are building would still look right if you had to examine it honestly, the way Ivan finally did. He suggests it's better not to wait until the end to ask.

A good starting point: the novella itself, in the Louise and Aylmer Maude or Richard Pevear and Larissa Volokhonsky translation. It can be read in a single evening.
