Here is your Rwanda and Congo briefing for Sunday, 22 November.

Washington peace track stalls over FDLR as US sanctions on Rwanda bite and Tshisekedi's referendum law fuels opposition.

Compiled from sources dated 5 to 18 November 2026 (older background where noted). Items marked (unconfirmed) are single-source or early reports. Limitation: article pages could not be fetched in this session, so this briefing rests on search-result summaries only, and nothing dated in the last 24 hours was found.

1. Top stories.

The Washington peace track is stalled, and a further round was reportedly due in Geneva on 19 November. Al Jazeera says the two sides remain divided over security commitments, chiefly the sequencing of Kinshasa's neutralisation of the FDLR against Rwanda lifting its "defensive measures". The outcome of the Geneva round was not found.

Washington has reportedly sanctioned Rwanda's defence ministry (unconfirmed date and scope). The Washington Post and ABC News are dated 28 October; the Treasury is quoted as saying the ministry deployed thousands of troops in North and South Kivu. Other outlets refer to earlier March 2026 sanctions on the Rwanda Defence Force, so this may be a repeat or a widening.

Tshisekedi signed a referendum law that the opposition says opens the way to a third term. Africanews reported the signing on 11 November and the opposition's vow to resist on 12 November.

The UN agencies project a record 30.3 million Congolese in Crisis-level hunger or worse for January-June 2027. They name eastern DRC as the sharpest concentration of need, alongside Ebola and cholera outbreaks.

2. Eastern DRC front lines.

Little current battlefield reporting surfaced for the last 72 hours. The most recent items describe continuing clashes between AFC/M23 and FARDC and Wazalendo around Masisi (Katoyi, Osso-Banyungu) and in the Minembwe highlands of South Kivu, with FARDC airstrikes on Malemo and Luola reported on 8 September.

Fighting in the Minembwe highlands has displaced over 200,000 civilians since early February 2026, according to ACAPS. This is a cumulative figure, not a new one.

M23 left Uvira in January 2026 under US pressure but still holds positions north of the city and in the southwestern highlands, per Human Rights Watch. HRW also reports Wazalendo harassment and abductions of Banyamulenge in Uvira.

The AFC announced its dissolution on 12 October into a new "Front pour la refondation de la nation" (FRN), which takes over its Doha commitments. The status of M23 as an armed movement stays unclear; this comes from a single search summary (unconfirmed).

3. Peace process.

The Washington track hinges on FDLR neutralisation. Kinshasa says it began in September, but Al Jazeera reports no notable disarmament or repatriation of FDLR members has been observed.

M23 is not party to the Washington accord, and the Doha process is described as stalled. Rwanda has promised to stop supporting M23, which Kigali denies ever doing; Kinshasa keeps alleging it.

In August, Kinshasa and AFC/M23 agreed a roadmap and a periodic review mechanism in Swiss talks. Implementation of prisoner exchange, ceasefire and humanitarian measures remains difficult.

The Doha Framework itself dates from 15 November 2025 and set out eight protocols, not a final settlement.

No AU, EAC or SADC developments were found in the window.

4. Rwanda.

Kigali's position is that it acts in self-defence and denies backing M23. Rwanda says Congolese forces and hostile militias, including the FDLR, are the threat.

The US Treasury, per ABC, says the Rwandan defence ministry supplies, trains and equips M23, and Treasury Secretary Bessent demanded withdrawal of troops from eastern DRC. These are Washington's claims; Kigali rejects them.

Rwanda's state-aligned New Times covered Washington oversight meetings as routine progress reviews (government-aligned outlet).

No domestic Rwandan political news was found in the window.

5. Kinshasa politics.

Tshisekedi has enacted the referendum law; the opposition coalition says it aims to allow unlimited terms. The C64 coalition (Fayulu, Katumbi, Kabund, Sesanga) demands his resignation and has rejected his offer of national dialogue.

The Constitutional Court ruled in July that the law complied with the constitution while raising concerns about some provisions.

Analysts note the opposition has struggled to draw large street crowds.

Background on the 2028 vote fight.

6. Burundi and Uganda.

Quiet: nothing significant in the last 72 hours.

Background: M23 claimed in December 2025 to have captured hundreds of Burundian soldiers, and some 90,000 Congolese fled to Burundi after the Uvira offensive; the border reopened in February 2026.

Uganda has troops in Bunia, Ituri, under an arrangement with Kinshasa.

7. Minerals, trade and the economy.

The December 2025 US-DRC and US-Rwanda frameworks remain the architecture, with Kinshasa listing critical minerals and gold assets as a strategic reserve and notifying Washington of cobalt quota changes.

Kinshasa offered the M23-held Rubaya coltan mine to the US in February. The UN estimates rebels collect at least 800,000 dollarsa month there.

The US Treasury sanctioned a network smuggling M23 minerals to Rwanda, and a Rwandan gold refinery, per its press releases (date reported as 25 June).

No new minerals-deal news was found in the last 72 hours.

8. Humanitarian situation and human rights.

Joint UN statement of 12 to 13 November: 30.3 million projected in acute food insecurity, January-June 2027.

Cholera has killed about 1,800 nationally, according to a 5 November report, with fears the rainy season spreads it to Kinshasa (unconfirmed, single source).

ACAPS lists an Ebola outbreak of 10,532 confirmed cases and 5,126 deaths across eight provinces as of 1 November (unconfirmed, I could not verify this scale).

WFP says it needs 214 dollarsmillion for eastern DRC through February 2027 and counts 7.8 million internally displaced.

9. International reactions and sanctions.

US sanctions on Rwanda's defence ministry (28 October) are the latest reported step, following March sanctions on the RDF and its officers. Critics such as the Oakland Institute call them symbolic.

Egmont Institute asks what comes next after the sanctions on the RDF.
