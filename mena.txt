Here is your Middle East briefing for Friday, 13 November.

Calibrated escalation: IRGC hits back in Bahrain as Gulf allies hold Trump's hand and the Houthis target Saudi Arabia's Hormuz bypass.

Today's main story is calibrated escalation without a decision. Iran's Revolutionary Guards said they hit a US early-warning radar in Bahrain overnight. It is their first claimed strike on a US asset in a Gulf state since early September, and they framed it as retaliation for a strike on a telecom tower in Sirik. Hours earlier, Donald Trump said Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait, the UAE and Bahrain had asked him to hold off on further strikes. At the same time the Houthis are working through Saudi Arabia's Hormuz bypass, with claimed hits on the East-West Pipeline, Yanbu and an Aramco site in Riyadh. That bypass now carries roughly 4 million barrels a day. Meanwhile the IRGC says more tankers that ignored its "traffic control" have exploded in the Strait. Elsewhere, Israel's caretaker period is becoming a constitutional fight over war powers while Gadi Eisenkot tries to build a minority government. A strike on Nasser Hospital and a baby's death from cold show how hollow Gaza's year-old ceasefire has become. The PUK's walkout from the Kurdistan Regional Government and the end of MINURSO in Western Sahara are two slower crises that are hardening into structural ones.

Top stories.

1. IRGC claims strike on US radar in Bahrain as Trump says Gulf allies asked him to hold fire.

What happened. Iran's Revolutionary Guards said their aerospace force targeted, and by some accounts "destroyed", a US early-warning radar in Bahrain. They called it retaliation for an attack on a telecommunications tower in Sirik, in Hormozgan province, on Thursday. Reuters-syndicated coverage calls it the first IRGC claim of an attack on US military assets in a Gulf country since early September. Neither Washington nor Manama confirmed it immediately. The statement came through the IRGC-affiliated Fars agency. Iranian state media reported the Sirik strike but gave no independent account of its cause or damage. The script is familiar: in June 2026 the IRGC made an almost identical claim against the Fifth Fleet headquarters after a Sirik telecom tower was hit.

On Thursday 12 November, in an interview with Stephen A. Smith at the White House, Trump said Gulf allies had asked him to pause further strikes on Iran. He named Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait, the UAE and Bahrain. "Saudi Arabia has asked me to please just let it be the way it is now for a while," he said. He added, "I'm not sure I agree with it," and when asked whether he would otherwise have kept attacking, answered "Yes, I would". He also claimed he could destroy Iran's energy infrastructure, bridges and factories within three days.

Background. The war began with US-Israeli strikes in late February 2026 and spread to Lebanon and the Gulf. Since then it has gone through declared victories, ceasefires, a June memorandum of understanding that later collapsed, and the current pattern of "no war, no peace". Washington now relies on a naval blockade and economic strangulation. Qatar's prime minister met Trump and JD Vance on 9 November, and Trump afterwards called Iran "militarily, effectively over" while keeping US forces "locked and loaded". Rubio says talks have produced no breakthrough and calls some Iranian demands "outrageous". A senior Iranian official told Reuters that Tehran has sent a "nuclear proposal" via Qatar, but will discuss it only after Washington meets seven preconditions.

Analysis. Taken together, the two statements show a war managed at low intensity by actors who all prefer it that way. The Gulf monarchies are the most exposed to Iranian retaliation and to Houthi fire. They have concluded that a frozen blockade is less dangerous than a new air campaign, and they are now saying so through Trump himself. That is a notable shift from the alignment of early 2026. It also gives Riyadh, Doha and Abu Dhabi a veto-like influence over the US escalation ladder, though Trump signals he resents it. For Tehran, the Bahrain claim does three jobs. It shows deterrence after the Sirik strike. It tests whether Gulf hosts will tolerate US use of their territory. And it targets an early-warning node rather than a manned facility, which keeps escalation controlled. Mojtaba Khamenei's written instruction of 31 October calls for an "immediate, impactful and geographically unrestricted response" to any attack, and today's response looks like that doctrine applied at the bottom of the ladder. The losers are Bahrain, which hosts the Fifth Fleet and has no say over the targeting, and anyone hoping the midterm defeat would push Trump quickly toward a deal.

What to watch. Whether CENTCOM or Bahrain confirms damage; whether Washington answers the Bahrain claim or absorbs it; and whether the Qatari channel produces a framework before the EU Foreign Affairs Council on 16 November.

2. The Houthis aim at Saudi Arabia's Hormuz bypass.

What happened. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree claimed a drone strike on Pumping Station No. 7 of the East-West Pipeline, a ballistic missile at the Yanbu oil facility, and a drone at Aramco in Riyadh. He also listed a drone on a military cargo aircraft at Sharurah and a missile toward Khamis Mushait air base, and rejected as a "grave falsehood" Riyadh's claim that Mecca had been targeted. The coalition says it intercepted two drones headed for Riyadh and the Eastern Province on Thursday, after downing ballistic and cruise missiles on Tuesday and Wednesday. Aramco has said nothing. On Monday Riyadh acknowledged that an attack on the pipeline caused a fire but did not affect operations. On 8 November an Aramco administrative building in the King Abdullah Financial District was damaged by falling debris. Saudi Foreign Minister Faisal bin Farhan received China's Middle East envoy Zhai June in Riyadh to discuss Houthi attacks and Hormuz. The UN Security Council unanimously renewed Yemen sanctions for another year, including asset freezes and travel bans, and demanded an end to the attacks on Saudi Arabia.

Background. Riyadh says it has rerouted about 4 million barrels a day of crude, roughly 4% of global supply, through the East-West Pipeline to Yanbu since Iran blockaded Hormuz. Houthi strikes on Saudi airports, ports and oil sites have escalated since the 10 October attack on King Khalid International Airport, which killed 14 people and wounded more than 300 according to the GCC. Saudi airstrikes have followed. The Houthi-run health ministry says 33 people were killed near Sanaa, a figure that cannot be verified. On the ground, the Yemeni government's "Dawn of Yemen" offensive (since early October) and a Houthi counter-push in Lahij have reopened the war across Lahij, Taiz, Marib, al-Jawf, Hajjah and al-Bayda.

Analysis. The Houthis act as Iran's coercive instrument against the one Gulf asset that blunts the Hormuz blockade. Hitting Yanbu and the pipeline raises the cost of Saudi neutrality without Tehran being directly responsible. That helps explain why Riyadh asked Trump to wait: a renewed US campaign against Iran would probably bring heavier Houthi fire on precisely this bypass. The Zhai June meeting is worth noting. As the largest buyer of Gulf crude and Iran's main commercial lifeline, China is the outside actor with the most leverage over both Tehran and the Houthis' supply chains, and Riyadh is now asking it to use that leverage. Syria's deployment of 600 special forces to Saudi Arabia, confirmed this week, turns this into a test of a new Saudi-centred security network that also includes the "Mecca" defence pact with Pakistan and Turkey.

What to watch. Any Aramco or Energy Ministry statement on Yanbu throughput; Saudi retaliation in Sanaa and Hodeidah; and whether China's envoy carries a message to Tehran.

3. Hormuz: tanker explosions continue as oil heads for a second weekly loss.

What happened. Reuters reports that Iran's military said four tankers were hit by explosions in the Strait of Hormuz after ignoring warnings. The IRGC Navy's own statement, carried by ISNA and Mehr, described two supertankers carrying more than 4 million barrels between them that blew up late Wednesday while "transiting the southern part of the strait, despite repeated warnings from its traffic control center". Iranian officials say at least ten tankers have been hit since Sunday. AFP reports some of them were escorted by US fighter jets. The IRGC also warned operators against switching off AIS or using "prohibited" routes. Brent settled more than 3 dollarshigher on Thursday on the tanker and Houthi news, but early Friday it was around 95.4 dollars–95.5. That puts it on course for a second consecutive weekly decline of about 2.4 to 2.9%, after a roughly 16% fall the week before. Traders read Trump's preference for blockade over bombing as limiting the risk of a major escalation. Separately, US crude stocks rose 4.7 million barrels.

Background. Hormuz carried nearly a fifth of world crude before the war. Iran now runs a selective transit regime: it has exempted India, China, Iraq and Qatar from its restrictions, while the US blockades Iranian ports. In the UAE, Ras Al Khaimah and Sharjah reopened beaches on 12 November after weeks of pollution from the supertanker Gem No.2, struck in a suspected Iranian drone attack in early October.

Analysis. Iran is turning control of the strait into a sovereign-sounding regulatory claim, with a "traffic control centre", authorised routes and exemptions for friendly states. Meanwhile it uses deniable mines or munitions against anyone who complies with the US convoy model instead. The market's calm reflects demand weakness and the bypass pipelines more than any reduction in risk. President Masoud Pezeshkian has also demanded compensation at COP31 for environmental damage from US-Israeli strikes. That builds a legal-diplomatic narrative to sit alongside the coercion at sea. Foreign Policy's question this week, about who will control Hormuz after the war, is now the central question for any settlement.

What to watch. Independent confirmation (from UKMTO, Ambrey or the ship owners) of the vessels named; war-risk premiums; and whether the exemption list grows.

4. Israel: Eisenkot's mandate, Lieberman's war-powers challenge and a caretaker prime minister.

What happened. President Herzog gave Gadi Eisenkot (Yashar) the first chance to form a government on 8 November. Sixty-seven MKs recommended him, including the Arab-led parties, against 46 for Netanyahu. He has 28 days plus a possible two-week extension. On Thursday Avigdor Lieberman, newly named chair of the Knesset Foreign Affairs and Defense Committee, warned that Netanyahu's caretaker government cannot take steps that might lead Israel into war without parliamentary oversight and the agreement of all security chiefs. The Times of Israel live blog reports that Haredi leaders are fielding "multiple overtures" as coalition-building speeds up. Ynet reports Eisenkot has authorised study of a minority government with outside support from Ra'am and United Torah Judaism, who would abstain on the investiture vote. Lieberman, however, refuses to back any government relying on Ra'am or the Joint List "even through their abstention", and Yair Golan reportedly prefers Arab support to a Haredi deal.

Background. The 27 October election produced a majority against Netanyahu on paper but no governing coalition. The anti-Netanyahu camp is split on two issues it has never resolved: Haredi conscription and the legitimacy of Arab parties as partners. If Eisenkot fails, Herzog can pass the mandate to Netanyahu or another MK, or declare that no one can form a government.

Analysis. For foreign policy, the key point is that Netanyahu remains prime minister until a new government wins a confidence vote. That means the man who has run the Iran, Lebanon and Gaza wars keeps executive control during the most sensitive diplomatic window since February. Lieberman is a hawk, so his intervention is not dovish. It is an attempt to subject a caretaker's decisions on war to institutional limits, and it echoes the Lebanese finding that talks are frozen "until a new Israeli government is formed". Partners therefore face a lame-duck problem: the US, Qatar, Egypt and Lebanon cannot easily lock in commitments with an Israeli government that may not exist in six weeks.

What to watch. Whether UTJ or Shas open formal talks; Liberman's stance on the Ra'am formula; and any Netanyahu move on Lebanon or Iran that would test the caretaker doctrine.

5. Gaza: a hospital strike, a baby dies of cold and the Board of Peace's one-sided statement.

What happened. A pre-dawn Israeli strike on 12 November hit the second floor of the surgical wing at Nasser Hospital in Khan Younis. It killed six people, including 52-year-old surgeon Rizq Abu Shaira and a 13-year-old boy, according to the Gaza health ministry, and the hospital says the facility is now out of service. The IDF said it was targeting Muhammad Abu Ismail, "commander of Hamas' Khan Younis Brigade", whom it said was hiding inside, but it gave no evidence. Hamas denies using the hospital. The same day, nine-month-old Kinan Ahmed Raqiq died of hypothermia in his family's tent in Khan Younis after days of storms. UN News reports that a dune collapsed onto tents west of Deir al-Balah, killing three children and two women. OCHA counts 26,000 households affected by storms as of 8 November and about 65% of Gazans still in tents or temporary shelter. Stocks of tents and blankets inside Gaza are "virtually exhausted", while more than 150,000 tents wait outside the Strip. The Board of Peace condemned Hamas's use of hospitals but did not condemn Israel for the strike, which drew UN concern. Netanyahu replied that Hamas "has not surrendered a single weapon".

Background. The ceasefire is about a year old. Under the Board of Peace's July disarmament framework, Hamas would "collect and store" weapons under a Palestinian technocratic committee, phased against Israeli withdrawals over 200 to 350 days. Israel is involved but has not signed. Commercial goods stopped flowing in October over an alleged Hamas levy of about 10,000 shekels per truck, which Hamas denies. Israel has also reportedly blocked a US contractor from building a base for the International Stabilization Force.

Analysis. This is a ceasefire in name that works as a managed siege. Israel keeps freedom of action (strikes on "commanders") and control of what enters. Hamas keeps its weapons and its control of the local economy. The Board of Peace, designed as the political scaffolding of a US-led settlement, is acting more and more like a party to the dispute than an arbiter, which damages its credibility with Arab guarantors. Haaretz reports that key water and sewage projects remain stalled despite Board pressure. Winter is now the main threat, and the tent bottleneck is a political choice, not a logistical one.

What to watch. Whether COGAT admits more of the 150,000 tents; any Board of Peace "consequences" mechanism; and ISF basing.

6. Kurdistan Region: the PUK walkout becomes a crisis of statehood.

What happened. Bafel Talabani called the rift with the KDP "the single largest political crisis in the history of the Kurdistan region". He said he recognises no parliament in Erbil and dismissed Masoud Barzani's offer of dialogue. The crisis began on 4 November. That day, 52 of 100 MPs, including three PUK defectors, reconvened parliament without the PUK and elected the KDP's Vala Fareed as speaker. The PUK then quit Masrour Barzani's ninth cabinet, and together with New Generation it is challenging the session at the Federal Supreme Court. Talabani has threatened to name a separate PUK leadership of the KRG if the KDP fills posts alone. Masrour Barzani met PM Ali al-Zaidi in Baghdad on 9 November about the 2027 budget. Baghdad wants at least 400,000 b/d of Kurdish oil from 2027, while Erbil offers 250,000 to 280,000.

Background and analysis. The PUK-KDP duopoly survived civil war in the 1990s and the 2017 referendum, and it has always relied on a dual-administration fallback. Three things make this round worse. First, Baghdad's Federal Supreme Court is now the arbiter of Kurdish internal politics. Second, the 8 November drone strike on Khor Mor gas field in PUK territory took about 2.8 GW off the regional grid. No one has claimed it, and Iraqi sources point to Tuz Khurmatu, a day after Kataib Hezbollah referred to the field. Third, Iran has an interest in a divided KRI. The main beneficiaries are Baghdad's centralisers and Tehran-aligned factions. The KRG's Western partners lose most.

What to watch. The Federal Supreme Court rulings; whether the PUK names parallel officials in Sulaymaniyah; and Khor Mor's restart.

Five Hezbollah members were arrested near Batroun and Bsharri, and Salam promised a strict investigation. Salam says the government's top priority is keeping Lebanon out of a new war.

Former Iraqi MP Ahmed al-Alwani was found dead in a Beirut apartment, and one Lebanese suspect is held.

Context: The Lebanese state is slowly asserting sovereignty against Hezbollah, within the June framework on disarmament and Israeli withdrawal, while Israeli pressure continues.

Syria.

International.

Damascus confirmed it is sending personnel, reportedly 600 special forces, to Saudi Arabia as "trainers and advisers", after al-Sharaa's 4 November Riyadh visit, a defence pact and 403 dollarsmillion in loans.

A French diplomat told Enab Baladi that the frigate Surcouf's visit marks a step in Syria-France military cooperation.

Local.

Israeli incursions in Quneitra and Daraa are provoking protests in four governorates.

In Suwayda, Internal Security and the Druze "National Guard" exchanged fire.

Context: Al-Sharaa's Syria is buying regional legitimacy as a security contributor to Riyadh, while sovereignty in the south and Druze autonomy remain unresolved.

Jordan.

International.

Jordan has repeatedly condemned Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia, most recently on 8 November. It also hosts US troops that NBC reported being repositioned.

Local.

The aftermath of the 23 October Karak church attack continues: three suspects were arrested, the king vowed an "iron fist", and universities are adding counter-extremism courses.

Context: A quiet news day. Amman is managing threats on three fronts (Syria's south, jihadist cells at home, and the West Bank) while aligning with Riyadh.

Iraq (incl. Kurdistan Region)

International.

Washington and Tehran are both watching al-Zaidi's confrontation with Kataib Hezbollah. KH accuses him of helping US intelligence and holds him "fully responsible" for any attack on its positions.

Iraq's Hormuz exemption from Tehran keeps its exports flowing.

Local.

The Kataib Hezbollah file is splitting the Coordination Framework.

Kurdistan: the PUK walked out and Talabani calls it the "largest crisis". The 2027 budget is unresolved, and Khor Mor is offline after the drone attack.

Context: Al-Zaidi is the first Framework PM to confront KH directly. The PUK-KDP rupture weakens Erbil just as Baghdad seeks more control over oil.

Iran.

International.

The IRGC claims the Bahrain radar strike. Tehran's seven preconditions remain attached to its Qatar-delivered proposal.

At COP31, Pezeshkian demanded mechanisms to compensate for environmental damage from the US-Israeli strikes.

Local.

The Assembly of Experts urged consideration of NPT withdrawal and a doctrine review, with nine bills in the Majlis.

Inflation is above 70%, a 7.6% contraction is forecast, and executions continue.

Context: A regime under blockade, led by an unseen Supreme Leader, is betting that maritime coercion and nuclear ambiguity will outlast US patience.

Turkey.

International.

As COP31 host in Antalya, Turkey brings Pezeshkian, Tebboune and other regional leaders together. Erdoğan keeps up his long-running push for US-Iran diplomacy and Ankara's offer to mediate.

Ankara is a signatory, with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, of the "Mecca" defence pact that Syria may join, which places Turkey inside the emerging Saudi-led security network.

Local.

Kurtulmuş and Bahçeli are moving the PKK monitoring commission forward.

Central bank inflation report and the helicopter crash in Malatya.

Context: Ankara is at once host, mediator and pact member. Its domestic Kurdish peace process and its regional role are increasingly linked.

Saudi Arabia.

International.

Trump says Riyadh asked him to "let it be the way it is now for a while", a strategic preference for containment over escalation.

Talks with China's Zhai June; Syrian troops deploying; and Canada's PM Carney condemned the Houthi attacks in a call with MBS.

Local.

Interceptions over Riyadh and the Eastern Province, and Houthi claims against Yanbu and the pipeline.

Context: The kingdom is fighting a two-front defensive war, against the Houthis directly and Iran indirectly, while trying to keep Washington from widening it.

International.

Named by Trump among the allies seeking a pause in strikes on Iran. A member of the Sudan Quad, which coordinates with the UN on an RSF track.

Beaches reopened in Ras Al Khaimah and Sharjah after the Gem No.2 spill. Ambrey reported a merchant vessel struck by a projectile about 14 nm northwest of RAK the previous day.

Context: The UAE is bearing the environmental and maritime costs of the Hormuz conflict on its own coast, and that strengthens its preference for de-escalation.

Qatar.

International.

Local.

Little domestic news. Qatar enjoys a Hormuz exemption from Tehran.

Context: Qatar's mediation brand is at its peak, and also at its most exposed if talks fail.

Local.

The authorities ordered cooperatives to secure food stocks and prepare 95 emergency shelters, and banned photography and live-streaming at the airport.

Context: Muscat's role as co-littoral state of Hormuz makes it indispensable to any settlement, even though Doha currently leads the mediation.
