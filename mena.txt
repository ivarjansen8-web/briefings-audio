Here is your Middle East briefing for Saturday, 21 November.

Houthi missiles over Riyadh as Trump weighs a new Iran offensive and Baghdad enters Jurf al-Sakhar.

The Saudi–Houthi war is now the hottest front of the wider Iran war. Early Saturday the Saudi-led coalition said it shot down three ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh, two days after a Houthi strike damaged the Public Investment Fund (PIF) tower and one day after the coalition began a "special operation" against Hodeidah port. That puts the Saudi capital's financial district, and the credibility of Vision 2030, inside the range of daily fire. In Washington, Trump is weighing whether to resume large-scale strikes on Iran as Netanyahu, still a caretaker prime minister, lobbies for a 4-to-7-day air campaign. Gulf states, Pakistan and Turkey are lobbying the other way. Oil markets are mostly calm: Brent fell for a third week, to about 92.57 dollars. In Iraq, Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi's forces entered Kataib Hezbollah's Jurf al-Sakhar stronghold under an Iranian-brokered compromise. It is the most important test of Iraqi state authority over the "resistance axis" in a decade. In Gaza, the IDF's largest incursion beyond the Yellow Line since the truce has ended, but the Board of Peace now openly warns that the ceasefire may collapse. Turkey's top administrative court has effectively ended Ekrem İmamoğlu's 2028 presidential bid, completing a week of opposition media seizures.

Top stories.

1. Houthi missiles over Riyadh and a coalition move on Hodeidah: the Saudi–Houthi war climbs the ladder.

What happened. Early on Saturday 21 November, the coalition said its air defences intercepted and destroyed three ballistic missiles fired from Yemen towards Riyadh. An AFP journalist in the capital reported hearing explosions, and emergency alerts went out overnight for Riyadh and the south. The Houthis said on X that they were striking targets "across Saudi Arabia, including Riyadh." That was their promised reprisal for what they called the coalition's "special operation" against Hodeidah. On Friday, coalition spokesman Turki al-Malki announced a "targeted operation" against port facilities that the coalition says are used to smuggle and move weapons and ballistic missiles. Yemen's (government) information minister said the port remained fully operational. On Friday Saudi air defences also downed drones and missiles over the south. Debris from two missiles intercepted over Jazan fell in the Al-Shuqaiq area, and Civil Defense issued warnings for Jazan and Abha. The Houthis claim they hit the Shuqaiq power station and a port in Jazan; those claims are unverified.

The escalation follows the strike on Thursday 19 November that hit the PIF tower, Riyadh's tallest building and the sovereign wealth fund's headquarters. It blew out windows across seven or eight floors and bent parts of the façade, with no injuries reported. The Houthi military spokesman said the targets were King Khalid International Airport and the financial centre. It was the second hit on a landmark in Riyadh's financial district in under two weeks, after Aramco's building was struck on 8 November. Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree has threatened that Jeddah port and "all Saudi Red Sea ports" become targets if Hodeidah is struck. Saudi authorities say at least 26 people have been killed in suspected Houthi drone and missile attacks on the kingdom since 23 September. Egypt's foreign ministry issued a statement early Friday condemning attacks on "civilian and economic facilities" in Riyadh. Etihad has cancelled all Abu Dhabi–Riyadh and Abu Dhabi–Dammam flights through 30 November.

Background. The Saudi–Houthi truce of 2022, which had survived the Gaza war and the Red Sea crisis, collapsed in 2026. According to the Red Sea crisis timeline, the Houthis resumed attacks on Saudi Arabia on 13 July, as the US–Iran ceasefire unravelled, and declared a blockade of Saudi ports and ships at Bab el-Mandeb a week later. In early September came a mass attack that set fires at oil facilities and wounded 73 people, together with a Houthi ground offensive against the Presidential Leadership Council.

Analysis. The targets matter more than the totals. By hitting the PIF and Aramco towers, the Houthis are aiming at the symbols of Saudi Arabia's post-oil economic model, not only its military. The cost is reputational and financial: investor confidence, airline schedules and insurance. Saudi public finances are already strained. The Finance Ministry reported a Q3 deficit of SAR 84 billion, up about 145% on the previous quarter. Hodeidah is Riyadh's coercive lever: the port is northern Yemen's commercial and humanitarian lifeline, and attacking it raises humanitarian costs that the Saudis have spent years trying to avoid. For Tehran, the Houthi front is cheap leverage while its own coast is blockaded. It squeezes the Gulf state Washington most needs on side, and it gives Riyadh a reason to press the White House for either a deal or decisive action. The Saudi public line, however, remains de-escalatory towards Iran (see story 2).

What to watch. Whether the Houthis act on the threat to Jeddah and the Red Sea ports, which would hit Saudi Arabia's Hormuz bypass export route. Whether the coalition widens the Hodeidah operation to cranes and fuel terminals. And whether the US enters the Saudi–Houthi exchange directly.

2. Trump weighs a new Iran offensive as Netanyahu lobbies and the Gulf pushes back.

What happened. Citing the New York Times, Haaretz and Israel Hayom report that Netanyahu is pressing Washington to join a four-to-seven-day air campaign against Iran. Israeli planning reportedly includes further strikes on nuclear sites and widespread attacks on energy infrastructure, which Trump has not approved. Axios, as relayed by Saudi outlet Sabq, says Trump does not rule out a broad joint operation but "will not rush". At a White House news conference on Friday, Trump said Iran wants an agreement but has not offered terms he can accept. He tied US gasoline prices to how the confrontation ends and called the chances of a deal "good". The AP reports from Cairo that officials from the Gulf, Pakistan and Turkey have stepped up calls to senior US officials to head off a resumption. One official argued that economic pressure on Iran is working well enough that renewed major combat is unnecessary. In Israel, Avigdor Liberman, an Iran hawk, said on Friday that there is "no security justification" for striking Iran now, and that if the prime minister decides to do so the decision would be politically motivated.

On the military side, the IRGC said its aerospace force destroyed a US MQ-9 Reaper over the Strait of Hormuz with a "newly deployed" air-defence system, calling it the 58th such drone. The US has not confirmed it. UKMTO said a tanker caught fire on Friday after an unknown projectile hit it eight nautical miles north of Khasab, with no casualties. The IRGC says the ship hit a mine. The Iranian army separately says two tankers hit mines east of the strait. Earlier in the week Iran sent drones at the US-operated radar at Bahrain's Sheikh Isa air base and at Kuwait's Ali Al Salem air base. The US retaliated with a strike on a missile launch site on Qeshm Island.

Background. The war began on 28 February, when US-Israeli strikes killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei. Mojtaba Khamenei now holds the post. Major combat gave way to a "no war, no peace" phase built around a US naval blockade of Iranian exports and Iranian harassment of Hormuz traffic. Washington has warned that its navy could target vessels doing ship-to-ship transfers of Iranian crude. Diplomacy runs through Qatar, the main channel, and Oman. On 17 November Araghchi met Sultan Haitham and Foreign Minister Badr al-Busaidi in Muscat and saw a Qatari delegation the same day, without a breakthrough. The atmosphere worsened after CNN, citing seven US officials, reported that Iran was developing options to target senior US officials in Washington with a dirty bomb or a chemical attack. Tehran called the claim "baseless", and the White House said there was no credible homeland threat.

Analysis. Three clocks are running out of step. Netanyahu's is domestic: he lost his majority on 27 October, and a short, successful campaign would strengthen his case for a "national unity" government (see story 5). Trump's is economic: oil prices are falling and US midterms are over. That weakens the argument for escalation while it frees his hands for it, and his gasoline remark shows which consideration he weighs more. Tehran's is coercive. As analysts quoted by CNN note, the recovery of Gulf exports to near prewar levels erodes Iran's oil leverage, which gives it an incentive to escalate at sea to raise the cost of the blockade. The Gulf states are caught in the middle. Earlier in the war, according to reports, some privately argued for fighting on until Iran was decisively defeated. Now they are paying for the stalemate, with drones over Kuwait and Bahrain and missiles over Riyadh, and they fear a renewed air war more than the status quo. Gargash said the region must prepare for a long period of "neither a clear resolution nor a stable order".

What to watch. Any US carrier or bomber movements and Israeli home-front instructions. Whether Qatar's channel produces a new written proposal. Whether Iran moves from drones to missiles against Gulf bases; the IRGC has said missiles are ready for "new geographical areas".

3. Iraq enters Jurf al-Sakhar: a state-building test negotiated in Tehran.

What happened. On Friday 20 November, Iraqi security forces deployed into Jurf al-Sakhar (officially Jurf al-Nasr), the Kataib Hezbollah (KH) stronghold in northern Babil about 50 km south of Baghdad. Iraqi civilians and state forces had been largely kept out of the town since 2014. PM Ali al-Zaidi ordered a unified command combining Iraqi Army, Federal Police and PMF units, effective 06:00 on the 20th, and ordered all closed roads in the area reopened. An army brigade, a Federal Police battalion and an Energy Police regiment will guard the oil pipelines. Lt. Gen. Saad Maan said KH forces would move elsewhere and a new headquarters reporting to the Federal Police would open. A KH source told AFP the group had merely "allowed the government a way out of the predicament it created" and would not leave. Al Arabiya reported a phased pullout over a month. More than 100,000 former residents remain displaced. Baghdad lifted its nationwide top-level security alert on Friday, a day after raising it.

Background. The trigger was oil. On 16 November al-Zaidi ordered forces into the area after an investigation accused KH of running a smuggling network that diverted hundreds of millions of dollars of state crude, dating back to 2021. Nine senior security and oil officials were dismissed. A Qatari report says the compromise was overseen by Quds Force commander Esmail Qaani.

Analysis. This is the sharpest test so far of the al-Zaidi government's claim to a monopoly of force, and it was passed only partly. The state has a presence in the town, and the alliance (PMF brigades inside the joint force) means KH has been absorbed into the arrangement rather than expelled. Qaani's reported role shows the limits: Iran still holds the veto over how its strongest Iraqi ally is treated, but Tehran also chose not to risk a war on a second front while it is blockaded. Washington will see the "oil smuggling" framing, coming amid "mounting US pressure" on Iraq's oil sector, as a sign that Baghdad is responding to sanctions risk. The same logic is now being turned on Erbil: al-Zaidi's spokesman said the army "could" enter the Kurdistan Region to pursue smugglers.

What to watch. Whether KH units actually leave on the reported one-month timetable. Whether any displaced families are allowed home, which is the real measure of sovereignty. Whether Baghdad stations SOMO staff at Kurdish oilfields.

4. Gaza: the central incursion ends, the truce frays, and the Board of Peace sounds the alarm.

What happened. The IDF said its tank-led ground operation around the Bureij and Nuseirat camps, its first confirmed major push beyond the "Yellow Line" since the October 2025 ceasefire, was "completed". The stated objective was a tunnel west of the Yellow Line said to threaten an IDF position. The operation covered residential blocks 660, 661 and 666, left extensive bulldozing damage to homes, industry and farmland, and ended with Salah al-Din Road reopened. Displaced families returned to their tents in the al-Daawa neighbourhood. Al Jazeera, citing a Palestinian security source, reports that Israeli-backed local armed groups took part. On 20 November, Israeli drone attacks killed at least seven Palestinians in central Gaza. Explosive-laden drones were reportedly used for demolitions in eastern Gaza City and near the Yellow Line east of Khan Younis. AFP counts more than 1,600 Palestinians killed since the truce began, and seven Israeli soldiers and a civilian contractor killed.

Background. The Board of Peace, the UNSC Resolution 2803 body supervising the Trump plan, sent its first annual assessment to the Security Council this week. It warns that the agreement could collapse and that "there would be no road map to return to, no administration to deploy and no enabling environment to rebuild." It says Israel has not let the technocratic administration take up its role and has not decided on arrangements for the Gaza civil police. Hamas has not disarmed. The International Stabilization Force is still delayed. Of 17 dollarsbillion pledged, 280 dollarsmillion has been deployed.

Analysis. The incursion tests the Yellow Line as a de facto border. Israel is treating it as a flexible security perimeter it can cross at will, much as it treats its "security zone" in south Lebanon. The alleged use of anti-Hamas militias, a policy Israel acknowledged in 2025, points to a strategy of fragmenting Gaza rather than handing it to the technocratic committee. Hamas, for its part, has no incentive to disarm while the ISF does not exist and IDF lines advance. The Board of Peace report effectively assigns blame to both sides, which gives Washington diplomatic cover but no means of enforcement. In Israel's coalition limbo, no caretaker government will make the concessions on police and administration that the report demands.

What to watch. Whether there is a UNSC session on the Board of Peace report. Whether further operations beyond the Yellow Line follow. Winter flooding: OCHA counts about 46,000 affected households and more than 40,000 damaged shelters.

5. Israel's post-election limbo: Netanyahu pitches "unity" as the Eisenkot bloc consolidates.

What happened. On 20 November caretaker PM Netanyahu renewed his call for a national unity government, citing the security situation after briefing leaders of Gadi Eisenkot's bloc. Haaretz reports that Eisenkot plans to put a government to a Knesset vote even if talks fail. Likud says unity talks with the Eisenkot/Yashar bloc will continue, with a second round expected on 22 November. On 17 November the Knesset elected Yashar's Matan Kahana as speaker by 66 to 52, with support from Ra'am and the Joint List, the first time since the vote that the anti-Netanyahu bloc has used its numbers.

Background. The 27 October election gave Netanyahu's bloc 53 seats and Eisenkot's 48. Arab parties hold 19 seats and the balance, but most Jewish party leaders rule out partnering with them.

Analysis. The arithmetic produced an anti-Netanyahu majority on procedural votes, such as the speakership, but not yet a governing coalition. Netanyahu's "unity" pitch and his Iran push (story 2) reinforce each other: a security emergency is the strongest argument for keeping him in charge. Eisenkot's bloc wants to use the speakership to advance term limits, an October 7 commission of inquiry and a rollback of the judicial overhaul. UTJ's informal contacts with Eisenkot, while it still voted for Likud's speaker candidate, are the key variable.

What to watch. The 22 November talks. Whether Herzog extends the mandate. Whether a Knesset vote on an Eisenkot government is scheduled.

6. Turkey closes the door on İmamoğlu as the media crackdown deepens.

What happened. The Council of State's 8th Chamber upheld Istanbul University's revocation of jailed Istanbul mayor Ekrem İmamoğlu's degree. That ends the administrative route and disqualifies him from the 2028 presidential race, since candidates must hold a university degree. The ruling, first reported by the pro-government daily Sabah, had not yet appeared in the UYAP judicial database. On 20 November Özgür Özel, who now leads the Yeni Parti, called it "cowardice" and said İmamoğlu remains the opposition's candidate. A day earlier an Ankara court had sentenced İmamoğlu and eight others to more than two years over the 2023 CHP congress. On 17 November police raided Sözcü TV and a court put it under the TMSF, five weeks after Halk TV was seized. Opposition voters are left without a major national broadcaster. Erdoğan also welcomed seven opposition-elected mayors and two acting mayors into the AKP.

Analysis. The pattern is a methodical removal of the 2028 alternative: the candidate by court ruling, the platforms by trusteeship, and local strongholds by defection. Ankara can do this cheaply because Western leverage is weak. Turkey is indispensable as a Gulf security partner (the Qatar pact, the Saudi-Pakistan "Mecca" pact), on Syria and as a convenor at COP31. A November. 20 FDD brief reports that Washington is weighing F-35 sales and sanctions relief. The domestic weakness is the economy: the fund-collapse scandal that Sözcü covered is, per Al-Monitor, the government's biggest problem this autumn.

Security, defence and conflict.

Saudi–Houthi: Three missiles intercepted over Riyadh (21 November). The PIF tower was hit (19 November). The coalition is conducting a Hodeidah "special operation" (20 November), and debris has fallen at Al-Shuqaiq.

Hormuz: Friday's tanker fire north of Khasab follows UKMTO's six verified attacks through 18 November and an attack on an ADNOC L&S vessel on 17 November that injured 11 crew. A Jordanian seafarer, Mohammad Hussam Al Din Al Otaibi, is missing.

Lebanon: Israel struck what it called a former Hezbollah missile production and storage site in the Bekaa on 20 November, its second strike in the east this week. Lebanon's NNA reported five airstrikes. The IDF still holds a self-declared "security zone" about 10 km deep in the south.

Lebanon (domestic): The arrest of five Hezbollah members between Bsharri and Batroun has become a political row. MTV reports that authorities acted on foreign intelligence, and a Hezbollah MP accused the state of "yielding to foreign dictates".

Sudan: The RSF and SPLM-N claim to have captured Bau locality in Blue Nile. Sudan Tribune's military sources say the army withdrew without a fight, though the army claims it destroyed 28 RSF vehicles at Derang. Army aircraft struck RSF headquarters in Nyala, and RSF commander Mishal Abdelrahman (27th and 28th groups) defected to the army.

Defence alignments: The Turkey–Qatar mutual defence pact signed on 13 November, which commits the two to a "unified stance" against external attack, adds to the August Saudi–Turkey–Pakistan Mecca pact. Gulf security architecture is diversifying away from sole dependence on the US.

Turkey (industry): Baykar delivered the first three Kizilelma jet-powered combat drones, which Erdoğan called "a historic day". It is not clear whether they are cleared for combat.

Diplomacy and international relations.

Shenzhen summit (19 November): The first Trump–Xi–Putin trilateral produced a four-point declaration on energy supply, nuclear risk reduction and UN reform that does not mention Iran or Hormuz. Trump's claim that Xi agreed that Hormuz must stay open has not been confirmed by Beijing. For Tehran this is a mixed outcome: no great-power endorsement of the blockade, but no Chinese pushback either.

EU–Syria: EU foreign ministers agreed on 16 November to revive the 2008 EU–Syria Association Agreement, with human-rights conditionality. Kallas said that if the EU does not engage, "the vacuum will be filled by other players." The EU has committed 620 eurosmillion for 2026 to 27.

Settlement sanctions: 15 or 16 Arab and Muslim foreign ministers, including Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Egypt, Jordan and Turkey, jointly welcomed the UK's sanctions on eight Israeli construction firms and Australia's parallel measures. They urged others to follow, explicitly mentioning E1.

UAE–Somalia rupture: Somalia gave the UAE ambassador 72 hours to leave, accusing Abu Dhabi of an "open invasion" and of building bases outside Somali command. The UAE condemned an "escalatory and hostile approach". Behind this are Berbera, Israel's recognition of Somaliland and the Red Sea rivalry between the UAE and Saudi Arabia/Turkey.

Egypt–Libya: Sisi hosted Dbeibah on 19 November, and nine agreements were signed on energy, reconstruction, consular affairs and fisheries. Cairo still rejects the Turkey–Libya maritime MoU as "null", which the eastern HoR has now endorsed.

IAEA: At the Board of Governors, the US, UK, France and Germany said the Agency has lost "continuity of knowledge" over Iran's HEU stockpile for 18 months. Grossi said he could not report on implementation of the Board's resolutions. Kuwait used the Board to call on Israel to join the NPT.

Lebanon post-UNIFIL: France is advancing a plan for French troops to patrol jointly with the LAF after UNIFIL's mandate ends on 31 December. Italy resists, Israel objects, and a US–France idea for a six-month transitional force of about 3,000 is being discussed. The UNSC reaffirmed its support for UNIFIL as the deadline approaches.

France–Algeria: Paris has asked the European Commission to examine Algeria's compliance with the EU association agreement, citing an "almost total" blockade of French imports and suspended urea shipments. Meanwhile MAP reports a new Morocco–France declaration of intent on judicial cooperation dated 20 November.

Economy, energy and trade.

Oil: Brent settled around 92.57 dollarson Friday and WTI at 83.98 dollars, down 3.4% and 5.2% on the week. It was the third straight weekly decline as Gulf export flows through Hormuz recovered to near prewar levels. The market is pricing a glut more than war risk, which reduces Iran's leverage.

Saudi fiscal stress: The Q3 deficit of SAR 84 billion puts the year-to-date gap near 150% of the annual target. Economists forecast a full-year deficit of about 5.6 to 6% of GDP against the government's 4.9%. Missile strikes on the PIF tower add a reputational premium to that bill.

Jordan: The first Jordan–EU Investment Conference at the Dead Sea (18 to 19 November) built on the 3 eurosbillion EU package for 2025 to 27. The Aqaba–Amman desalination and conveyance project, meant to supply 300 million m³ a year, was presented as its flagship.

Iraq/KRI oil: Baghdad's smuggling probes now reach the KRG. Baghdad alleges output above 400,000 b/d, while the KRG puts it at 250,000 to 280,000 b/d. The Oil Ministry wants SOMO monitors in Kurdish fields.

Oman: Ahead of National Day, Omani officials highlight 5.1% real GDP growth in Q2 2026, public debt down to 34.6% of GDP and a regained investment-grade rating. As a mediator outside the line of fire, Muscat is a relative winner of the war.

Morocco: October CPI rose 0.8%. Bookings from Spain were up 48.6% year on year in early November. Rabat reaffirmed its automotive partnership with the EU despite worries about a "Made in Europe" rule.

Egypt: Unemployment rose to 6% in Q3 2026, according to headlines on the state-aligned Egypt Today.

Morocco–Mauritania: Morocco Week in Nouakchott set a goal of MAD 6 billion in bilateral trade by 2030, up from about MAD 3.7 billion in 2025.

Humanitarian and human rights.

Gaza: Winter floods have affected about 46,000 households. UNRWA says 1.98 million people need shelter or non-food help. The UN says vacuum trucks, pumps, tents and blankets are still blocked, and WFP has cut assistance by about a third for lack of funds.

West Bank: The Times of Israel reports that a settlement security coordinator shot dead an unarmed Palestinian in the central West Bank on 20 November, and the IDF is reviewing the incident. Settler attacks on olive harvesters were reported in Birin, Teqoa, Nabi Saleh and al-Maniya. These reports come mostly from Palestinian outlets. ACLED says 2026 is on track to be the most violent year of settler violence on record.

Sudan: Fighting in Blue Nile has displaced more than 1,400 families from Geissan, Wad al-Mahi and Bau. A Port Sudan court handed RSF leader Hemedti a second death sentence in absentia, along with his two brothers and 11 others, over the looting of the medical supplies fund. Courts that do not reach the accused do little for accountability, but they serve the SAF's legitimacy narrative.

Egypt: The UN rights office says the entire six-person newsroom of fact-checking site Matsadash remains in detention.

Turkey: CPJ calls for the trusteeships over Sözcü TV, Halk TV and TELE1 to be reversed.

Iran: Flour rationing was announced on 17 November. The Writers' Association reports a finalised death sentence on moharebeh charges. Opposition sources report protests by retirees and workers. NCRI is the MEK's political arm, so treat its figures as unverified.

Iraq: More than 100,000 people from Jurf al-Sakhar remain displaced a decade on. Their return is the human-rights test of the new arrangement.
