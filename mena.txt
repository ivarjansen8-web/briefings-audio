Here is your Middle East briefing for Sunday, 8 November.

Washington eyes a new-moon strike on Iran as Qatar's channel strains; Eisenkot gets Israel's mandate; Saudi–Houthi war turns deadlier.

Five things defined the region in the 24 to 48 hours to Sunday 8 November. First, the US–Iran war has moved into a decision window: Trump says a choice between renewed strikes, continued economic strangulation and a deal is coming "soon", CENTCOM is reported to have finished planning for a short, intense campaign, and US officials flag the 9 November new moon, while the US keeps hitting Qeshm Island and the IRGC claims more tankers mined in Hormuz. Second, Qatar's mediation is under strain but still alive. Doha denies that talks have collapsed, Trump spoke with the emir, and Tehran has sent a nuclear proposal while keeping its "seven conditions" (blockade lifted, sanctions relief, frozen funds, reparations, an end to Israeli operations in Lebanon) as the price of entry. Third, Israel's post-Netanyahu moment is taking shape: President Herzog is due to hand Gadi Eisenkot the mandate on Sunday evening after 67 MKs recommended him, but a governable coalition will be hard to build without either the Haredim or the Arab parties, and Likud is contesting the result through a Shin Bet "election interference" probe. Fourth, the Saudi–Houthi war is getting worse. Riyadh reports at least 15 missiles and drones intercepted this month, the Houthis claim six operations on 7 November alone, and Houthi health officials say Saudi-led strikes near Sanaa killed 33. Fifth, the Arab state system is realigning around Riyadh and Ankara. Saudi Arabia signed a defence pact and 403 million dollars in loans with Damascus, Syria is weighing entry into the Saudi–Turkish–Pakistani "Mecca pact", and Egypt's reopening of its Tripoli embassy after 12 years signals that Cairo is hedging on Haftar.

1. Washington's decision window on Iran: "minor strikes", a tightening blockade and a new-moon calendar.

What happened. The war that the United States and Israel launched against Iran on 28 February has been in a fragile, violated pause for weeks. In the last 48 hours it has moved closer to a decision. On Friday 6 November, after landing in West Palm Beach, Trump said US forces had destroyed a newly built Iranian "anti-aircraft device" on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz. He called the operations "minor strike[s]" and said US forces had watched the site being built for about two months before hitting it as soon as it was finished. Iranian state media reported two missiles hitting Qeshm near Suza. IRIB carried an army statement claiming air defences had intercepted an American MQ-9 over the island, and CENTCOM offered no operational detail. Euronews captured the doublespeak: the strikes came as Trump claimed "progress" in talks and insisted "we are not fighting."

At sea, CENTCOM says an aircraft fired on the stern of the M/V Huda to disable it after its crew ignored repeated warnings. By the count cited in coverage, that makes it the 11th vessel disabled since the blockade of Iranian ports was reinstated on 14 July. On the Iranian side, the IRGC Navy claimed that two more oil tankers struck mines and caught fire on 6 November, after claiming four tankers "destroyed" on 4 November. It said the strait will stay closed until Iran's seven conditions are met (Greek City Times; Tehran Times, state-aligned). The US military has called earlier claims of this kind false, and none of the November claims has been independently confirmed.

The bigger signal is from Washington. After a national-security meeting on Thursday 5 November, Trump said a decision was coming "soon" and that he was considering three options: new strikes, continued economic strangulation, or a deal. CENTCOM is reportedly finalising preparations to resume major combat operations without a new directive yet. Officials noted that 9 November is a new moon, which favours air operations. The reported target set is a short, intense campaign against nuclear, missile and drone sites and Iran's energy and oil sector. The Media Line adds that Israel has warned Washington of possible Iranian chemical-weapons use, a claim it attributes to Israeli sources.

Background. The war began with US–Israeli strikes on 28 February, which killed Ali Khamenei and brought his son Mojtaba to power. He has not been seen in public since. Hormuz has been closed or throttled for most of the year. A June memorandum briefly stabilised transit but collapsed in July, and the blockade followed. Since late October, Qatar has been the main channel. That included a letter from Vice-President Vance to security chief Mohsen Rezaei, which Araghchi called a "positive step" while ruling out a meeting.

Analysis. Three dynamics are converging. (1) Domestic US politics. Coverage frames the timing around the 3 November midterms, with oil-price commentary referring to Trump's "midterm loss". NBC, via Iran International, reported that some advisers warned a new Congress could complicate military action through investigations, funding limits or war-powers resolutions. That argues for acting before January, not after. (2) Escalation management. Washington is clearly trying to keep coercion below the threshold of "resumed war". The Pentagon has called these "defensive" strikes, while Trump publicly plays them down, so that the Qatari channel survives. Tehran is mirroring this: the mine claims raise costs for shipping and insurers without directly targeting US forces. (3) Iran's internal hardening. Mojtaba Khamenei's 4 November written statement warned that hopes of a policy shift in Washington after the midterms were "seriously mistaken", called concessions an invitation to "greater greed", and warned that new US attacks would widen the war. That narrows Araghchi's room to trade nuclear concessions for a Hormuz reopening.

Winners and losers. Qatar gains centrality and Oman keeps its niche through the Hormuz corridor talks with Tehran. Saudi Arabia, which is absorbing Houthi fire, and the UAE, whose aviation and shipping remain disrupted, are the main Gulf losers from prolonged ambiguity. Russia is positioning itself: secondary trackers report that Iran's SNSC secretary met Putin and the GRU chief in Moscow on 5 November to discuss the US–Iran negotiations. I could not verify this independently, so treat it as unconfirmed.

What to watch. Whether strikes come around the new moon (9 to 10 November), or Trump announces a "deal framework" instead. Watch any CENTCOM statement shifting from "defensive" to "offensive" language, and whether insurers and the IMO respond to the mine claims.

2. Qatar's channel and Iran's "seven conditions": a Hormuz-for-sanctions deal in outline.

What happened. Qatar denied reports that the negotiations had collapsed and said its mediators remain in close coordination with both sides. AP sources said Iran is reviewing Qatari proposals meant to narrow differences on the nuclear file. Trump spoke to Emir Tamim about Qatar's efforts toward a "comprehensive agreement". A senior Iranian official told Reuters that Tehran had sent a nuclear proposal to Washington via Doha. Per the Jerusalem Post, it is to be discussed only once the US meets Iran's preconditions. Araghchi has named several of these: lifting the sanctions imposed after the "Islamabad memorandum", reducing US forces in the region, restoring oil waivers and releasing frozen funds, including funds held in Qatar. Other reporting adds war reparations and an end to Israeli operations in Lebanon. Iran and Qatar have discussed reopening Hormuz within two days, down from five, once US conditions are met. Fox News reports that the deal under discussion would trade a Hormuz reopening for sanctions relief, with nuclear talks to follow.

Background. This is the third major diplomatic architecture of the war, after the April two-week ceasefire and the June MoU. Oman, meanwhile, has run a parallel technical track with Iran on a "temporary joint maritime corridor" and joint demining.

Analysis. The core dispute is about sequencing. Washington wants nuclear issues on the table "from the outset", per the Vance letter as reported. Tehran wants an economic and military de-escalation package first and treats the nuclear file as "second phase". Doha's two-day implementation idea is meant to give Trump a quick, visible win (ships moving) in exchange for front-loaded relief. For Tehran, the Lebanon condition is the most revealing. It ties Hezbollah's fate to the Iran deal and makes Israel a potential spoiler. For Israel's incoming government, whoever leads it, a US–Iran deal that constrains Israeli action in Lebanon would be a first-order problem. Think-tank debate is polarising on exactly this point. A Washington Institute paper (3 November, per search summary) urges another compromise like the June MoU, while insisting that any post-war Hormuz regime preserve transit passage with no Iranian tolls or veto. Chatham House's Mohsen Milani argues Tehran will try to make its wartime leverage over the strait permanent.

What to watch. A US written response via Doha; whether Witkoff, Vance or Rubio travels to the Gulf; and whether Iran's foreign ministry starts talking about "positive signals" or about "deadlock".

3. Israel: Eisenkot gets the mandate, but has no obvious majority.

What happened. After the 27 October election left Netanyahu's bloc at 53 seats and the opposition bloc at 48, both short of 61, factions representing 67 MKs recommended Yashar chairman Gadi Eisenkot in consultations. Factions with 46 seats backed Netanyahu, and United Torah Judaism recommended no one. The Times of Israel liveblog reported that Herzog will formally task Eisenkot at 5pm on Sunday 8 November, and AFP-sourced headlines already describe him as "tasked". Eisenkot will then have 28 days, extendable by 14. Netanyahu, in his first appearance since the vote, warned against an "Arab-Jewish coalition". On Saturday he called on the "change bloc" to join a national unity government that includes his whole bloc. Yashar replied by inviting "Likud and its friends" into an Eisenkot-led coalition "without extremists".

The arithmetic problem. The 67 recommendations include Arab-led parties; Ra'am, for instance, said it would back Eisenkot. But like Netanyahu, Eisenkot has ruled out a government relying on them. The other route runs through the Haredim. A senior UTJ official said the party would consider backing an Eisenkot government if draft-exemption legislation advances, but Eisenkot has made universal military or national service a condition of entry. Netanyahu is exploiting the contradiction, accusing Eisenkot, Bennett, Lapid and Liberman of hypocrisy for courting the Haredim. Eisenkot has vowed not to serve under Netanyahu even in a rotation.

The legitimacy fight. The Central Elections Committee certified the results after finding no evidence of fraud. In parallel, committee chair Noam Sohlberg asked Shin Bet director David Zini to investigate suspected foreign funding and election bribery, with reporting pointing to Qatar and Turkey, though the letter named no country. Zini reportedly asked the attorney general to approve questioning four senior Arab politicians as suspects and accused the Shin Bet's own legal adviser of obstruction. Likud is preparing to appeal the results over alleged voter-mobilisation apps. On Saturday night, Tel Aviv protesters demanded that a new government fire Zini.

Analysis. For a foreign-policy reader, the key point is that the probe gives Netanyahu a narrative of an "Arab-assisted" and "foreign-funded" government. That raises the political cost of any Eisenkot–Arab party arrangement, even passive support from outside the coalition. Eisenkot is a former chief of staff and war-cabinet member. He is no dove on Iran or Hezbollah, but he would be more responsive to Washington, more open to a PA role in Gaza, and less tied to the settler right. For the Gulf and for the US–Iran track, a Netanyahu caretaker period that lasts through a possible new US campaign is the worst case: a lame-duck PM with every incentive to escalate. Ynet's analysis of "Eisenkot's five bad choices" lays out why a fresh election in early 2027 remains a real possibility.

What to watch. The formal handover on Sunday evening; UTJ's formal position; whether Likud files a Supreme Court appeal; and the attorney general's ruling on Zini's request.

4. The Saudi–Houthi war gets deadlier.

What happened. Saudi air defences intercepted cruise and ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh and southern regions on 8 November. The Saudi-led coalition says it has stopped at least 15 missiles and drones across the kingdom this month, including launches toward the Eastern Province and Madinah. The Houthis claim six operations on 7 November against Saudi oil facilities, Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport and a National Guard camp in Najran. Riyadh has not confirmed damage. Houthi-run health officials say strikes on al-Shariya village, east of Sanaa, killed 33 and wounded 39, mostly women and children. Yemen's internationally recognised government blamed an errant Houthi ballistic missile instead, and Saudi Arabia's Center for International Communication said it was investigating. The US embassy in Amman issued a 7 November alert urging heightened vigilance across the Middle East because of Houthi threats.

Background. The 2022 UN-brokered truce collapsed in July. According to the BBC account summarised in coverage, Riyadh struck Sanaa airport to stop an Iranian plane from landing, and the Houthis retaliated against Abha airport and Saudi shipping. In October, a strike on KKIA reportedly killed at least 13 and wounded over 300, and an attack on Dammam airport killed one. That led Washington to order non-emergency staff and families out of Saudi Arabia. On 3 November, Yemen's interior minister Ibrahim Haidan accused Iran of embedding 14 IRGC and seven Hezbollah "experts" with the Houthis, specialised in drones, ballistic missiles, electronic warfare and smuggling. Hezbollah called the claim "baseless".

Analysis. For Riyadh, this is the scenario Vision 2030 was meant to avoid: the capital's main airport is reportedly under a GACA movement-by-movement approval regime since 4 November (single-source, House of Saud, a partisan blog), and Etihad has suspended Abu Dhabi–Riyadh and Abu Dhabi–Dammam flights until 19 November. The Houthis are acting partly as Iran's pressure valve on the Gulf states, which are pushing Washington toward a deal. But they also have their own agenda in reopening the 2015 to 22 war on better terms. Riyadh's strategic response is visible in Syria and the Mecca pact (below): building an Arab–Turkish–Pakistani security network that does not rely only on US guarantees.

What to watch. Any Saudi confirmation of Aramco damage; whether Washington folds the Houthis into a renewed Iran campaign; and Omani and UN efforts to restore the 2022 truce framework.

5. Riyadh's new Levant: defence pact and loans for Damascus, and Syria eyes the Mecca pact.

What happened. During Ahmed al-Sharaa's second Riyadh visit in under a month, Saudi and Syrian defence ministries signed a cooperation agreement on 4 November. Its terms were not disclosed, but Damascus called it the "most notable" deal of the visit. The Saudi Fund for Development signed six concessional loans worth over 403 million dollars for energy, housing, health and infrastructure. Days earlier, Foreign Minister Asaad al-Shaibani told the Manama Dialogue that Damascus is evaluating entry into the Mecca alliance, the mutual-defence pact signed by Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan on 7 August.

Background. Saudi Arabia has been al-Sharaa's main Arab backer since Assad's fall in December 2024. Syria is meanwhile trying to secure its borders on all sides. It says it foiled a Hezbollah-linked plot against the Homs refinery, the country's only operating one, and Hezbollah denies any presence in Syria. It has also contained a crisis with Jordan, whose late-October strikes on suspected ISIS sites killed at least seven in Deraa and Suwayda, through a joint security committee. Iraq's PM Ali al-Zaidi made the first Iraqi prime-ministerial visit to post-Assad Damascus on 28 October.

Analysis. Mecca-pact membership would formally put Syria in a Sunni-majority security bloc led by Riyadh and Ankara, with a nuclear-armed Pakistan as the third pillar. That matters for three audiences. Israel, which has insisted on a demilitarised southern Syria and opposes Turkish deployments, would face a Syria with treaty partners. The US-mediated Syria–Israel security talks have been frozen since the Iran war began, and Shaibani says "we do not trust Israel currently". Iran and Hezbollah see their former land bridge turned hostile. Turkey gains a formal framework for its influence in Syria. Critics such as the Middle East Forum argue membership would be "largely cosmetic" and would mostly confirm Damascus's subordination to Ankara. AGSI's analysis is the best primer on what is at stake.

What to watch. Disclosure of the defence agreement's content; Turkey's ratification of the Mecca pact, which is still pending; and Israeli reactions.

6. Egypt hedges in Libya as the Turkey–Libya maritime deal reshapes the East Med.

What happened. Egypt reopened its embassy in Tripoli on 7 November after a 12-year closure. Its foreign minister met PM Abdul Hamid Dbeibah on the sidelines of the CEN-SAD executive council in Tripoli. The move follows the eastern House of Representatives' unanimous vote on 20 October to approve the 2019 Turkey–Libya maritime memorandum, which Cairo, Athens and the EU reject as infringing their maritime zones.

Analysis. For a decade, Cairo's Libya policy rested on Haftar. The eastern parliament, Haftar's political arm, has now handed Ankara its main East Med legal instrument. That shows how far the Haftar family has moved toward Turkey. Egypt's reply is to rebalance toward Tripoli rather than break with Benghazi. Washington, through Massad Boulos, is pushing Dbeibah to unify institutions under the "4+4" framework and is threatening spoilers with sanctions (Germa Substack roundup, secondary aggregator). Libya's west–east divide is becoming less a proxy line between Turkey and Egypt and more a competition over who manages Libya's reunification on Ankara's terms.

Both sides deny striking civilian and UN sites, and calls for independent investigations are growing.

Context: In its fourth year, the war has become a drone-and-infrastructure contest centred on Kordofan, with the army edging forward and the economy collapsing.

Palestine (Gaza and the West Bank)

International.

The Board of Peace's July 15-point disarmament roadmap and the International Stabilization Force remain the official framework. Implementation is stuck on sequencing (Israel wants disarmament before withdrawal) and on troop commitments, with only Kosovo and Albania confirmed as of September.

Erdoğan told the TRT World Forum that the Gaza agreement Turkey helped broker "is not being implemented" (TRT World, state broadcaster).

Local.

Gaza: near-daily strikes (at least four killed on 3 November; 1,580 since the ceasefire per health officials), winter storms flooding tents, and gunfire hitting tents at a northern Rafah displacement site.

West Bank: settler attacks on the olive harvest continue under military protection, per Palestinian monitors (PIC, Hamas-affiliated).

The 28 November PLC elections are in doubt amid reports of postponement (INN, partisan).

Context: Gaza is in a frozen "ceasefire with killing", and the PA's democratic renewal, which donors depend on, is slipping again.

Lebanon.

International.

Iran's preconditions in Qatar include an end to Israeli operations in Lebanon, tying Lebanon's future to the US–Iran deal. UNIFIL's mandate expires at year-end, and Aoun wants a temporary international force to prevent a vacuum.

The Security Council expects a 1701 briefing this month. The Secretary-General's report describes a sequencing deadlock: Israel wants Hezbollah disarmament first, Hezbollah wants Israeli withdrawal first.

Local.

Israeli artillery hit 15 towns on Friday. On Saturday there was a major explosion in Qantara, shelling of Haddatha and Aita al-Jabal, and the closure of Deir Mimas.

A drone strike in Qabrikha killed a motorcyclist, the first such death since September. The IDF said it targeted suspects observing troops. L'Orient Today reads it as a message to Hezbollah.

Lebanese media report that Israel is preparing for a "new confrontation" with Hezbollah.

Context: The June US-brokered framework has frozen the war, but Israel continues demolitions in the buffer zone and is building a road in the Shebaa valley. If the US–Iran track fails, the south is the most likely place for escalation.

Syria.

Saudi defence agreement and 403 million dollars in loans; Mecca-pact membership under study.

Talks with Israel have been frozen since the Iran war began, and Damascus demands a return to pre-December 2024 lines.

Ties with Jordan are being repaired after deadly Jordanian strikes, with a joint security committee and the Nasib crossing reopened.

Local.

The government says it foiled a Hezbollah-linked plot against the Homs refinery. Hezbollah denies it.

The response to flash floods in the northeast, including al-Sharaa's visit to affected areas, and dismantling of an ISIS cell in Aleppo.

Context: Damascus is anchoring itself in a Saudi–Turkish orbit while fighting a low-level shadow war with Hezbollah and ISIS.

Jordan.

International.

The King returned from an official visit to Kazakhstan.

Context: Jordan's security establishment is fighting jihadist spillover from southern Syria and domestic radicalisation while the region burns.

The PUK's reported withdrawal from the KRG cabinet and talks to avert rival administrations.

Context: Baghdad is trying to turn wartime neutrality into regional brokerage, but the KDP–PUK split threatens to hand Baghdad and Tehran leverage over a divided Kurdistan.

Iran.

International.

US strikes on Qeshm; the blockade tightening; the nuclear proposal via Qatar; the IRGC's mine claims.

Accusations from Yemen's government of 14 IRGC "experts" with the Houthis.

Context: A wounded, more securitised Islamic Republic under an invisible leader is betting that its ability to disrupt Hormuz outlasts Washington's political patience.
