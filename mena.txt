Here is your Middle East briefing for Monday, 23 November.

Iran hits Bahrain, Houthis eye Aden as Trump sets a New Year deadline.

Five things defined the past 24 hours. First, the US–Iran "no war, no peace" phase moved further from a settlement. The IRGC claimed missile and drone strikes on US Patriot radars in Bahrain, and Manama said a civilian industrial site on Sitra was set on fire. Iran's navy said two more "violating" tankers caught fire after hitting mines east of Hormuz. Donald Trump said he is "inclined" to resume the war after New Year if there is no deal. Second, Qatar's mediation is now the only live channel: Qatar's PM sees Marco Rubio in Washington today, after a Qatari delegation held unofficial talks in Tehran. Third, the Houthis claimed a strike on Saudi Arabia's Rabigh refinery while their ground forces press toward Aden. That turns Yemen from a sideshow into a second front against Saudi Arabia. Fourth, Israel's caretaker politics collided with West Bank violence. Likud–Yashar unity talks ended without a deal, two weeks before Gadi Eisenkot's mandate runs out, as settler arson swept at least five villages and Eisenkot called it "Jewish terrorism". Fifth, from Khartoum to Benghazi, drones of disputed origin are hitting sensitive sites. Sudan blames Ethiopia for a strike that hit a UN building, and an explosion at the Benghazi naval base may have hit a Russian warship. External powers are finding it harder to stay out of North Africa's wars.

Top stories.

1. Iran–Gulf escalation: IRGC hits Bahrain, Hormuz "mine" incidents multiply, Trump sets a New Year horizon.

What happened. Iran's IRGC Aerospace Force said it carried out a combined missile and drone operation against "US military positions at the Al-Riffa and Awali bases in Bahrain", striking two radars belonging to US Army Patriot batteries. It called this retaliation for US drone overflights and an attack on a radar site on Hengam Island. Iranian state outlets also claimed the "20th" US MQ-1 drone downed over the eastern Strait of Hormuz. Bahrain's Interior Ministry said a "civilian industrial facility" was hit and that smoke rose over Sitra island, home to the state-owned Bapco refinery. Two people were injured: a Bahraini citizen in stable condition and a Pakistani resident in critical condition. Washington has neither confirmed nor denied a hit on the radars. CENTCOM did call "FALSE" an earlier IRGC claim to have damaged a US F-16.

At sea, the Iranian navy said two oil tankers caught fire after hitting naval mines in a "prohibited area" east of the Strait of Hormuz, north of Ghanam Island. It said the ships had switched off their navigation systems and warned that any tanker entering the zone "would face a similar fate". Separately, UKMTO reported a tanker incident "involving military forces" in the Gulf of Oman, and it was unclear whether it was linked. Al Jazeera's Tehran correspondent counted about 25 tanker incidents in the week to 21 November, roughly three a day. The Joint Maritime Information Centre counts seven tankers hit since 16 November.

In a Fox News interview on Sunday, Trump said he is "inclined" to resume strikes on Iran after New Year if negotiations fail. He called Iran's negotiators "professional tap dancers" ("All they do is talk, but it's all BS") and threatened that blockade-runners would end up "at the bottom of the sea, like Iran's navy". Israeli outlet Ynet, citing US officials, reported that Washington has postponed any potential strike by at least a week.

Background. The war began with the US–Israeli attack of 28 February. A June framework (variously called the Islamabad MOU or the June memorandum) ended large-scale hostilities, but talks then stalled over navigation in Hormuz. Iran now runs a "Hormuz mechanism" under which passage needs Iranian authorisation, and the US answers with a naval blockade of Iranian ports and escorted convoys. Trump says Iran stepped up missile and drone attacks after the 3 November US midterms.

Analysis. Tehran's escalation looks calibrated rather than reckless. It strikes US assets on Gulf soil, which forces host governments to absorb the costs. It keeps deniability at sea through the "mine" framing. And it raises the cost of the blockade without crossing into mass-casualty attacks. Critical Threats argues that by running the blockade, Iran forces Washington to choose between letting it collapse and returning to a war that strains relations with Gulf allies. The Gulf monarchies are the squeezed middle. Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Jordan and Egypt all condemned the Bahrain strike. Yet Bloomberg and the Soufan Center both report that Gulf leaders fear a renewed US–Israeli campaign more than the current attrition and will lobby against it. Trump's New Year horizon gives the mediators about five weeks. It also gives Israel, which has reportedly prepared both for a strike and for a pre-emptive Iranian one, a window to lobby.

What to watch. Any independent US confirmation or denial of damage to the Patriot radars. Whether Iran tests the blockade with a convoy. How insurers price the "prohibited area" east of Hormuz.

2. Qatar's mediation: unofficial talks in Tehran, Rubio meeting today.

What happened. Deputy FM Kazem Gharibabadi said a Qatari delegation held two hours of talks in Tehran on Friday night. He stressed that these are not yet formal negotiations, and that if they become so, nuclear technical details would be discussed on the condition that enriched uranium stays inside Iran. Araghchi called the visit "not unusual at all". He said intermediaries constantly carry proposals between the sides, and that "as long as Iran's demands are not met, the discussion on the Strait of Hormuz and its opening is pointless". Qatar has not commented publicly, but its foreign ministry confirmed that Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani meets Secretary of State Rubio in Washington on Monday. The UAE's Abdullah bin Zayed also spoke to Rubio.

Background. Iran's conditions have been stable since September: it would reopen Hormuz within about seven days if the US lifts the blockade, waives oil sanctions and observes a ceasefire that includes Lebanon. Trump insists on a "permanent" nuclear deal. One single-source report (House of Saud) says Araghchi has declared the Islamabad MOU dead while leaving the door open to a new deal. Treat that with caution.

Analysis. Doha is the only actor with working lines to both the White House and the IRGC-dominated system. Oman's track stalled in September over Saudi objections, and China, despite pressing Tehran, has not delivered a breakthrough. The sticking point is now sequencing more than substance: Iran wants sanctions relief and an end to the blockade first, and Washington wants nuclear constraints first. Gharibabadi's line that enriched uranium must stay in Iran is a red line that clashes with likely US demands to ship it out.

What to watch. The State Department readout of the Rubio–Al Thani meeting. Whether the language moves from "mediation" to "framework" or "text". Any Iranian reaction blaming Washington for "maximalism".

3. Yemen becomes a second front: Rabigh claim and the Houthi march on Aden.

What happened. Houthi spokesmen said they scored a "direct hit" on Saudi Aramco's Rabigh refinery on the Red Sea coast. Reuters noted the extent of any damage was unclear, and Saudi authorities have not confirmed any impact. The Houthis also claimed a strike on a radar facility at Wadi ad-Dawasir, south of Riyadh. The coalition said it intercepted a ballistic missile aimed at the western region and a drone aimed at Riyadh. Rabigh has now appeared in Houthi claims on 25 October, 29 October and 22 November, none of them confirmed by Riyadh.

On the ground, the Houthis reached Tor al-Baha, about 80 km from Aden, after government units pulled out of Dhubab and the Bab al-Mandeb coast to defend the temporary capital. Reuters reports that Aden's defenders are fragmented and that the Southern Transitional Council has stayed out of the latest fighting while vowing to defend the south "if needed". A New Arab report dated 22 November describes Houthi reinforcements moving toward Abyan. Analysts read this as an attempt to encircle Aden from the east and cut its fuel and gas supply. Taiz is described as fully besieged.

Background. The Houthis entered the Iran war in July with strikes on Saudi tankers (the Encelia and the Layla) that pushed Brent above 100 dollars. They then declared a "blockade" of Saudi oil flows, and Jazan was hit repeatedly. Some 4.5 million b/d of Saudi exports through Yanbu, Riyadh's bypass around Hormuz, are now exposed. The coalition has hit Sanaa, Saada and Hodeidah port, and the two sides have exchanged threats against each other's ports.

Analysis. This is the most underrated strategic story in the region. If Aden falls or is besieged, the internationally recognised government loses its last urban seat. The Houthis would then hold both shores of Bab al-Mandeb's Yemeni side, and Saudi Arabia's Red Sea export route would become as vulnerable as Hormuz. For Iran, this is cheap leverage that does not require direct IRGC action against Saudi Arabia. Riyadh's options are poor. Sana'a Center researcher Adel Dashela blames poor coordination, competing agendas and uneven training among anti-Houthi forces for the setbacks. The Saudi–Emirati rivalry in the south, flagged by the Sana'a Center, could resurface just when unity is needed. Reports that Syria might send forces to help Saudi Arabia, which Damascus has denied, and talk of activating the Saudi–Turkish–Pakistani "Mecca" defence agreement show Riyadh looking for manpower it lacks.

What to watch. STC mobilisation in Aden and Lahj. Any Houthi attempt on Abyan's coastal road. Whether Saudi Arabia escalates against Hodeidah port despite its humanitarian role.

4. Israel: coalition deadlock meets "Jewish terrorism" in the West Bank.

What happened. The second round of talks between Netanyahu's Likud and Eisenkot's Yashar ended without agreement. Al Jazeera, citing Channel 12, reports that the sticking points were who serves as prime minister first (Netanyahu wants to; Eisenkot rejects any rotation) and whether Itamar Ben-Gvir is included. Haaretz framed the meeting as an agreement to keep talking, with Eisenkot also courting the Haredi parties but unwilling to let the process "drag on". Eisenkot's mandate expires on 6 December, with a possible 14-day extension. If unity fails, Yashar reportedly plans to seek a Knesset vote for a minority government.

Overnight, settlers torched about a dozen vehicles in at least five West Bank villages, including Deir Ballut, and sprayed racist graffiti on homes. Eisenkot called it "Jewish terrorism in every sense of the word" and demanded arrests. FM Gideon Sa'ar blamed a "handful of extremists" and complained that European media ignore stepped-up enforcement. Ben-Gvir accused Eisenkot of "selling out" to the Arab parties. OCHA data cited by ABC put settler attacks at an average of six a day in 2026.

Background. In the 27 October election, Netanyahu's bloc won 53 seats and the opposition bloc 48, with Eisenkot's party on 21, so neither side has 61. President Herzog nonetheless gave Eisenkot the first mandate on the strength of 67 recommendations, 19 of them from Ra'am and the Joint List. Liberman refuses any government that depends on Arab parties and any that includes Netanyahu.

Analysis. Israel's arithmetic now runs through three vetoes: Liberman against the Arab parties, Eisenkot against Netanyahu as PM, and Netanyahu against anything else. A minority government with outside Arab support, which Ahmad Tibi has openly floated, would be historic. But it would give Netanyahu's campaign a "national suicide" line he has already rehearsed. The settler violence strengthens Eisenkot's appeal to the centre. It also highlights that the caretaker government, with Ben-Gvir still at the police ministry, controls enforcement. For foreign policy, a long interregnum means Israel faces Trump's Iran decision, the Board of Peace ultimatum on Gaza and the UNIFIL exit with a caretaker cabinet, which tends to favour inertia and military over diplomatic options.

What to watch. A third Likud–Yashar round. Haredi parties' price. Whether Eisenkot formally requests the extension. Any arrests over the arson.

5. Gaza: the Board of Peace threatens to "hand the keys" to the UN as Israel expands control.

What happened. An Israeli drone strike on a van in Gaza City killed three people. Aid groups said the van carried food. The IDF said the men were Palestinian Islamic Jihad members, including a company commander, falsely presented as aid workers. World Central Kitchen said the vehicle was not affiliated with it. Gaza's health ministry put deaths since the October 2025 ceasefire at 1,661. Hundreds of displaced people protested near the "Yellow Line" in Gaza City. Maghazi's mayor said more than 500 housing units had been destroyed in under a month as part of a "deliberate, gradual displacement". Israeli troops now control about two-thirds of the Strip, and Netanyahu reportedly directed the IDF to occupy 70% after disarmament talks stalled.

Background. In its second report to the Security Council, the US-led Board of Peace said there can be no progress until the International Stabilization Force deploys, Israel meets its commitments and Hamas disarms. It also said Israel had blocked the technocratic Palestinian administrative committee. Senior adviser Josh Gruenbaum told Ynet the board could hand Gaza back to UN oversight or leave the file to the next US administration. He accused elements in Netanyahu's government of obstruction.

Analysis. For the first time a Trump-created body is publicly blaming Israel for the stalled transition. That matters for the Israeli coalition talks: Eisenkot can present himself as the leader who can unlock phase two with Washington. But the threat is double-edged. A Board of Peace exit would hand the file to a UN system Israel distrusts and the US has defunded, and Israeli officials told Yedioth they have "no intention" of withdrawing. Meanwhile the creeping expansion of Israeli control is changing facts on the ground. With winter rains, more than 70,000 tents were reported flooded earlier this month.

What to watch. Any Israeli cabinet response to Gruenbaum. Movement on ISF troop contributors. Further operations beyond the Yellow Line.

6. Drones over North Africa: Khartoum UN building hit, mystery blast at Benghazi naval base.

What happened. Drones struck Khartoum between about 2:00 and 4:30 am on Sunday, around the airport and residential areas. The UN said "a UN premises with UN colleagues in the building was damaged", and OCHA called the risk "unacceptable". ABC later reported at least four civilians killed and nine injured. A Sudanese army source said the drones were launched from Ethiopia's Bahir Dar airport, "a continuation of Ethiopian aggression". Ethiopia denies it. Egypt and Saudi Arabia, both backers of the Sudanese army, condemned the strikes.

In Benghazi, a pre-dawn explosion hit ammunition depots at the naval base. The Libya Observer, citing military sources, reported strikes by "unidentified aircraft". Eastern authorities told Xinhua that a Russian vessel docked at the base may have been targeted in a drone attack. Naval analyst H I Sutton, quoted by Defence Blog, said a Russian corvette, provisionally the Merkury, was "almost certainly destroyed". Haftar-aligned media called the blasts accidental, and LNA chief of staff Khaled Haftar ordered heightened readiness.

Analysis. Both incidents show long-range drones spreading into conflicts where attribution is deliberately murky. If Ethiopia's role is confirmed, Sudan's war gains a direct interstate dimension along the Nile, with Egypt firmly on Khartoum's side, which adds to the old GERD dispute. If a Russian warship was hit in Benghazi, someone (Ukraine is the obvious suspect, though there is no evidence yet) has taken the Russia–Ukraine naval war to Haftar's port. That would test Moscow's Mediterranean posture after it lost some of its Syrian footholds, and test Haftar's value as a host. It also comes days after Egypt's rapprochement with Tripoli (see Libya below).

What to watch. Russian MoD comment on the Merkury. Any claim of responsibility. UN Security Council reaction to the Khartoum strike. Sudan–Ethiopia border incidents.
