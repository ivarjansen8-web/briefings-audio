Here is your Middle East briefing for Sunday, 22 November.

Iran strikes Bahrain and readies a tanker convoy to break the blockade, Houthis keep missiles on Riyadh, and the Gaza truce nears collapse.

Three escalation tracks are now running at once, and they feed each other. In the Gulf, Iran struck what it called US positions in Bahrain on Saturday night, with fire reported at a civilian industrial facility. More than 14 Iranian tankers are massing at Kharg Island to test a US blockade that CENTCOM says is in its 130th day, and Qatar's mediation has gone back to Tehran after a fruitless Berlin meeting with Witkoff and Kushner. On the Arabian Peninsula, the Saudi–Houthi war has become an exchange of blows aimed at regime prestige. The coalition struck Hodeidah port in a "special operation", the Houthis answered with ballistic missiles on Riyadh for a third night running (two days after hitting the PIF tower), and Houthi ground forces are tens of kilometres from Aden. Two diplomatic tracks are also sliding back. The Board of Peace says the Gaza ceasefire faces "imminent collapse" and is threatening to hand the Strip back to the UN. In Lebanon, President Aoun's Independence Day speech named Israeli occupation and Iranian interference as "inseparably linked" obstacles to sovereignty, while Israeli strikes continued. Inside Israel, Eisenkot's coalition talks with Likud open today, and they will decide who makes Israel's next war-and-peace calls. Inside Iran, rolling blackouts start today, a reminder that the blockade's economic pressure is real.

Top stories.

1. Iran hits Bahrain and readies a tanker convoy to break the US blockade.

What happened. Late on Saturday 21 November, Bahrain sounded sirens, told residents to shelter, and said its air defences intercepted several Iranian aerial attacks. The government said one attack hit a "civilian industrial facility", causing a fire but no injuries. The Bahrain Defence Force later attributed material damage to shrapnel from interceptions. The IRGC said it launched a missile-and-drone attack on US positions, naming US air-defence radar systems at al-Rafa and Awali airbases, and claimed to have destroyed two Patriot batteries and a radar. Neither Bahrain nor the US military confirmed this. Iranian state media added that a US interceptor fired from the Fifth Fleet base crashed on Sitra island and started a large fire. That claim is unverified and comes from a state outlet.

At sea, UKMTO reported two tankers hit by "unknown projectiles" north and east of Khasab on Oman's Musandam peninsula. Iran's navy separately said a tanker struck a mine in a "restricted area" east of the Strait and caught fire, which has not been independently confirmed. The IRGC also claimed to have shot down two US drones, an MQ-9 and an MQ-1, over Hormuz, which it counts as its 58th MQ-9 kill. CENTCOM called a similar claim near Qeshm "false" and denied an Iranian army claim to have damaged a US F-16 over the Gulf.

The larger story is the convoy. Satellite imagery and ship-tracking data show more than 14 tankers at Kharg Island with crews assigned and Iranian flags raised, and a convoy is expected to head for the Gulf of Oman "in the coming days". Deputy FM Kazem Gharibabadi called the blockade "an act of war" and said Iran "will take all necessary measures to lift it". Iranian officials have threatened regional oil facilities if the ships are fired on. CENTCOM says the blockade, in force since mid-July, has run 130 consecutive days.

Context. The current war began on 28 February 2026 with the US–Israeli air campaign and the killing of Ali Khamenei. An interim truce collapsed on 8 July after Iranian attacks on merchant shipping, and the US counter-blockade of Iranian ports followed. Attacks on Bahrain resumed on 13 November after a roughly two-month lull. On 18 November Iran widened them to Kuwait's Ali Al Salem air base, which it described as retaliation for US strikes, and it now uses drones more than missiles. UKMTO counted more than 30 incidents in and around Hormuz in October, the highest monthly total of the war.

Analysis. Crisis Group's Hamidreza Azizi calls Tehran's approach "deliberate, calibrated escalation": steadily raising the military, economic and political cost of keeping the blockade, without necessarily seeking all-out war. The convoy fits that logic. It forces Washington to choose between a kinetic interdiction of Iranian-flagged hulls, with escalation risk and possible strikes on Gulf oil infrastructure, and letting the blockade erode. That choice is complicated by Trump's own signal that renewed military action is something to "talk about after the beginning of the year".

Bahrain is the exposed small state. It hosts the Fifth Fleet, cannot set the pace of the conflict, and carries the domestic sensitivity of a Shia-majority population. It is also a test of how far GCC solidarity goes: Qatar and the UAE condemned the attacks, but neither has changed posture. Markets are notably calm. Brent ended the week around 91 dollars–92 on the way to a third straight weekly loss, because escorted flows through Hormuz are back to roughly pre-war volumes. That gives Iran an incentive to make its threats more credible, not less.

What to watch. Whether the Kharg convoy sails, and whether the US Navy boards, disables or shadows it. Any Iranian follow-through against Gulf energy infrastructure. Whether Bahrain or Kuwait asks for public GCC or US reassurances.

2. Saudi–Houthi war: Hodeidah "special operation", missiles on Riyadh, Houthis closing on Aden.

What happened. On Friday 20 November the Saudi-led coalition announced a "special operation". It struck Hodeidah, Sanaa, Saada and Taiz, targeting what spokesman Turki al-Maliki called "operational capabilities at Hodeida port that were used in smuggling operations and the transport of weapons and ballistic missiles". The Houthis said 41 people were killed in 24 hours, a figure not independently verified. They threatened Jeddah and other Saudi Red Sea ports and told airlines that Saudi airspace was "unsafe".

At about 01:53 on Saturday Riyadh's civil defence issued an alert. A minute later the coalition said it had intercepted three ballistic missiles aimed at the capital, and later a drone heading for the south (Al Jazeera; Al Arabiya, Saudi-owned). Civil Defence issued eight emergency alerts in 24 hours. On Friday Saudi defences had also intercepted five drones and three ballistic missiles, two of them aimed at Jazan. The UN said it was following events "with concern" and warned that the fighting threatens aid supply routes.

Context. This round follows the 19 November strike that blew out windows across seven to eight floors of the PIF tower, home of the sovereign wealth fund and Riyadh's tallest building. Reuters sources said a missile struck it; Gulf Business said debris from an intercept caused the damage. An 8 November attack had already hit Aramco's building in the same district.

On the ground, government forces withdrew from Bab al-Mandeb to defend Aden, and the Houthis moved along the coast. Reported distances from Aden range from about 40 miles to 60 to 80 km. On 20 November Maritime Executive described confused fighting and an apparent Houthi effort to cut Aden off from the rest of the country. Yemen's parliament has passed a bill designating the Houthis a terrorist organisation; it awaits PLC chair Rashad al-Alimi's signature.

Analysis. This is the most serious threat to the Saudi homeland since the 2019 Abqaiq attack, and arguably worse, because it targets the symbols of Vision 2030 (the PIF and the financial district) rather than oil facilities alone. The Hodeidah operation tries to restore deterrence by striking the Houthis' logistical lifeline, but Hodeidah also carries most of northern Yemen's imports, so the humanitarian and legal cost will land on Riyadh. Strategically, Riyadh is building a coalition of supporters. Syria is reportedly sending advisers and trainers, with AFP's Yemeni sources claiming "thousands" of non-combat troops, which Damascus denies. Turkey has ratified the Saudi–Pakistan–Turkey "Mecca Pact". The Houthi push on Aden could break the Saudi-backed southern order and has direct consequences for Bab al-Mandeb shipping.

What to watch. A Houthi strike on Jeddah or the Red Sea ports, as threatened. Whether Aden's southern defenders (PLC and southern forces) hold the coastal approach. Any US role beyond the Iran theatre. Alimi's decision on the terror-designation law.

3. Gaza: the Board of Peace warns of "imminent collapse" and threatens to give the keys to the UN.

What happened. Strikes killed nine Palestinians across Gaza over Friday and Saturday, according to The National. Gaza's civil defence said four were killed in Bureij on Friday, and Palestinian media reported three killed in Gaza City on Saturday. The IDF said it targeted fighters. WAFA (the PA's official agency) said a vehicle carrying meals for displaced people was hit. Josh Gruenbaum, the Trump-appointed senior adviser who runs the Board of Peace day to day, told Ynet: "if the political leadership doesn't want to honor its commitments", the board would "throw the keys back to the UN". He insisted the board was not asking Israel to withdraw or compromise its security.

Context. The board's first annual report to the Security Council (17 to 18 November) said both Israel and Hamas had missed key commitments. It singled out Israeli restrictions on supplies and equipment, together with funding shortfalls, and Israel's failure to set conditions for training and deploying Palestinian civilian police. It warned that if the truce collapses there will be "no road map to return to". The ISF has not deployed: Israel has not legally authorised it, and only three Moroccan officers have arrived as an advance element. Hamas accepted the board's disarmament roadmap in July, but with no ISF or police force there is nobody to hand weapons to, so the 15 November handover target passed. The board wants Israel to sign an ISF authorisation and approve a pilot disarmament scheme in Rafah. Winter storms and flooding are adding to the humanitarian crisis.

Analysis. For the first time, a US-created body is publicly blaming the Israeli government for blocking Washington's own plan. Analytically, the "keys to the UN" threat is leverage aimed at Israel's coalition politics: it raises the cost to Likud of obstruction just as government formation reaches its decisive phase. It is probably not a real exit plan, because handing Gaza to the UN would be a humiliating admission of failure for the Trump administration. The losers so far are Gazans, stuck between "war and peace", and the Arab states (Egypt, Qatar, the UAE, Morocco as an ISF contributor) that have staked political capital on the plan.

What to watch. Whether Eisenkot's coalition talks include commitments on ISF authorisation. Security Council follow-up. Winter aid access.

4. Lebanon: Aoun names Iran alongside Israel; strikes in the south continue.

What happened. In his Independence Day address from Dbayeh (broadcast on 21 November, the eve of the 22 November anniversary), President Joseph Aoun said Israel's occupation of Lebanese territory and Iranian interference were "two inseparably linked obstacles" to full sovereignty. He told Tehran it has no right to interfere in Lebanon's affairs, reaffirmed the push for Hezbollah's disarmament, and demanded Israeli withdrawal, an end to attacks, and the release of Lebanese detainees. Hezbollah answered with its own statement: "there can be no independence while more than 500 square kilometres of Lebanese territory remain under Israeli occupation". It accused the authorities of giving up the right to hold Israel accountable. The same day, Lebanon's NNA reported Israeli airstrikes on Kfar Tebnit, drone munitions on Nabatieh al-Fawqa and Shoukin, overnight shelling of Mayfadoun and Zawtar, and explosions in Wadi al-Khiam (Latin Times; A News, Turkish pro-government).

Context. Hezbollah joined the war on 2 March, after the US–Israeli attack on Iran. Lebanon's health ministry counts at least 4,386 killed and 12,407 wounded since then. A US-mediated framework agreement signed in June (Lebanon, Israel, US) set out Hezbollah disarmament, phased Israeli withdrawal and Lebanese army deployment, but implementation has stalled. Speaker Berri's line has been that disarmament can start "immediately" once Israel ends its occupation.

Analysis. No Lebanese president has so openly put Iran rhetorically alongside Israel as an obstacle to sovereignty. The move is aimed at Gulf and Western donors, at Washington ahead of any next round of the framework talks, and at a Shia constituency exhausted by a war it did not choose. It also exposes the state's dilemma. Israel's continuing strikes and occupation of more than 500 km² give Hezbollah its strongest argument against disarmament, so every strike weakens Aoun's hand. Iran, fighting for its own survival, has less to give Hezbollah than ever, which makes this the best moment for the Lebanese state to assert itself and also the most dangerous.

What to watch. Naim Qassem's formal response, expected around the anniversary of the November 2025 ceasefire. The UAE's re-engagement: Health Minister Al Sayegh met Aoun this week. Any new framework-mechanism meeting.

5. Israel: Eisenkot opens coalition talks with Likud today, with Iran in the background.

What happened. Gadi Eisenkot, whom President Herzog tasked with forming a government, invited Likud to coalition talks starting Sunday 22 November. His Yashar party presents them as aimed at a "broad" government. Likud reportedly wants a commitment not to set up a state commission of inquiry into 7 October. UTJ expects a new Haredi enlistment proposal. Eisenkot says he will try for a minority government if broad talks fail, possibly by early December, and Liberman has vowed to oppose that.

Context. The 27 October election left Netanyahu's bloc with 53 seats and Eisenkot's with 48. The Joint List and Ra'am hold 19. On 17 November the Knesset elected Yashar's Matan Kahana as speaker with 66 votes, replacing Likud's Amir Ohana. Netanyahu and Likud still claim, without evidence, that the vote was "stolen". On 20 November Liberman, who chairs the Foreign Affairs and Defence Committee, told Channel 12 there is "no security justification for striking Iran now" and that a strike ordered by the caretaker PM would be "politically motivated".

Analysis. The most plausible route to 61 is Eisenkot's bloc plus Shas and UTJ, as Liberman himself now argues. That requires a deal on Haredi enlistment that Eisenkot's secular partners will find painful. Opening talks with Likud is partly about legitimacy: it answers the "stolen election" narrative and signals unity to the security establishment and to Washington. For foreign policy, an Eisenkot-led government would probably be more willing to authorise the ISF and engage the Board of Peace, and less inclined to strike Iran unilaterally. It would not soften policy on Hezbollah or the West Bank much. A caretaker Netanyahu with war powers is the main short-term risk, as Liberman's warnings imply.

What to watch. Readouts of today's Likud–Yashar meeting. Shas and UTJ positioning. Any security-cabinet session on Iran.

The UAE health minister's visit signals Gulf re-engagement.

Syria.

International.

Deepening Saudi alignment: a defence cooperation agreement, 403 million dollars in loans, and reported Syrian advisers in Saudi Arabia.

The economy minister was replaced and al-Shaar moved to an advisory role (Hawar/ANHA, SDF-aligned).

Iraq (incl. Kurdistan Region)

International.

The Jurf al-Sakhar deal, and Qaani's mediating visit, tests whether Baghdad can rein in Iran-aligned factions as US forces withdraw.

PM al-Zaidi says the push for state authority "began" at Jurf al-Nasr, and the highest security alert has been lifted.

Reports of a fatal Basra power-station fire could not be confirmed with a dated source.

Context: Iraq is renegotiating the balance between state and factions just as the US security umbrella shrinks.

Bahrain strikes, the tanker convoy, drone claims and Qatari mediation (see Top story 1). Araghchi rules out exporting the 60% stockpile, though unnamed sources say dilution under the IAEA has been offered.
