Here is your Middle East briefing for Tuesday, 24 November.

Mines, drones and deadlock: Hormuz grinds on as the Houthis close on Aden and Ukraine sinks a Russian warship in Libya.

Four things mattered most in the past 24 to 48 hours. First, the US–Iran war has moved into a grinding maritime phase. Iran's Revolutionary Guards say three tankers hit mines in the Strait of Hormuz while under US air escort, Tehran vows "all available means" against the US blockade, and Washington has widened sanctions again instead of resuming strikes. Second, the Saudi–Houthi front is escalating. The Houthis claim a strike on the Rabigh refinery, the Houthis say a Saudi-led strike in Hodeidah killed 17, and Houthi forces are reported within 60 to 80 km of Aden. Third, Israel's coalition deadlock continues: a third round of Likud–Yashar talks ended without a deal ahead of the 6 December deadline, while settler violence in the West Bank drew Eisenkot's charge of "Jewish terrorism". Fourth, the war is spilling outward. Ukraine says it sank the Russian corvette Merkury in Benghazi harbour, pulling Libya into the Russia–Ukraine war, and drones hit a UN building in Khartoum, with Sudan's army again blaming Ethiopia. Taken together, the region's conflicts are being fought through blockade, mines and drones rather than decisive offensives, and every regional state is hedging until Washington decides, reportedly not before the new year, whether to escalate or settle.

Top stories.

1. Hormuz: mines, drones and a blockade that neither side can win quickly.

What happened. Iran's IRGC Navy said in a statement carried by IRNA that three "offending" oil tankers "suffered a severe explosion and fire after hitting naval mines" in the Strait of Hormuz. It said one was hit near the southern coast and two east of the strait while under "extensive US military air escort". Counts differ: some Iranian sources speak of up to six vessels affected between Sunday and Monday, while other accounts mention two. There is no independent confirmation of the mine strikes. On the same day the US Treasury widened sanctions on more than two dozen Iranian exchange houses, front companies and officials. Tehran's foreign ministry calls the blockade "military aggression" and says Iran will use "all available means" to counter it. Its conditions are lifting the naval blockade and sanctions and implementing the provisions of the June memorandum of understanding. Iran says Hormuz could be reopened within two days if Washington accepts its list.

On 22 November the IRGC also claimed to have downed a US MQ-1 drone east of the strait, which it called the 20th of its type and the fourth in 24 hours. It also claimed strikes on US air-defence radars at Bahrain's Al-Rafa and Awali bases, which the UAE denounced as a "flagrant violation" of Bahraini sovereignty. Trump said Iran had launched more drones and missiles in the past week than at any time in the nearly nine-month war, but that they had "done no damage". Qatar's prime minister told Al Jazeera the region cannot withstand another US–Iran escalation.

Background. The war began on 28 February with US–Israeli strikes that killed Ali Khamenei. Mojtaba Khamenei succeeded him on 8 March and has not appeared in public since. The US has blockaded Iranian ports since 13 April, apart from a pause from 18 June to 13 July. The Qatar- and Pakistan-brokered "Islamabad MoU" was signed on 18 June and collapsed in July. Washington reinstated the blockade on 14 July and Iran closed the strait to foreign shipping. Visible Iranian crude exports are near zero. Reuters counted 37 ship attacks in October, the highest monthly total of the war. The Republicans' midterm rout, which Reuters partly attributed to the war, has narrowed Trump's options. After the vote he played down new strikes, and Axios reports he will wait until the new year to decide.

Analysis. Both sides are now waging a war of economic attrition. Washington is layering naval interdiction, the new overland "Task Force Iron Gate" set up on 7 November and serial Treasury designations under "Operation Economic Outcast". Iran is trying to make the blockade too costly to sustain rather than to break it militarily, as one academic told Al Jazeera. CTP-ISW assesses that Iran's mine claims are partly informational, meant to raise insurers' and shippers' perceived risk of transiting without Iranian permission. In that sense the IRGC statement is itself a weapon whether or not the mines are real. The Gulf monarchies lose most. They are being struck (Bahrain and Kuwait), they host the US assets Iran targets, and they cannot export freely, yet they have little leverage over either Washington or Tehran. Qatar's mediating role grows, and so does the risk to it. Beijing has the most to gain from a negotiated reopening and is sending envoy Zhai June to Iran, Oman and Saudi Arabia this month.

What to watch. Whether insurers and the Joint Maritime Information Center confirm hull damage. Whether Iran escalates against Gulf infrastructure, as it did against Bahrain on 15 to 17 November. Whether Qatari mediation produces an interim "freeze-for-freeze" (strait reopening against blockade relief) before Trump's new-year decision point.

2. Yemen: the Saudi–Houthi war reignites as the Houthis close on Aden.

What happened. The Houthis said on Sunday 22 November that they had directly hit Saudi Aramco's Rabigh refinery and petrochemical complex on the Red Sea coast. Riyadh did not confirm damage, and the coalition said it intercepted a ballistic missile aimed at the western coast. Videos of a fire could not be verified. On Monday 23 November the Houthis' telecommunications ministry said a coalition air strike on telecom facilities in Hodeidah killed 17 workers and wounded 11. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree reported strikes on Sanaa, Lahj, Taiz, Hodeidah, Saada and Hajjah. AFP could not verify the claims and Riyadh did not comment. On the ground, Houthi shelling hit trucks and buses in Lahj, killing civilians, and clashes continued in Dhale, Lahj, Shabwa and Hajjah.

Background. Yemen was drawn into the Iran war in July, when the Houthi–government truce unravelled. In September Axios reported that Mohammed bin Salman twice asked Trump to strike the Houthis and was refused. Washington instead offered intelligence and targeting support. On 19 November MBS met CENTCOM commander Adm. Brad Cooper in Riyadh while Houthi missiles were fired at the capital. The Houthis have taken Al-Maqatirah and Al-Madaribah districts in Lahj and reached about 60 km from Aden. Reuters describes Aden's defenders digging trenches while "feuding among rival factions, and between their Gulf backers" undermines the defence.

Analysis. This is the most dangerous Yemeni moment since 2015. The anti-Houthi camp has fractured. The UAE has left the coalition, and the Southern Transitional Council was ejected from the Presidential Leadership Council and relaunched as the "Transitional Council of South Arabia". It now conditions any defence of Aden on a southern secession referendum. For Riyadh, the attacks on Rabigh, which sits at the western end of the East-West pipeline, matter strategically: that pipeline is the kingdom's main way of bypassing a closed Hormuz. A Houthi capability to threaten it means Saudi Arabia's Red Sea export outlet is no longer safe. If Aden falls, Iran's partner would hold both sides of Bab al-Mandab's approaches, and the south's question of statehood would reopen. In Iran-war terms, the Houthis are Tehran's most effective remaining instrument of horizontal escalation.

What to watch. Whether the Karish/Kirsh and Lahj fronts break. Whether the UAE quietly rearms southern units. Whether Washington moves from intelligence support to direct strikes, which it has so far refused.

3. Israel: coalition limbo, settler violence and the Iran file.

What happened. The third round of talks between Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar and Netanyahu's Likud ended on Monday evening in the Knesset without a deal. Negotiators Orit Farkash-Hacohen and David Peter "discussed legal issues" and set a follow-up meeting with no date. The sticking points remain who leads, with Netanyahu demanding to go first, and Likud's insistence on including Itamar Ben-Gvir. Eisenkot rejects both. The Times of Israel reports Eisenkot and Bennett split over whether to give Likud a deadline. Democrats leader Yair Golan urged Eisenkot to end the "deceitful" talks. Eisenkot's fallback is a minority "emergency national government" resting on the Change Bloc's 48 seats. Overnight on 22 November, settlers torched at least a dozen cars and vandalised homes in Kafr Malik, Deir Abu Mash'al, Deir al-Sudan and At-Tuwani. Eisenkot called it "Jewish terrorism in every sense of the word".

Background. In the October election Netanyahu's camp won 53 seats and the Eisenkot-led opposition 48. Herzog gave Eisenkot the mandate on 8 November. Netanyahu remains caretaker PM with authority over the military. On 11 November Eisenkot wrote urging him to avoid risky operations during the transition, read widely as a warning against new Iran strikes.

Analysis. The coalition arithmetic leaves three paths: a Netanyahu-inclusive unity government, a fragile minority government, or new elections. Each carries a different foreign policy. A unity government with Netanyahu would likely keep Ben-Gvir's settler constituency inside the cabinet and make restraint in the West Bank unlikely. An Eisenkot-led minority government would be more cautious on Iran and more attentive to Washington and Amman, but would struggle to pass anything. Herzog said publicly that Israel must "pay attention" to Jordan's concerns and called the Egypt and Jordan treaties the foundation of regional peace. That signals the security establishment's worry that settler violence and annexationist rhetoric are corroding Israel's most important Arab relationships. Meanwhile the caretaker period gives Netanyahu formal power without political accountability, which is exactly what Eisenkot's letter targeted.

What to watch. The 6 December mandate deadline. Any Herzog extension. Whether the Haredi parties or Arab parties, both of which Eisenkot is also courting, become kingmakers in a minority scenario.

4. Libya becomes a Russia–Ukraine battlefield: the sinking of the Merkury.

What happened. Zelensky said Ukraine used seven domestically built naval drones in an early-morning attack on Sunday 22 November to sink the Russian Steregushchiy-class corvette Merkury at Benghazi port. He said the strike also hit the cargo ship Angara and destroyed a Ka-27 helicopter, and called it "exemplary". Satellite imagery from Vantor shows the ship capsized with only the bow above water. Libyan military officials told AP that at least four drones were intercepted and two hit the ship. The eastern authorities said the ship had stopped to refuel on its way back to Russia. Benghazi's civil defence spoke of an explosion at an ammunition depot inside the naval base. The Tripoli-based Government of National Unity condemned "any unauthorized foreign military presence on Libyan soil", and the Presidential Council vowed "military, political and legal measures". UNSMIL called for an independent investigation and warned against dragging Libya into foreign conflicts.

Background. In March Russia blamed Ukrainian sea drones launched "from the Libyan coast" for sinking the LNG tanker Arctic Metagaz. Libyan officials told AP that Ukrainian forces operate in western Libya under a covert, Western-endorsed arrangement. Russia has used Haftar's east, including Benghazi and inland airbases, as a logistics hub for its Africa Corps since the fall of Assad weakened its Syrian bases.

Analysis. This is the first Ukrainian sinking of a Russian surface combatant outside the Black Sea, and it exposes the fragility of Moscow's Mediterranean posture after Tartus. For Haftar, it is humiliating. He has spent 2026 diversifying away from Russia: Ankara struck a deal with the LNA in July, and the eastern parliament ratified the 2019 Turkey–Libya maritime memorandum on 20 October. He now faces pressure to choose between Moscow's presence and Western tolerance. For Tripoli, the condemnation is double-edged, since the AP-reported Ukrainian presence is in the west. Egypt reopened its Tripoli embassy on 7 November after 12 years. Together with Turkey's eastern turn, that shows Libya's external patrons converging on a post-Russian order.

What to watch. Russian retaliation against Ukrainian assets in Libya. Haftar's response, including whether Russian access to Benghazi is curtailed. Any UN Security Council session.

5. Sudan: drones hit a UN building in Khartoum, and the Ethiopia accusation returns.

What happened. Dawn drone strikes on Sunday 22 November hit homes and a UN building in a residential area of Khartoum. UN humanitarian coordinator Denise Brown said "a UN premises with UN colleagues in the building was damaged", and OCHA called the risk "unacceptable". Casualty tolls differ: one outlet reports at least four dead, mostly children. Sudan Tribune called it the first attack on UN premises in Khartoum since the army retook the city in 2025. An army source said the drones were launched from Ethiopia, which denies it, while a UN statement reportedly attributed them to the RSF. Saudi Arabia and Egypt condemned the attack.

Background. The army-led Sovereignty Council published a report this month documenting 70 attacks between 15 February and 27 October involving 187 one-way attack drones. It named launch sites at Asosa, the Kurmuk-Gizen border area and Bahir Dar air base in Ethiopia. Since May, Port Sudan has accused the UAE of supplying the drones, which Abu Dhabi denies. In Kordofan, El Obeid has become the launchpad for the army's westward offensive. The front line remains close to the city's crowded market.

Analysis. The drone war has made the "recaptured" capital insecure again, deterring returns and reconstruction, which is likely the point. The Ethiopia accusation is regionally explosive. It links the Sudan war to the Ethiopia–Eritrea–Tigray triangle and to the Gulf rivalry: Riyadh and Cairo back the army, while Abu Dhabi is accused of backing the RSF. Saudi and Egyptian condemnations within hours show the alignment hardening. The damage to UN premises raises the cost for the humanitarian operation at the worst moment.

What to watch. Whether Port Sudan escalates diplomatically against Addis Ababa, for example by recalling its ambassador again or approaching the Security Council. Whether the army's Kordofan push reaches En Nahud.

6. Gaza: a "ceasefire" that keeps killing, and a winter without shelter.

What happened. Israeli strikes killed at least three people on 23 November, including UNRWA worker Adel al-Helo near a well in Jabalia camp. A strike on a truck that witnesses said carried food and World Central Kitchen-branded papers killed three. WCK said the vehicle was not affiliated with it, and the IDF said it targeted Islamic Jihad militants. The health ministry's cumulative toll is 74,746 killed and 176,234 wounded. Reuters counts more than 1,600 Palestinians and five Israeli soldiers killed since the October 2025 ceasefire. Last week Israel staged its first major ground incursion west of the "Yellow Line" since the truce (18 to 19 November) before declaring it "completed". Board of Peace envoy Nickolay Mladenov met Sergey Lavrov in Moscow on Gaza reconstruction, which the Times of Israel describes as stalled.

Background and analysis. The first winter storm flooded the shelters of about 26,000 households. At least five people died when an embankment collapsed near Deir al-Balah. Aid groups held only about 2,800 tents in Gaza against a need of around 121,000. UN aid chief Tom Fletcher rejected waiting for an Israeli government to be formed before winter supplies move. Mladenov has told the Security Council that "reconstruction financing will not follow where weapons have not been laid down". That sequencing (Hamas disarmament before money) amounts to a structural deadlock while Israel's government is in limbo. His Moscow stop shows the Board looking for any lever, including Russian influence with Hamas.

What to watch. Further incursions west of the Yellow Line. The next storm. Whether the PA's postponed elections and its reform agenda gain any traction with the Board.
