Blockade versus counter-blockade: Riyadh hit again, Baghdad moves on Kurdish oil, Haftar lands in Ankara.

Five things mattered most in the last 24 to 48 hours. First, the Gulf war economy is hardening. The US naval blockade of Iranian ports and Iran's counter-squeeze on Hormuz are both in force; Tehran vows "all available means" against the blockade, and Brent jumped about 2 dollarsto roughly 91 dollarsafter new reports of tankers hit near the strait. Second, the Houthi–Saudi front has become a war of capitals. A second strike in under a week on the Public Investment Fund tower in Riyadh wounded 11 people, and coalition airstrikes on Sanaa followed overnight. Third, Iraq's prime minister Ali al-Zaidi has opened a second domestic front. A week after squeezing Kataib Hezbollah out of Jurf al-Sakhar, he ordered federal control of oilfields, exports and border crossings in Kurdish-held areas, and Erbil called the move "in no way acceptable". Fourth, Gaza's "ceasefire" is fraying on both its political and its humanitarian track. Israel killed another Hamas internal-security chief, doctors warned of meningitis, and the Board of Peace says Israel's refusal to sign the stabilisation-force agreement is the main obstacle. Fifth, Turkey is turning itself into the broker of Libya: Khalifa Haftar made his first visit to Ankara and Erdoğan is planning a Libya trip. All five run on one logic. Washington has pushed any decision on renewed strikes on Iran to 2027, and regional actors are using the interval to redraw lines of control: over sea lanes, oil rents, militias and clients.

1. Hormuz: blockade versus counter-blockade, with no off-ramp before January.

What happened. The US naval blockade of Iranian ports remains in force, and attacks on tankers in and around the Strait of Hormuz continue. On 23 November Iran said it would use "all available means" to counter the blockade. Al Jazeera's Tehran correspondent noted that more than 80 percent of Iran's imports and exports normally move through Hormuz or the southern ports. Reporting from Abu Dhabi describes a live argument inside Iran over whether to push a convoy of laden tankers through the US cordon. Other reports say the Supreme National Security Council halted the dispatch of 14 tankers meant to test the blockade, but the picture remains unclear. Tehran says Hormuz could reopen "within two days" if Washington halts hostilities, lifts the blockade and waives sanctions. The Joint Maritime Information Center (JMIC) kept the strait's threat level at "severe". Gulf monitoring cited in Gulf News coverage logged "unknown projectiles" where Iran claimed naval mines. Markets reacted: Brent settled up 2.03 dollarsat about 91.40 dollarson Tuesday after an intraday low of 88.10 dollars, its weakest since late August. WTI January futures settled at 82.05 dollars. Analysts cited in those reports put oil throughput through Hormuz at roughly half of normal levels.

Background. The 2026 Iran war began on 28 February with strikes that killed Ali Khamenei. Mojtaba Khamenei was named Supreme Leader on 8 March and has not been seen in public since (Iran International, opposition-leaning, London-based). The US blockade began in April. It was lifted under a June arrangement and reinstated on 14 July after Iran re-closed the strait (United Against Nuclear Iran, a pro-sanctions advocacy group). In August, Mohsen Rezaei, newly in charge of the Supreme National Security Council, announced an "exclusion zone" starting from the US blockade line. In September, CENTCOM struck Iranian tankers off Kharg, near Jask and in the Gulf of Oman. Iran has since exempted Indian, Chinese, Iraqi and Qatari ships from its restrictions. That gives Tehran a selective, politically priced chokepoint rather than a total closure.

Analysis. Each side's coercion is now mainly economic, not kinetic. Washington's bet is that the blockade can do the work of renewed bombing. Trump told Fox News he might resume strikes "after the new year" if there is no deal, and he called Iranian negotiators "professional tap dancers". Kpler's analysis, "The US may not need more strikes on Iran as blockade tightens", makes the same point from the market side. Tehran's bet is that a market with very thin spare capacity cannot tolerate indefinite disruption. Prices are cushioned only by the IEA's forecast of a fourth-quarter surplus and by China and India buying more Russian crude. The convoy debate is the key indicator. A convoy would turn the standoff into a direct naval test of the blockade. Halting it keeps Iran's leverage on the Gulf states, through strikes on Bahrain and Kuwait, rather than on US warships. For Gulf states this is the worst of both worlds: they absorb Iranian retaliation while Washington defers the decision. That explains Qatar's urgency (below). It also explains why the 18 to 19 November Shenzhen summit of Trump, Xi and Putin produced a joint line on "stable energy flows", which Tehran denounced as "foreign interference".

What to watch. Whether Iran's laden tankers anchored off its coast start moving, and whether the US intercepts or strikes them. Whether JMIC keeps the threat level at "severe". The Qatari and Pakistani mediation channel. Any shift in Trump's "after New Year" timeline, which Netanyahu is reportedly lobbying to bring forward.

2. Houthis strike Riyadh's PIF tower again; Saudi jets pound Sanaa.

What happened. On Tuesday 24 November a projectile hit the Public Investment Fund (PIF) tower in Riyadh's King Abdullah Financial District, the city's tallest building, for the second time in less than a week. At least 11 people were wounded: two Saudis, two Indians, three Nepalis and four Egyptians. The Saudi-led coalition called it a "cowardly terrorist attack". Accounts differ on whether a drone or missile debris did the damage. Late on Tuesday, airstrikes hit Sanaa. Houthi-run al-Masirah called it "Saudi aggression" near Sanaa International Airport (Xinhua, Chinese state media). Houthi-run Saba, citing health authorities, reported 41 killed and 35 injured in coalition strikes over 48 hours across Sanaa, Hodeidah, Lahj, Taiz and Saada. Saba also said a strike on an equestrian club killed 30 Arabian horses. These figures are unverified and come from Houthi-controlled media.

Background. The first PIF-tower strike, on 19 November, broke windows on up to eight floors and caused no injuries. On 8 November the Houthis claimed a hit on the Aramco building in the same district. Since the regional war began, the group has targeted Saudi airports, ports and oil facilities. In October Egypt's parliament authorised Sisi to deploy forces to defend Saudi Arabia. On 11 November Foreign Minister Badr Abdelatty confirmed that Egyptian troops, armour, air-defence batteries and surface-to-surface missiles have been in the kingdom for months, guarding "sensitive" sites.

Analysis. The target choice is the message. The PIF tower and the Aramco building are the physical symbols of Vision 2030 and of Saudi Arabia's pitch to foreign capital. Hitting them twice in a week, with foreign workers among the wounded, tells investors that Riyadh's financial district is inside the war zone. That compounds the fiscal stress: unverified reporting puts the 2026 deficit far above budget and describes PIF capping overseas investment at 20% of assets (House of Saud, a less established outlet). The 2022 to 2025 Saudi–Houthi de-escalation is effectively dead. Riyadh is back to air campaigns, and it now relies on Egyptian air defence, a reversal of the old pattern of Gulf money buying Egyptian loyalty. Egypt gains leverage and Gulf financial support. It also risks Houthi retaliation against Red Sea shipping and the Suez revenue it has only partly recovered. Kuwait's foreign minister used the moment to restate support for UN-led efforts on Yemen. The UN track, however, has no traction while the Houthis treat Saudi Arabia as a front in Iran's war.

What to watch. A third strike on Riyadh, and whether US or Egyptian air defences are seen to be engaged. Saudi strikes on Hodeidah port, which would hurt humanitarian imports. Any Houthi move against Egyptian assets or the Bab el-Mandeb.

3. Baghdad versus Erbil: al-Zaidi orders federal takeover of Kurdish-held oilfields, exports and crossings.

What happened. On 24 November Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi ordered the federal Oil Ministry to manage oilfields under Kurdish security control in four provinces: Kirkuk, Nineveh, Diyala and Salah al-Din. Kurdish security control on the ground stays in place. Erbil must now get federal ministry approval for crude and refined-product exports, and Iraq's Border Ports Authority takes charge of official crossings in the Kurdistan Region. The Border Forces Command is to close unofficial crossings. Zaidi also dismissed Kirkuk's border-crossings chief and police chief over a smuggling probe at the state-run North Oil Company. KRG Prime Minister Masrour Barzani called the measures "in no way acceptable" and said they contradicted the substance of talks held with Baghdad two weeks earlier. Region President Nechirvan Barzani expressed "deep surprise and concern" and rejected dialogue "framed in military terms".

Background. Zaidi took office in May 2026. Over the past fortnight he has gone after Kataib Hezbollah, the most powerful Iran-aligned militia, over oil smuggling. On 14 November he fired nine officials. He then gave a deadline for the militia to leave its Jurf al-Sakhar stronghold. Quds Force commander Esmail Qaani flew to Baghdad to defuse the crisis, and Iraqi forces deployed into Jurf al-Sakhar on 20 November under a negotiated arrangement. Kataib Hezbollah wants the full handover delayed until 30 June 2027, the government's deadline for bringing all weapons under state control (FDD's Long War Journal, hawkish US think tank).

Analysis. Zaidi is using anti-smuggling, a framing that is hard to oppose, to consolidate central control over rents on two fronts at once: the Shia militia economy and the Kurdish oil-and-customs economy. The timing reflects the regional moment. Hormuz is disrupted, Iranian leverage in Iraq is stretched, and Washington wants a stronger Baghdad. Fighting both at once is risky, though. The Kurdish parties are a natural ally against the militias, and pushing Erbil into a defensive alliance with Shia factions could undo his Jurf al-Sakhar gains. Reuters-based coverage calls it his "riskiest move yet". For Turkey, which handles Kurdish exports and runs its own Libya and Syria plays, Baghdad's control over crossings matters directly. Zaidi visited Ankara in July for pipeline talks.

What to watch. Whether Kurdish security forces physically block Oil Ministry teams in Kirkuk. KDP–PUK unity. Pipeline flows and federal salary transfers to Erbil, Baghdad's traditional lever. US mediation.

4. Gaza: the ceasefire hollows out while the disarmament roadmap stalls on Israel's signature.

What happened. Israeli strikes killed at least seven Palestinians on Tuesday, including in al-Mawasi and Jabalia camp. The IDF and Shin Bet said they killed the latest head of Hamas's internal security apparatus in a Gaza City strike. Hamas said the killing is part of an Israeli plan to create a "state of chaos". Gaza's health ministry issued an epidemic alert across the Strip after a rapid rise in meningitis cases. A funeral was held for UNRWA employee Adel al-Helo. Democracy Now also reported on a new Oxfam study finding contamination by heavy metals, explosive residue, asbestos and other pollutants across 99% of Gaza's territory. Israel, under international pressure, pledged to advance delayed sewage repairs. The Times of Israel also reports Jared Kushner warning Israel of "global-pariah status" as the Gaza plan stagnates.

Background. The Board of Peace's first annual report, published on 17 November, warned the ceasefire could collapse. It said Israel has not allowed the technocratic administration to begin work. A board adviser said the Status of Forces Agreement for the International Stabilization Force has sat unsigned by Netanyahu for more than six months. Board officials have floated handing Gaza "back to the UN" if this continues. Hamas and the other factions accepted a 15-point disarmament roadmap. Under it, heavy weapons, tunnels, depots and personal arms pass to a National Committee for the Administration of Gaza under a UN-recognised transition. Israel has not signed it. Netanyahu insists on full disarmament before any withdrawal or reconstruction. The Security Council mandate behind the board runs to the end of 2027.

Analysis. Israel's targeted killings of Hamas internal-security commanders have a governance logic as well as a military one. Removing the police apparatus produces the "chaos" that both Hamas and the Board warn about, and it undercuts the claim that a Palestinian transitional authority could take over. With Israel in caretaker mode after the 27 October election (see story 5), Netanyahu has little to gain from signing a status-of-forces agreement that would constrain the IDF. The Board's UN threat is the first sign of US-side frustration turning into leverage. Watch whether Kushner's "pariah" warning shows up in US policy rather than staying in rhetoric. Arab states that pledged troops or money, including Egypt, Indonesia, Pakistan and the Gulf, are reluctant to deploy into a non-permissive environment without Israeli consent.

What to watch. Any Israeli movement on the status-of-forces agreement during coalition talks. WHO and UNICEF verification of the meningitis outbreak. Further killings of Hamas security officials and the possibility of a Hamas response.

5. Israel's post-election limbo: Eisenkot holds the mandate, Netanyahu fishes for the Haredim.

What happened. The Times of Israel liveblog for 24 November reports that Netanyahu tried and failed to hold talks with United Torah Judaism (UTJ) lawmakers. President Herzog gave the mandate to Yashar leader Gadi Eisenkot, who received 67 recommendations to Netanyahu's 46. Eisenkot's deadline is 6 December. He has invited Likud to "marathon" unity talks, and his camp has floated bringing a minority government to a vote if needed. Meanwhile, settler attacks around Ramallah on the night of 22 November, which torched homes and about a dozen cars in Deir al-Sudan, Deir Abu Mash'al and Kafr Malik, became a national political issue. Eisenkot called them "Jewish terrorism".

Background. In the 27 October election, Netanyahu's bloc won 53 seats and Eisenkot's alliance 48, neither reaching 61. UTJ wants a Haredi draft-exemption law passed before it joins any coalition. Avigdor Liberman has dropped his veto on the Haredi parties if they accept conscription. The Shin Bet has criticised Defence Minister Israel Katz for refusing administrative detention for Jewish extremists. Army Radio reports a 74% two-year rise in extremist attacks during the olive harvest.

Analysis. The Haredi draft is the hinge of Israeli politics. Netanyahu cannot meet UTJ's demand without losing the secular right, and Eisenkot cannot meet it without losing Liberman. A minority Eisenkot government relying on outside support, possibly including Arab parties, which Netanyahu has already framed as illegitimate, would be fragile. It would, however, change Israel's posture on Gaza's stabilisation force, on settler violence and on relations with the EU, where Kallas says she is "ready to engage with the next government". If neither side forms a government, Israel heads to elections in spring 2027 while still at war on three fronts.

What to watch. Likud–Yashar talks. Whether UTJ engages with Eisenkot. The 6 December deadline. Whether Herzog extends it.

6. Turkey courts Haftar, as Libya's frozen conflict becomes a Russia–Ukraine theatre.

What happened. Khalifa Haftar arrived in Ankara on 24 November at Erdoğan's invitation. It is his first visit to his old adversary. No official readout had been published at the time of writing. Erdoğan plans his first visit to Libya in 15 years, in December, as unity talks stall. Hakan Fidan and intelligence chief İbrahim Kalın met Tripoli premier Abdulhamid Dbeibah in Istanbul on 21 November. The visit came days after a Ukrainian strike on a Russian warship in Libya, which Al Jazeera says raises questions about Libya's reunification process.

Background. Weeks ago the eastern House of Representatives ratified the 2019 Turkey–Tripoli maritime memorandum it had long opposed. On 19 November Dbeibah signed nine agreements with Sisi in Cairo amid a maritime dispute. A power-sharing formula under discussion would keep Dbeibah as premier and make Saddam Haftar head of a presidential council. Western Libyan factions resist this, and Dbeibah rejects any new transitional body.

Analysis. Ankara is trying to become guarantor to both Libyan camps, moving from patron of Tripoli to the hub of the whole country. That would lock in the maritime memorandum against Greece and Egypt and give Turkey construction and energy deals. Russia's naval logistics in the east, now exposed to Ukrainian strikes, make Haftar more willing to hedge. Egypt, which has historically backed Haftar, is courting Dbeibah in response. Brussels and Athens have the most to lose from a Turkish-brokered settlement that endorses the maritime memorandum.

What to watch. The Haftar–Erdoğan readout. The dates of Erdoğan's Libya trip and any Haftar–Dbeibah meeting. Russian reactions. Greece's response to further endorsement of the maritime memorandum.

In Gaza, seven were killed on 24 November, including the head of Hamas internal security. A meningitis epidemic alert is in force.

In the West Bank, settler arson hit Deir al-Sudan, Deir Abu Mash'al and Kafr Malik, and Hamza Abdo was shot dead near Beit El.

Abbas's legislative elections, originally set for this Saturday, have been postponed to September 2027.

Context: One year into the "ceasefire", Gaza has neither war nor peace. The PA has deferred its own renewal, and the West Bank is moving toward the settler-violence crisis the Shin Bet has warned about.

Lebanon.

International.

The US is consulting countries on a post-UNIFIL force to verify disarmament, possibly fewer than 500 observers. Lebanon wants a UN mandate.

New US sanctions target the Wa Ta'awanu charity and an IRGC-to-Hezbollah gold-and-cash network. Analysts expect limited effect on the group.

Local.

On Independence Day (22 November), Hezbollah said there can be "no independence" while Israel holds more than 500 km² of Lebanese territory. President Aoun again demanded a full Israeli withdrawal and an end to Iranian interference (Arutz Sheva, Israeli right-wing outlet).

Israeli strikes and shelling continued across Marjayoun, Bint Jbeil, Tyre and Nabatieh on 24 November, with no casualties reported.

Context: The June trilateral framework is stuck on sequencing: Israel wants disarmament first, Hezbollah wants withdrawal first. The verification force is the US attempt to break the circle.

Syria.

International.

EU foreign ministers backed moving ahead with an EU–Syria Association Agreement. The US is discussing a multinational demining-training mission.

Israeli forces continue incursions in Quneitra, including a checkpoint on 24 November.

Local.

A suspected IS attack in Deir ez-Zor wounded two soldiers. Ordnance-clearance teams are surveying the Damascus–Palmyra–Deir ez-Zor road.

SDF integration into state institutions is due by year-end. The process is now about defining local roles for former SDF units.

Context: Damascus is gaining Western acceptance faster than it is gaining control on the ground. IS cells in the east and Israeli presence in the south define the limits.

Jordan.

International.

King Abdullah hosted Uzbekistan's President Mirziyoyev (23 to 24 November) and issued a joint statement. He left for the UK on 24 November.

The Jordan–EU Investment Conference with von der Leyen (19 November) and a visit by US Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin underline Jordan's role as a stable Western partner.

Local.

Ayman Awaisheh became Public Security Directorate chief on 22 November.

After the Karak church attack, the King has vowed to confront "terrorist outlaws" and named Israeli expansionism as the region's greatest threat.

Context: Jordan is converting its stability into Western and Asian investment while managing a rare jihadist attack at home.

Iraq (incl. Kurdistan Region)

International.

Qaani's mediation over Jurf al-Sakhar shows Tehran still acts as arbiter of Iraq's militia politics, even weakened.

Al-Zaidi's planned Cairo visit and the Egypt–Jordan electricity link point to an Arab hedge. Iraqi ships are exempt from Iran's Hormuz restrictions.

Local.

Baghdad ordered federal management of Kurdish-held oilfields and control of Kurdish exports and border crossings. Masrour Barzani called it "in no way acceptable".

Federal forces are in Jurf al-Sakhar, but how far Kataib Hezbollah has withdrawn and the fate of displaced residents are still unclear.

Kirkuk's border-crossings chief and police chief were dismissed over the North Oil Company smuggling probe.

Context: Iraq's most assertive centralising premier in years is testing the militias and Erbil at once. Iran's distraction gives him room, but the two-front fight is a big gamble.

Iran.

International.

Iran vows "all available means" against the blockade and is debating a tanker convoy. Qatar and Pakistan are carrying proposals, and Trump has pushed any strike decision to 2027.

The IAEA says it cannot account for Iran's nuclear material. The EU is preparing new sanctions. The GCC and Arab League condemned the strike on Bahrain.

Local.

State media released video of the Assembly of Experts venue that chose Mojtaba Khamenei, a sign of continuing legitimacy anxiety.

The rial has more than halved in a year and Tehran has power cuts. Opposition sources report continuing executions of protesters (NCRI, opposition).

Context: A wartime system with an invisible leader is betting it can outlast a blockade. The squeeze on revenue, currency and electricity is tightening.

Turkey.

International.

Turkey is hosting Haftar, preparing an Erdoğan visit to Libya and meeting Dbeibah. Ankara is positioning itself as the broker for all of Libya.

Erdoğan's harsh rhetoric on Gaza continues alongside Turkey's role as a Gaza guarantor.

Local.

Erdoğan is seeking broad consensus on the "Terror-free Türkiye" process. The New Party is boycotting the monitoring committee (Daily Sabah, pro-government).

Bloomberg reports a strategy to retake Istanbul through arrests and threats. The central bank sees 29.5% inflation at year-end.

Context: Turkey is gaining ground abroad, in Libya, Syria and the PKK process, while tightening control at home. The Iran war is adding to inflation.

Saudi Arabia.

International.

After the second PIF-tower strike the coalition resumed bombing Sanaa. Egypt is helping defend the kingdom. Saudi Arabia has not publicly commented on the Egyptian deployment.

China's special envoy visited Riyadh this month as Beijing seeks to keep Gulf energy flowing.

Local.

Eleven people, mostly foreign workers, were wounded in the financial district. The US embassy issued an alert.

Fiscal pressure and PIF strategy changes are reported but unverified.

Context: The kingdom's détente era is over. Riyadh is fighting the Houthis again while defending the credibility of Vision 2030.

United Arab Emirates.

International.

The UAE condemned Iran's "renewed hostile attack" on Bahrain. ADNOC Drilling's Kuwait expansion shows Emirati capital moving across the Gulf.

Local.

Two crashes in Ras Al Khaimah killed 27 people, prompting fleet-safety measures.

Mohammed bin Rashid launched "Mira", an AI government assistant. Preparations for the 55th Eid Al Etihad are under way.

Context: It was a quiet day for the UAE. Abu Dhabi keeps a low profile while its neighbours absorb Iranian and Houthi fire.

Qatar.

International.

On 24 November al-Ansari called for Hormuz to reopen and for US–Iran talks to resume. He warned escalation would undermine mediation, after the PM's meetings with Vance and Rubio.

Doha's earlier seven-day trust-building plan is still on the table.

Local.

Qatari ships are exempt from Iran's Hormuz restrictions, which partly protects LNG exports.

Context: Qatar's mediation is its insurance policy. Its LNG exports depend on the strait staying at least partly open.

Kuwait.

International.

The Foreign Minister reaffirmed support for UN efforts in Yemen and for Yemen's unity.

The GCC is setting up an advanced command base in Kuwait after drone attacks.

Local.

A diesel-theft ring was broken up with 13 arrests, and enforcement of company-ownership irregularities is under way.

Kuwait faces a 6 billion dollars debt payment next year.

Context: Kuwait is on the front line of Iranian retaliation and is turning to collective GCC defence.

Bahrain.

International.

Iran's strike on Sitra, near the Bapco refinery, injured two people. The IRGC claimed it was targeting US bases. The GCC and the Arab League condemned the strike.

Local.

On 16 November 64 Shia defendants were sentenced to life over an alleged Iran-linked group.

Context: As host of the US Fifth Fleet, Bahrain is Iran's most exposed target. The war is hardening sectarian security policy at home.

Oman.

International.

Sultan Haitham's November visit to Beijing, the first by an Omani head of state, focused on Hormuz. Xi said the Gulf should not become an arena for great-power rivalry.

Local.

National Day holidays run 25 to 28 November, after a royal pardon of 344 prisoners.

Context: Little happened in the past 48 hours. Oman's quiet diplomacy over Hormuz is on hold during the holidays. Iran attacked navigation systems on Didamar island earlier this month.

Yemen.

International.

The Houthi campaign against Saudi Arabia is directly tied to the Iran war. Egypt's deployment to Saudi Arabia and the Houthis' warnings to Cairo widen the circle.

Local.

Coalition strikes hit Sanaa (near the airport), Hodeidah, Lahj, Taiz and Saada. Houthi media claim 41 killed in 48 hours.

Saba (Houthi-run) reports a strike that killed 30 Arabian horses in northern Sanaa.

Context: Yemen is again a hot Saudi–Houthi war. The UN track and the internationally recognised government are sidelined.
