Here is your Middle East briefing for Saturday, 14 November.

Gulf allies rein in Trump on Iran as Turkey and Qatar seal a defence pact and the Houthis pound Saudi Arabia.

Five things set the region's direction over the past 24 to 48 hours. First, the US–Iran "no war, no peace" standoff got sharper in two ways at once. Trump said he would hold off on new strikes at the request of Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Bahrain and Kuwait. Meanwhile the IRGC claimed a hit on a US radar in Bahrain (CENTCOM says it shot down both drones) and said two more tankers struck mines in Hormuz. The Gulf monarchies are now openly acting as a brake on Washington. Second, Turkey and Qatar signed a mutual-defence pact in Istanbul. Together with the GCC's new forward command in Kuwait and August's Turkey–Saudi–Pakistan arrangement, it adds to a regional security architecture that no longer assumes the US umbrella is enough. Third, the Saudi–Houthi war is now Riyadh's most direct front: missiles and drones aimed at Riyadh and the Mecca region, strikes on the East–West pipeline, and a government counter-offensive in Yemen that has displaced more than 300,000 people since July. Fourth, in Israel the pressure around Gadi Eisenkot's 28-day mandate is no longer only about arithmetic. Netanyahu's push for renewed action against Iran, opposed by IDF chief Zamir, is now a factor in coalition-building. Fifth, Gaza's fourth winter in tents is turning a political stalemate over Hamas disarmament and the Board of Peace into an acute shelter and health emergency.

Top stories.

1. Trump pauses new Iran strikes at Gulf request as the IRGC tests the blockade from Bahrain to Hormuz.

What happened. Trump said he would hold off on new military action against Iran "for now" after appeals from regional partners. He named Qatar, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain, Kuwait "and others" as preferring that Washington not launch fresh attacks, and said the naval blockade of Iranian ports would stay in place. Some reports quote him as saying of the Saudi request, "I'm not sure I agree with it." In the same period the IRGC said it had struck a US early-warning radar "on Bahrain Island." It called this retaliation for a US air attack on a telecommunications tower in Sirik, on Iran's Hormuz coast. US forces said they shot down two IRGC one-way attack drones headed for Bahrain and that nothing was damaged. Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei warned that states giving military or logistical help to US operations would be treated as participants. That warning is aimed squarely at Bahrain, which hosts the US Fifth Fleet.

In the Strait, the IRGC Navy said two oil tankers exploded and caught fire after striking sea mines. It said they were crossing "without coordination with Iranian authorities," on an "unauthorized route," with transponders switched off. The claim has not been independently confirmed. CENTCOM rejected a similar IRGC claim earlier and maintains that the main lanes were cleared. Washington, for its part, has widened its pressure campaign: CENTCOM has launched a task force to disrupt Iran's land-based supply routes, extending the months-long naval blockade inland. The economic war has also personalised. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, architect of the "Economic Outcast" sanctions campaign, has moved onto a military base after Iran-linked threats. On 13 November he said he "won't be cowed" and that the campaign was working.

Background. The war began with joint US–Israeli strikes on 28 February 2026. An April ceasefire and a June US–Iran memorandum of understanding that opened a 60-day negotiating window did not end fighting in and around Hormuz. Washington reinstated the naval blockade in July. After meeting Qatari PM and Foreign Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani on 9 November, Trump declared that Iran "militarily, is effectively over" and called new strikes "quite unlikely". He then said on Truth Social that "we don't need a deal with Iran… We've got their Strait". Rubio had already said the parties were not "on the verge of a breakthrough." Domestically, Trump has acknowledged that "part of" the Republicans' midterm losses "was Iran," and the incoming Democratic House majority will chair every oversight committee.

Analysis. NPR sums up the White House line as "keep things as they are". The blockade plus sanctions is cheaper than renewed bombing, plays better after the midterms, and lets Trump claim victory. For Gulf capitals the calculation is defensive. The IMF projects GCC output to shrink 4.8% in 2026, with Saudi Arabia in a rare recession (–3.3%). Renewed US strikes would almost certainly bring Iranian retaliation on desalination plants, ports and airports. The paradox is that the Gulf asks for restraint while hosting the radars and bases that make it a target, and Baghaei's "participants" doctrine is designed to exploit that tension. Iran's own leverage is coercion at sea. Mine claims, real or not, raise insurance premiums and deter traffic without Tehran having to sink anything. Its exemptions for Indian, Chinese, Iraqi and Qatari shipping turn the Strait into a tool of differentiated diplomacy. The clearest winner of the pause is Riyadh, which gains leverage over Washington. The losers are Israel's hawks: Netanyahu has reportedly been lobbying Trump to resume the war (see story 4).

What to watch. Whether CENTCOM publishes evidence on the tanker incidents. Any Iranian follow-up against Bahrain. The IAEA Board of Governors' November session: the agency is "increasingly concerned" about being denied access to the underground Isfahan enrichment facility and the roughly 200 kg of highly enriched uranium believed to sit in tunnels there. And the EU Foreign Affairs Council, reportedly early next week, which may take up French calls for cyber-sanctions on Iran.

2. Turkey and Qatar sign a mutual-defence pact: the Gulf's security insurance diversifies.

What happened. On Friday 13 November in Istanbul, President Erdoğan and Emir Tamim bin Hamad signed a mutual-defence agreement at the 12th meeting of the Turkey–Qatar Supreme Strategic Committee. It was one of five agreements signed. According to the Turkish presidency, both sides commit to a unified response to any external armed attack on either party, to consult on threats, and to expand joint exercises and defence-industrial cooperation. The full text has not been published. Ankara says the pact targets no third country and is open to others, naming Egypt.

Background. Turkey has had a base in Qatar since the 2017 blockade crisis. Qatar was hit by Iranian fire in the war's opening weeks, and was struck by Israel in Doha in 2025. In August, Turkey joined a separate defence arrangement with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan. On 10 November, GCC defence ministers meeting in Manama approved a forward command of the Unified Military Command in Kuwait, with assigned forces. Secretary-General Jassim Al-Budaiwi gave no details on its size or timeline. Saudi Arabia also signed its first defence cooperation agreement with post-Assad Syria on 4 November.

Analysis. This is hedging, not alignment. Qatar remains the host of al-Udeid and Washington's preferred mediator: Sheikh Mohammed was at the White House on 9 November. The war has taught Doha that US guarantees did not stop either Iranian or Israeli munitions from landing on its territory. A treaty with a NATO member that has a deployed garrison and a capable drone and air-defence industry adds a second layer of deterrence, and a channel to Ankara's ties with Tehran. For Turkey it is another strategic win in a year when Erdoğan is hosting COP31 and positioning Ankara as an indispensable Muslim-world security provider. It also puts Turkish guarantees directly in the Gulf at a moment when Israel–Turkey relations are poor. The open invitation to Egypt matters too: a Cairo–Ankara–Doha triangle would have been unthinkable five years ago. The losers are those who wanted a US-led "regional security architecture" with Israel at its core, an idea the Gaza and Iran wars have made politically radioactive in Gulf capitals. Al Jazeera's op-ed page captures the mood: the Gulf "must build security that can outlast this war".

What to watch. Whether the text is published and whether it has an automatic-response clause (Article 5-like) or only commits to consultation. Reactions from Riyadh, Abu Dhabi and Tehran. And whether Egypt responds to Ankara's invitation.

3. The Saudi–Houthi war: missiles toward Riyadh and Mecca, an embattled pipeline, and a ground war in western Yemen.

What happened. The Saudi-led coalition said it destroyed three cruise missiles headed for the Riyadh region, two drones aimed at Mecca and the south, and two missiles fired west. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree denied targeting Mecca, saying the group strikes only "oil facilities and military bases". Earlier in the week the East–West pipeline in the Medina region was hit again, causing what the Energy Ministry told SPA was a "limited fire". Debris from an "attempted attack" also damaged the Aramco tower in Riyadh's financial district. Al Jazeera's live page for 13 November reported Houthi claims of further Aramco attacks and Saudi–Chinese talks on the Houthis. Last month a Houthi strike on King Khalid International Airport killed 13 and wounded more than 300. Houthi-run Saba, a partisan source, reports that Saudi strikes killed at least 33 near Sanaa on 7 November and that more than 100 strikes hit Marib, Sanaa, Hodeidah, Taiz, Lahj and al-Jawf in 24 hours. These figures cannot be verified. On the ground, the government's "Dawn of Yemen" counter-offensive is a month old. The Houthis have besieged Taiz for over a month and in early November took al-Maqatirah in Lahj, about 80 km from Aden. Both sides claim heavy enemy losses. IOM data put displacement above 300,000, nearly all of it in two months. The UN counts more than 1,600 dead since the 2022 truce collapsed in July.

Background. The Saudi–Houthi détente of 2022 to 2025 collapsed in July 2026 under the pressure of the Iran war. On 20 July the Houthis declared a blockade of Saudi ports and shipping. They then took ground along the Red Sea coast in September, which triggered the government offensive. The East–West pipeline to Yanbu matters more than ever, because Iranian restrictions in Hormuz make Red Sea export routes Riyadh's lifeline. That explains why the Houthis are targeting it.

Analysis. Riyadh is fighting a two-front coercion problem: Iran's chokehold on Hormuz and the Houthis' threat to the Red Sea bypass. Its answer has three parts. Militarily, it is backing the government's ground offensive. Diplomatically, it has gone to Beijing: NSA Musaad al-Aiban carried a letter from MBS to Xi on 2 November, and Reuters reported that China then privately asked Tehran to rein in the Houthis. In Washington, it is asking the US not to escalate with Iran. This is a coherent de-escalation strategy aimed at Tehran, not Sanaa. It assumes Iran still has decisive influence over the Houthis, which the movement's growing autonomy calls into question. Beijing has publicly denied a Wall Street Journal report that Chinese components were found in Houthi missiles. The G7, meeting in Paris on 9 to 10 November, condemned Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia and urged Iran to stop arming the group. Humanitarian collapse in Taiz and Marib, a market strike in al-Waziyah that killed at least 30 (blame contested; Al Jazeera), and displacement toward Aden (which already hosts more than 56,000 IDPs) are weakening the government side's social base even where it advances.

What to watch. Whether a proposed pause tied to lifting Saudi naval restrictions on Yemen (the Houthis' "humanitarian issue") gets traction through Muscat or Beijing. Any Houthi strike on Yanbu or Jeddah. And whether the Lahj front threatens Aden.

4. Israel: Eisenkot's mandate meets Netanyahu's Iran push.

What happened. President Herzog gave Gadi Eisenkot (Yashar) the mandate on 7 to 8 November after factions with 67 of 120 seats recommended him. That total includes the Arab-led Ra'am (8) and Joint List (11), whose recommendations were "outside" support. Eisenkot has 28 days from 8 November, extendable by 14. His four-party bloc (Yashar; Bennett and Lapid's B'Yachad; Liberman's Yisrael Beytenu; Golan's Democrats) holds 48 seats, against 46 for Netanyahu's camp. Three of the four have pledged not to govern with Arab parties. On 12 November Haredi leaders were said to be fielding "multiple overtures". KAN reported that Ra'am and the Joint List are in contact with United Torah Judaism. Ra'am's Mansour Abbas has said he would join for the internal security portfolio, while admitting that even an Arab-backed minority government probably won't happen.

On Iran, Channel 12 and KAN report that IDF Chief of Staff Eyal Zamir again warned against "ill-advised" action that Netanyahu wants. KAN says Zamir supports a possible future operation but thinks the timing is wrong. He is reportedly alone among the security chiefs in opposing an immediate strike that could derail coalition talks. Ynet and Yedioth say Netanyahu repeatedly urged Trump in a weekend call to restart the war. Netanyahu's office called reports of a "shouting match" "complete nonsense". Eisenkot wrote to Netanyahu demanding he "act with national responsibility." Netanyahu's office replied that Eisenkot was trying to form a government "with terror supporters."

Background. The 27 October election ended Netanyahu's majority. Arab turnout nearly doubled; experts attributed this to organic social-media mobilisation. Likud claimed the vote was "stolen" through Qatari money and fraud. Police and election officials found no basis for this, and the Shin Bet indicated any foreign interference was limited. CNN reported that Netanyahu pushed for a joint strike before the election and Trump refused, partly because of Zamir's warning that it could force a postponement of the vote.

Analysis. Netanyahu is caretaker prime minister with a war he wants to restart and an opponent who needs calm to assemble a coalition. Every option open to Eisenkot means breaking a pledge. He can rely on Arab votes, which Bennett and Liberman reject. He can bring in Shas and UTJ, which collides with his own promise to pass a Haredi draft law. Or he can accept a unity government with Likud, which he broke off after calling Netanyahu's speech "delusional". The Netanyahu camp's "stolen election" and "terror supporters" framing is meant to make the Arab option toxic. A security crisis, especially renewed war with Iran, would strengthen the case for a unity government and Netanyahu's continued centrality. So Zamir's resistance and Trump's reluctance are, in effect, protecting Israel's democratic transition. For foreign-policy watchers the key point is this: an Eisenkot-led government would probably be more cooperative with Washington on the Board of Peace and Lebanon, but no more dovish on Iran.

What to watch. Haredi decisions (Shas and UTJ). Whether Ra'am accepts the four "governing principles" Yashar sources mention. Any security-cabinet meeting on Iran. And the 6 December deadline (28 days from 8 November), with the possible 14-day extension.

5. Gaza's fourth winter: floods, hypothermia and a stalled disarmament roadmap.

What happened. OCHA's report for 13 November says heavy rain since 6 November may have affected more than 46,000 households, including more than 5,000 reportedly sleeping without shelter. That figure was given as 26,000 households on 8 November. A UN tally cited by The National put 6,000 family shelters destroyed and 17,400 damaged. The UN aid chief criticised delays in relief and rejected waiting for Israel to form a government. Democracy Now reported that a Palestinian infant died of hypothermia in a Khan Younis tent, and that 49 states called on Israel to ease limits on shelter, fuel, water and medical supplies. The WHO reports a rise in respiratory infections after flooding. Violence continues under the ceasefire. A civil defence official told AFP that an Israeli strike on Nasser Hospital in Khan Younis on 12 November killed six and wounded about 20. A Palestinian was killed by Israeli fire east of Gaza City, and the IDF said it struck a weapons-production site this week. An aid group running Gaza's only clinic that makes 3D-printed masks for burn victims says Israel is blocking its imports because it won't submit a list of local staff.

Background. The US-brokered ceasefire of October 2025 and UNSCR 2803 created the Board of Peace and the plan for an International Stabilization Force. On 31 July the Board announced a 15-point disarmament roadmap mediated by Egypt, Qatar, Turkey and the US. It provides for verified decommissioning, a new Palestinian police force and phased Israeli withdrawal. Hamas still controls roughly half the Strip and rejects "imposed guardianship." Albania and Kosovo have committed to the ISF. In Paris this week the G7 restated support for implementing 2803, said Hamas can have no role and must decommission, and called for progressive Israeli withdrawal.

Analysis. The humanitarian file is now tied to Israeli coalition politics. A caretaker government has little incentive to loosen restrictions on "dual-use" shelter and fuel items, and COGAT's staff-list demands on NGOs are tightening control over who operates in Gaza. Each winter death strengthens the Arab and European case that Israel's restrictions, not Hamas alone, are obstructing reconstruction. The Arab Center's argument that analysts misread Hamas disarmament (it is a political bargain over the group's future, not a technical decommissioning exercise) is the right lens. Without an Israeli government able to sign off on withdrawal lines, the roadmap stalls by default.

What to watch. The next OCHA updates on shelter entry approvals. Any Board of Peace meeting on ISF deployment. Whether a new Israeli coalition agreement mentions the Board or the Palestinian Authority. And casualty figures from the Nasser Hospital strike.

6. Iraq: Baghdad's showdown with Kataib Hezbollah, and Kurdistan's split government.

What happened. On Thursday 12 November, Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi met PMF chairman Falih al-Fayyadh, chief of staff Abdul Aziz al-Mohammadawi and Badr leader Hadi al-Ameri for more than three hours. The aim was to defuse a confrontation with Kataib Hezbollah over Baghdad's campaign to put factional weapons under state control. Zaidi reportedly insisted that "those who break the law must be dealt with under Iraq's laws." Kurdistan24 reports he rejected a proposed Coordination Framework deal under which KH would give up weapons in exchange for government posts, and security sources warn of a possible armed clash. Alhurra reported that Zaidi called off a planned raid on KH's Jurf al-Sakhar stronghold after it leaked. Separately, Iraq expects its first purchases of air-defence systems to arrive soon. This follows the 8 November drone strike that halted Dana Gas's Khor Mor field and cut about 2.8 GW, roughly two-thirds of Kurdistan's power. Iraq's national security chief said an intra-Kurdish attack was not ruled out. The KRG's natural resources minister called that "nonsense".

Background. Zaidi has set 30 June 2027 as the deadline for disarming Iran-aligned factions. KH and al-Nujaba reject handing over weapons. In Kurdistan, parliament reconvened on 4 November after a two-year hiatus and elected a presidency board. The PUK rejects that session's legitimacy, and Bafel Talabani announced the PUK's withdrawal from the KRG cabinet. US envoy Tom Barrack has urged the KDP and PUK to reconcile and cooperate with Baghdad.

Analysis. Zaidi is testing whether an Iraqi premier can use the weakening of Iran to break the "muqawama" factions' veto. The Shia Coordination Framework that put him in office is split, and some members fear a long fight would make others rally behind KH. Ameri's role is pivotal: Badr is both an Iranian ally and an establishment insider. For Washington, a successful disarmament process would be the most significant regional dividend of the war. A botched raid could reignite attacks on US forces and Gulf-linked energy assets like Khor Mor. In the north, the return of "administrative dualism" (KDP in Erbil, PUK in Sulaymaniyah) weakens the Kurds just as Baghdad is reasserting itself.

What to watch. Any movement around Jurf al-Sakhar. Findings of the Khor Mor investigative committee. And whether the PUK's withdrawal hardens into a parallel administration.

International.

A US reunification proposal, reported by AFP, would keep Dbeibah as prime minister and make Saddam Haftar head of the Presidential Council. Massad Boulos has warned spoilers of sanctions.

Egypt reopened its Tripoli embassy after 12 years and objected to the 2019 Turkey–Libya maritime MoU. Erdoğan received Dbeibah while hosting Khaled Haftar.

Egypt's intelligence chief Hassan Rashad again hosted Saddam Haftar in Cairo for talks on the political process.

Local.

The fight over the Central Bank of Libya governorship and the dinar's slide are the domestic undercurrent. A reported US suspension of dollar clearing is unconfirmed. Boulos says the UN will soon call a new round of talks.

Context: Cairo and Ankara are converging on a Dbeibah–Haftar family condominium. Foreign Affairs calls it a "false peace" that doesn't address the economy of rent.

Egypt.

International.

The foreign minister met the EU Special Representative for the Middle East on Palestinian issues. Egypt condemned continued attacks on Saudi Arabia, and Cairo is courting Japanese investment.

Turkey's invitation for Egypt to join the Turkey–Qatar defence framework puts Cairo in an unusual position between Ankara and its Gulf patrons.

Egypt has become the first non-European country to gain EU digital-signature trust recognition.

Local.

A Sisi decree lets Red Sea state land be used for debt reduction. Madbouly ordered a ministry-by-ministry plan to reach about 69.5% debt-to-GDP by 2030.

Halliburton signed a 50-well drilling programme.

Context: Egypt's model since Ras El-Hekma, trading land for Gulf capital, is becoming formal fiscal policy, and critics say it only buys time.

Sudan.

International.

No new major diplomatic initiative surfaced in the window. The army's report of a drone crossing from Ethiopia into Blue Nile adds to accusations of an expanding foreign-supplied drone war.

Local.

The army and the Joint Force claim to have retaken Sarif Ankoush on the al-Khuwei–En Nahud road, threatening the RSF's main West Kordofan stronghold. This follows the earlier capture of Iyal Bakhit.

West and south-west of El Obeid, both sides claim the same villages.

An RSF drone strike on IDPs in Bani Shako (Blue Nile) killed 9 to 16 people. The army says it tracked more than 180 drones in two months.

Context: The war's centre of gravity has shifted to Kordofan and to drone strikes on infrastructure. Both are hard to verify, and both hit civilians hard.

Mauritania.

International.

Bourita's discreet visit to Nouakchott after MINURSO's expiry shows Rabat courting Mauritania's "positive neutrality" on the Sahara. Neither side has issued an official statement.

Ghazouani attended COP31 in Antalya. Earlier this month he received Spain's interior minister Grande-Marlaska on migration (AMI; AMI, state agency).

Local.

The national dialogue and the third-term debate dominate. Opposition parties are united against amending term limits.

Context: Nouakchott is being pulled between Rabat and Algiers just as the Sahara loses its UN buffer.

Israel.

International.

Netanyahu and Trump are reported to be at odds over resuming the Iran war. Netanyahu's office denies a shouting match.

The commander of the US Navy's 5th Fleet concluded a visit to Israel.

Biden's memoir accuses Netanyahu of "empty promises" on Gaza civilians. Chris Van Hollen, an Israel critic, will challenge Chuck Schumer for Senate Democratic leader, a sign of the US party's shift.

Local.

Eisenkot's mandate (deadline around 6 December) depends on Haredi or Arab support. Haredi leaders are fielding several offers.

Zamir is reportedly alone among the security chiefs in opposing an immediate strike on Iran. Defence Minister Katz insists they "see eye to eye".

Police deployed snipers in Rahat amid record killings in Arab communities.

Context: Israel is in a caretaker interregnum in which the security establishment, not the prime minister, is acting as the main check on escalation.

Palestine (Gaza and the West Bank)

International.

49 states urged Israel to ease limits on winter supplies. The G7 reaffirmed UNSCR 2803 and said Hamas must decommission.

The UN aid chief rejected the idea that relief should wait for a new Israeli government.

Local.

Gaza: More than 46,000 households hit by floods; an infant died of hypothermia in Khan Younis; an Israeli strike on Nasser Hospital on 12 November killed six (per a civil defence official cited by AFP); respiratory infections are rising.

West Bank: Settlers attacked Atara after the army removed two outposts (five injured), assaulted Ya'bad olive harvesters and poisoned sheep in al-Auja. Israeli forces raided towns and arrested former prisoners (Haaretz; PIC, Hamas-aligned).

Context: Gaza is stuck between a roadmap that exists on paper and an occupation regime that controls the inputs. In the West Bank, the pattern is settler retaliation for even limited enforcement against outposts.

Lebanon.

International.

The G7 called for a "robust, credible and long-lasting" security architecture as UNIFIL's mandate expires at year's end, welcomed the EU support mission, and called for Hezbollah's disarmament.

Implementation of the June US-brokered Israel–Lebanon framework (phased IDF withdrawal linked to disarmament) has stalled.

Local.

The army seized a large arms depot in Beirut's southern suburbs and made an arrest. This follows October's interception of a half-ton explosives shipment and charges against 13 people.

Qassem rejects disarmament before withdrawal and calls the framework "null and void." Israeli strikes and demolitions continue in the south, for example a large detonation in Zawtar ash-Sharqiyah on 12 November.

Context: The Lebanese state is disarming Hezbollah slowly from the inside while Israel holds a self-declared buffer zone. UNIFIL's end is the next cliff edge, and parliamentary elections have already been pushed to May 2028.

Syria.

International.

Saudi Arabia signed its first defence pact with post-Assad Syria and 403 million dollars in loans during al-Sharaa's second Riyadh visit in under a month. Damascus denies it will send troops to Yemen.

Al-Sharaa discussed regional developments with Erdoğan by phone (SANA, state agency).

Local.

Aleppo protesters demanded justice for Assad-era abuses and criticised al-Sharaa's meeting with an alleged war criminal.

Fuel-price protests and a reshuffle of ministers and governors reflect pressure over economic hardship.

Context: Damascus is turning Gulf patronage into security guarantees while the domestic social contract (justice, prices, inclusion) wears thin.

Jordan.

International.

The king co-chaired an Aqaba Process round in Kazakhstan (5 to 6 November) and told parliament that "Israeli expansionism" is the region's greatest threat.

Local.

On 11 November the king chaired a meeting on energy infrastructure. Water security, with a 4 billion dollars plan to reduce dependence on Israeli supply as the water deal lapses, is now framed as a sovereignty issue.

The Karak church attack investigation continues: three suspects arrested.

Little else in the 48-hour window.

Context: Amman is protecting itself on three fronts: jihadist violence at home, West Bank annexation dynamics, and dependence on Israel for water.

Iraq (incl. Kurdistan Region)

International.

Tom Barrack pressed KDP and PUK leaders to reconcile. Bahrain reinstated its ambassador to Baghdad.

Iranian exemptions for Iraqi shipping in Hormuz, together with Baghdad's air-defence purchases, show Iraq balancing between Washington and Tehran.

Local.

Zaidi's three-hour meeting with PMF leaders and Ameri tries to defuse the clash with Kataib Hezbollah. He reportedly rejected weapons-for-posts deals.

KRI: The PUK has left the KRG cabinet. Arguments over who hit Khor Mor continue, and Iraqi officials point to Iran-backed militias.

Context: This is the most serious state attempt since 2014 to subordinate the factions. It is happening while Kurdistan fragments.

Iran.

International.

The IRGC claims a strike on the Bahrain radar and mine hits on two tankers; Baghaei warns that host states are "participants." Iran faces a widened US blockade and a land-route task force.

The IAEA is "increasingly concerned" about being refused access to Isfahan. France blames the IRGC for cyberattacks, and the G7 pledges continued pressure.

FM Araghchi called US attacks a violation of international law.

Local.

Political executions continue (Karaj, 8 November). Death sentences in Mashhad and pending cases in Isfahan follow from the December–January cost-of-living uprising.

Power has concentrated in the Supreme Leader's office (Mojtaba Khamenei) at the expense of parliament and President Pezeshkian, per opposition-leaning Iran International. Record inflation is the main domestic grievance.

Context: Tehran is surviving through repression at home and calibrated coercion at sea, betting that Washington's appetite and the Gulf's tolerance will run out first.

Turkey.

International.

Signed the mutual-defence pact with Qatar. Hosting COP31 in Antalya with intensive leader-level diplomacy (Middle East Eye; Daily Sabah, pro-government).

Courting both sides in Libya: Dbeibah and Khaled Haftar.

Local.

Eight defendants were freed in the İmamoğlu municipality trial.

The fund scandal is eroding AKP support, and an immunity debate targets CHP leader Özgür Özel.

Context: Abroad, Erdoğan has rarely been stronger as a security provider. At home, a corruption scandal and the opposition trial are testing the AKP's dominance.

Saudi Arabia.

International.

Persuaded Trump to pause strikes on Iran. Working the Beijing channel on the Houthis. Signed defence and loan deals with Syria.

The G7 condemned Houthi attacks on the kingdom.

Local.

Interceptions over Riyadh and the Mecca region, a pipeline fire in Medina province, and damage to the Aramco tower.

The economy is in recession (IMF: –3.3% in 2026). Etihad's suspension of flights to Riyadh and Dammam shows the business cost.

Context: Vision 2030's premise of a calm region has collapsed. Riyadh is now managing war on its southern border and an Iranian chokehold in the east.

United Arab Emirates.

International.

Hosted the Russia–Ukraine–US talks (10 to 11 November). MBZ received Arab League chief Nabil Fahmy.

Among the Gulf states Trump named as urging him not to strike Iran.

Local.

About 1,000 workers a day are clearing oil from the Gem No. 2 supertanker along the Umm Al Quwain, Sharjah and RAK coasts. Shipping sources say the tanker was hit by an unidentified projectile on 9 October; mangrove recovery could take months.

Gulf News's daily war briefing for residents and flight disruptions show the war's effect on everyday life.

Context: Abu Dhabi is positioning itself as a global convenor while absorbing the war's environmental and economic costs at home.

Qatar.

International.

Signed the defence pact with Turkey. PM Sheikh Mohammed met Trump and Vance on Iran (9 November). Iran exempts Qatari shipping in Hormuz.

Likud's unproven claim that Qatar funded Arab turnout in Israel's election has made Doha a target in Israeli domestic politics.

Local.

The Emir attended COP31. Qatar Airways resumed daily Riyadh flights. The Emir sent condolences to Kuwait's Emir.

Context: Doha is mediator, US base host and now Turkish treaty ally all at once, a maximal hedging strategy.

Kuwait.

International.

Will host the GCC Unified Military Command's new forward headquarters.

The PM led the delegation to COP31 on the Emir's behalf.

Local.

Cooperatives were ordered to secure food stocks, and 95 emergency shelters are being prepared. The pension-loan ceiling was raised to 40%.

Mourning for Sheikha Intisar Jassem Mohamed Al Malek Al Sabah.

Context: Kuwait is becoming the GCC's northern military anchor at the same time as it hollows out its parliamentary tradition.

Bahrain.

International.

The IRGC claimed a hit on a US radar on the island; CENTCOM says both drones were downed with no damage. Tehran's "participants" warning targets Manama directly.

Hosted the GCC Joint Defence Council (10 November). Reinstated its ambassador to Iraq.

Local.

A plan to raise the pension-eligibility age hit resistance in the Shura Council. The King praised the Mara'ee 2026 agricultural platform.

Context: As the Fifth Fleet's home, Bahrain is the Gulf state most exposed to Iran's attempt to punish basing.

Oman.

International.

Muscat is trying to revive US–Iran mediation. Sultan Haitham met Iran's chief negotiator, and his November state visit to Beijing ties Oman into China's mediation push.

Local.

No major domestic development in the 48-hour window. Iranian drones have hit Omani ports during the war despite its neutrality.

Context: Oman is the one Gulf producer whose hydrocarbon output hasn't fallen, and the region's quietest diplomatic channel.

Yemen.

International.

Saudi Arabia is lobbying China to restrain Iran and the Houthis. The G7 urged Iran to stop arming the group.

Local.

The month-old "Dawn of Yemen" offensive, the Taiz siege and the Houthi push in Lahj, about 80 km from Aden. More than 300,000 displaced.

Houthi-run Saba (partisan) reports dozens killed in Saudi strikes near Sanaa and a strike on the al-Mufazir gas facility.

Context: The 2022 truce is dead. Yemen has become the Iran war's second front and Saudi Arabia's own war again.
