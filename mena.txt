Here is your Middle East briefing for Friday, 20 November.

Riyadh's PIF tower hit as Washington weighs a new Iran campaign and Gaza's truce frays.

Four threads tightened at once in the 24 hours to Friday morning, and together they show a region where the Iran war is now setting the terms for almost everything else. First, Washington is openly weighing a return to large-scale strikes on Iran. Netanyahu, still caretaker prime minister and still short of a coalition, is lobbying for a 4-to-7-day joint air campaign built on contested chemical and biological weapons intelligence, while Tehran escalates its "boiling frog" pressure in the Strait of Hormuz. Second, a Houthi missile hit the Public Investment Fund tower in Riyadh's financial district. It was the second strike on a flagship Saudi corporate site this month, and it came as the Houthis close in on Aden after taking the heights over Bab al-Mandeb. Vision 2030's showcase is now inside the war zone. Third, the Trump–Xi–Putin summit in Shenzhen produced a declaration on oil supply and nuclear risk that never mentions Iran or Hormuz. That is a reminder that Beijing and Moscow will not do Washington's coercive work for it, and it is not necessarily the comfort Tehran wants. Fourth, the Gaza ceasefire is fraying. The Board of Peace warned the UN Security Council of imminent collapse, and Israel then made its first major ground push beyond the "Yellow Line" since October 2025. Elsewhere, Baghdad's face-saving deal with Kataib Hezbollah over Jurf al-Sakhar, Cairo's final IMF staff-level agreement and Dbeibah's nine deals in Cairo are the stories most likely to matter next month.

Top stories.

1. Washington weighs a new Iran offensive as Netanyahu pushes a 4-to-7-day campaign.

What happened. Several outlets report that President Trump is weighing a renewed offensive against Iran and that Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu is pressing the US to join a four-to-seven-day air campaign. Israel sent Mossad chief Roman Gofman and Military Intelligence chief Shlomi Binder to Washington. Gofman reportedly met CIA Director John Ratcliffe and presented intelligence that Iran is advancing a chemical weapons programme. Trump told Fox News there was an "indication that Iran is looking at gas and they're looking at … the biological weapons". According to the New York Times, as summarised in these reports, US intelligence has seen "evidence of stepped-up efforts" to bolster chemical and biological capacities, but the "prevailing view" is that Tehran would not use them first and is trying to deter large-scale attack. Axios reported that some US officials think the Israeli intelligence is "driven by an agenda". The possible targets listed are Iran's rebuilt missile and drone production, IRGC command facilities and, more ominously, energy infrastructure. Israel's Channel 12 reports that Trump has backed out of ready-to-launch strikes several times in recent weeks, and he told Axios he is not "rushing" and may wait until the new year. Iran's Foreign Ministry spokesman called the chemical-weapons allegations "fraudulent". In the Senate, Republicans again blocked a war-powers resolution on Iran.

Background. The war began with US-Israeli strikes on 28 February 2026. A Pakistan-mediated ceasefire held from April to early July, and fighting resumed in July as diplomacy stalled. Since then the conflict has settled into a pattern. A US naval blockade of Iranian ports is in force. Iran hits shipping in and around Hormuz and drones US facilities in the Gulf, and the US answers with "self-defence" strikes, most recently on an IRGC missile site on Qeshm Island on 17 November. The new supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, has not been seen or heard in public since March. He is believed to have been badly wounded in the strike that killed his father. President Pezeshkian said on 9 November that the leader had ordered an end to the "neither war nor peace" stalemate. Tehran has conveyed seven conditions for talks, which Secretary Rubio has called "outrageous". Qatar says its mediation has "gained momentum".

Analysis. The WMD framing should be read alongside Israeli domestic politics. Netanyahu's bloc won 53 seats in the 27 October election and he is now caretaker; a short, intense campaign alongside Washington would put security back at the centre of coalition bargaining (see Israel below). For Trump, the incentives point the other way. Reports say he has privately vented at Vice-President Vance for blaming the GOP's midterm losses on the war. Oil has slid about a fifth this month on a glut, and CSIS warned on 10 November that some US munitions stocks are critically short. The Iraq-2003 echo, in which allied intelligence on unconventional weapons is used to justify escalation, is not lost on parts of the US administration. Gulf states would carry the immediate risk: Tehran has repeatedly threatened to hit civilian infrastructure near US or Israeli bases if its own is targeted. For Europe, a new round would deepen the energy and refugee spillover just as the EU is re-engaging Syria.

What to watch. Whether Trump uses his Friday press availability on the way home from Asia to rule anything in or out; further leaks about the intelligence assessment; the US carrier posture; and whether Doha or Muscat produce a new channel before the 1 January marker Trump has floated.

2. Houthi missile hits Saudi wealth fund's tower in Riyadh as the Yemen war moves toward Aden.

What happened. On Thursday 19 November, a Houthi attack damaged the 80-storey PIF Tower in the King Abdullah Financial District (KAFD), headquarters of Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund. Saudi Civil Defense reported material damage to several floors of the tower and to neighbouring buildings, with no injuries. Reuters' sources described a direct missile hit. The coalition said "debris" had fallen, so it is not yet settled whether the damage came from a direct hit or from an interception. The Saudi Foreign Ministry condemned the attack and urged the permanent members of the UN Security Council to act on the relevant resolutions. Saudi aircraft struck Sanaa early on Thursday, according to the Houthi-run Saba agency, and the coalition said it had destroyed the ballistic-missile launch platform in Dhamar used against Riyadh. Saudi Gazette reports the coalition intercepted five Houthi missiles aimed at the kingdom. This was the second attack on a major Saudi business complex this month, after an 8 November strike damaged an Aramco building.

Background. The Saudi–Houthi truce that held from 2022 has collapsed into a two-front war. On 15 November, government forces gave up the high ground over Bab al-Mandeb and redeployed to defend Aden after a coastal supply line was cut. The Houthis now hold almost all of Yemen's Bab al-Mandeb shoreline and are pressing on two approaches to the temporary capital. The coalition said it struck 60 Houthi targets in Sanaa, Hodeidah, Amran and Saada on 17 November, and the Houthis say 17 people were killed. Yemen's parliament has voted to outlaw the movement; the law now awaits signature by Presidential Leadership Council chairman Rashad al-Alimi.

Analysis. Physically, the PIF Tower is a building. Politically, it is the symbol of the post-oil economy Mohammed bin Salman is selling to investors. Hitting it, after Aramco, tells capital markets that Riyadh's financial district is within range at a moment when the PIF is already under strain. Returns have reportedly fallen to around 5.8% and the fund is exploring securitisation and stake sales (House of Saud, an outlet of uncertain standing). For the Houthis, the pressure on Riyadh supports a ground campaign aimed at Aden and at control of both Red Sea chokepoints, which would hand the "axis" a lever over global shipping just as Iran squeezes Hormuz. Riyadh's dilemma is the old one: escalation in Yemen ties it to an open-ended war, while restraint invites more strikes. It has also been left out of the Qatar–Oman channel to Tehran, which limits its diplomatic options.

What to watch. Whether the Houthis claim the strike; Saudi retaliation beyond air raids; whether the Aden front holds (The National judged an imminent assault unlikely as of 18 November); and the insurance and investor response in Riyadh.

3. Hormuz: more tanker attacks, a missing Jordanian sailor, and oil that refuses to spike.

What happened. Iran's navy said through state media that two oil tankers hit naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz on 19 November and "exploded". No maritime authority has confirmed this, and CTP-ISW has long assessed such mine claims as partly an information operation. The attack that can be verified came on the evening of 17 November. A vessel operated by ADNOC Logistics and Services was struck by "an unknown projectile" about 13 nautical miles north of Khasab in Oman's Musandam, injuring 11 crew. Vanguard Tech identified the ship as the Liberian-flagged product tanker Navig8 Messi. A Jordanian third navigation officer, Mohammad Hossam Al Din Al Otaibi, is still missing. Jordan's embassies in Abu Dhabi and Muscat are coordinating a search with drones, and his father told Roya that crew had jumped into the sea. ADNOC has reportedly had at least 20 vessels hit since the war began. Brent settled up 3 cents at 91.98 dollarson 19 November, while WTI fell to 83.21 dollars, its lowest since 26 August. Brent is down about a fifth this month because the IEA says supply is outpacing demand for the first time since the war began.

Background. Commercial traffic through Hormuz has been crippled since early March. IMF PortWatch logged 2 transits on 15 November against a pre-crisis baseline of about 85 a day. UANI, an advocacy group, counts about 27.7 US-escorted or US-facilitated transits a day and 138 IMO-confirmed incidents. The US blockade has been widened to cover ship-to-ship transfers of Iranian cargo, and CENTCOM set up a task force on 7 November to interdict weapons coming in over the Caspian and land routes. TankerTrackers says Iran appears to be preparing to export oil again despite the blockade. Iran also attacked Bahrain on 15 and 18 November, firing drones at a US radar at the Riffa/Sheikh Isa base, and Washington answered on Qeshm.

Analysis. Bloomberg's framing, "Iran intensifies Hormuz tanker attacks just as oil prices slide", captures Tehran's problem. With OPEC+ output rising, Russian exports resilient and Gulf producers rerouting, the oil weapon is losing force. Iran is therefore raising tempo, and casualties, to keep insurers and shippers deterred. The targeting of ADNOC is pointed. The UAE is now India's fastest-growing supplier, and India bought more Gulf crude than Russian crude in November for the first time since the war began, reportedly 2.1 million b/d against 1.8 million b/d. Jordan, with a citizen missing, has a new domestic reason to press for de-escalation. The Arab League condemned the attack.

What to watch. Independent verification of the Iranian mine claims; whether Iran actually tries to run the blockade with loaded tankers (a direct US–Iran naval test); and the fate of Al Otaibi.

4. Shenzhen trilateral: Trump, Xi and Putin agree on oil and nuclear risk but not on Iran.

What happened. Presidents Trump, Xi and Putin held their first trilateral summit on 19 November in Shenzhen, on the sidelines of APEC. They issued a four-point declaration on "strategic stability and international security" covering UN reform, oil supply, the International Criminal Court and reducing nuclear risk. The text mentions neither Iran nor the Strait of Hormuz. Trump says Xi agreed in their bilateral that Hormuz must stay open and that Iran must not get nuclear weapons. The Chinese readout did not mention Iran, and AFP reported no sign that Trump secured help from either leader on ending the war. Foreign Minister Araghchi, speaking in Oman, condemned "foreign interference" in the region without naming the summit (Breitbart, a partisan outlet).

Background. Beijing and Moscow have shielded Iran diplomatically throughout the war. At the IAEA Board this month, Iran, Russia and China issued a joint statement making full inspections conditional on an end to military strikes and guarantees against repetition. Chatham House argued on 2 November that Tehran sees closer ties with Moscow as key to its short-term survival.

Analysis. The summit shows the limits of Iran's great-power partnerships in both directions. China and Russia would not sign anything naming Iran, so Washington has no multilateral cover for escalation. They did sign a joint commitment to boost oil supply, which works against Tehran's main lever. For Gulf producers that is good news; for Iran it is a quiet signal that its "partners" put market stability ahead of its war aims. The ICC clause will be read in Israel and Palestine as hostile to the Court's current investigations. Europeans, absent from the room, will note a great-power directorate forming over issues where they have large stakes.

What to watch. Whether Beijing follows through by easing pressure on Iranian crude buyers or by pushing Tehran on shipping, and the Iranian domestic debate over "Look East".

5. Gaza's ceasefire under strain: Board of Peace alarm, a ground push past the Yellow Line, and a flooded winter.

What happened. In what AP called its first annual assessment, the Board of Peace told the UN Security Council on 17 November that the October 2025 ceasefire faces "imminent collapse" without immediate action. It blamed both sides: Israel for continued strikes and for blocking the technocratic Palestinian administration and the International Stabilization Force (ISF), and Hamas for military activity and failure to disarm. The next day the IDF launched what was widely described as its first major ground push west of the "Yellow Line" since the ceasefire. Tanks moved east of Nuseirat camp to destroy a tunnel the army said posed "an immediate and direct threat". The army said on 19 November that the operation was over and reopened Salah al-Din road near Al-Bureij and Nuseirat. Gaza's Government Media Office says about 6,000 people fled on the first day, and Civil Defence puts the total near 10,000 (Hamas-run bodies, figures unverified). The Health Ministry reported six killed in the 24 hours to 18 November and further deaths in eastern Gaza City on 19 November. The post-ceasefire toll stands at at least 1,658.

Background. Phase two of the Trump plan has never really started. The Board of Peace holds about 18 dollarsbillion in pledges, but reconstruction has not begun, and nearly 1.4 million people remain in makeshift shelters. Storms since 6 November have affected more than 46,000 households and damaged more than 40,000 family shelters. More than 150,000 tents bought by aid partners sit outside Gaza awaiting Israeli approval. UN relief chief Tom Fletcher has said "Now we're told to wait for the formation of a government… this is not an acceptable excuse". Al Jazeera reports that more than 85% of Gaza Civil Defence vehicles are out of service.

Analysis. The sequencing deadlock is structural. Israel will not withdraw or admit the ISF and the technocratic body until Hamas disarms. Hamas ties disarmament to those deployments and to withdrawal. Without the ISF, troop-contributing states will not commit. Israel's caretaker government has, in effect, given itself a reason to freeze decisions until a coalition forms, a delay Fletcher openly rejects. The Nuseirat operation tests whether the "Yellow Line" is a ceasefire line or simply a forward position. The Board of Peace is a Trump creation, so its warning that both sides are failing is also a signal of Washington's limited bandwidth while it is consumed by Iran.

What to watch. UNSC follow-up; whether shelter materials are released before the next storms; any Israeli coalition outcome that unlocks ISF entry; and further incursions past the line.

6. Iraq: Baghdad's compromise with Kataib Hezbollah over Jurf al-Sakhar.

What happened. On 19 November, Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi announced a compromise with Kataib Hezbollah (KH). A joint operations centre of army, federal police, PMF and energy-police units was to open in Jurf al-Sakhar at 06:00 local time on Friday 20 November. A senior official had said KH had until Friday to leave. Accounts diverge. Iran's state-run Press TV says KH fighters will relocate, while The Cradle reports that KH's official position is that it will not leave, and The National's podcast says the group remains in control. The terms have not been published.

Background. Jurf al-Sakhar (officially renamed Jurf al-Nasr), south of Baghdad, has for years been a no-go zone run by KH, as Al Jazeera's explainer details. Officials have linked it to weapons storage and, according to a government committee, oil smuggling. Al-Zaidi's plan to bring all weapons under state control has slipped from 30 September 2026 to June 2027, and Trump has reportedly warned of financial penalties or direct strikes if disarmament stalls.

Analysis. This is classic Iraqi state-building by co-optation. Bringing the PMF into the "joint" force lets Baghdad claim sovereignty without a firefight it might lose, and lets KH say it handed nothing to the state. The audience is Washington. Al-Zaidi needs to show progress to head off US punitive measures while Iran's "axis" is under maximum pressure, and Tehran needs to avoid opening an Iraqi front. If the centre actually opens and KH thins out, it will be his first real win. If KH stays in place, Washington will read the deal as cosmetic, and the risk of US or Israeli strikes on Iraqi soil rises. Eurasia Review captures the scepticism among experts.

What to watch. Friday's deployment on the ground; KH statements; and whether the formula is applied to other faction-held zones.
