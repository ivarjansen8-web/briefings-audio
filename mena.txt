Here is your Middle East briefing for Wednesday, 11 November.

Eisenkot's Knesset opens in fury as Washington eyes an Iran exit and Houthi missiles reach Riyadh's financial heart.

Five threads mattered most in the 24 hours to Wednesday morning. First, Israel's 26th Knesset was sworn in with a majority that does not belong to Benjamin Netanyahu: Gadi Eisenkot holds the mandate with 67 recommendations, and Netanyahu used the ceremonial opening to accuse Arab-led parties of "stealing" the election, which led Eisenkot's Yashar to cancel talks with Likud. Second, Washington is signalling that it is stepping down from the Iran war. After meeting Qatar's prime minister, Trump said further military action is not needed "for now", while Rubio cautioned that no breakthrough is near and Tehran denied any planned Vance–Rezaei meeting. Fifth, Gaza's first winter storm flooded about 70,000 tents and killed at least five people, while Egypt made two significant moves: a permanent terrorist designation of the Muslim Brotherhood and reopening its embassy in Tripoli.

1. Israel: a new Knesset, an Eisenkot mandate and a Netanyahu speech that hardened the lines.

What happened. On Tuesday 10 November the 120 members of the 26th Knesset were sworn in while some 200 right-wing protesters demonstrated outside against the government Eisenkot is negotiating. The ceremony was marked by heckling and taunts. Netanyahu's speech is traditionally a toast to the new parliament. He used it to demand a "unity government", to claim that Arab members of the Knesset want Israel's destruction, and to repeat that Arab-led parties had stolen the election. Eisenkot called the speech "delusional, divisive, full of hate and shameful", and Yashar cancelled a meeting with Likud scheduled for Wednesday.

How we got here. On 8 November President Herzog gave Eisenkot the mandate after he received 67 recommendations to Netanyahu's 46. Likud remains the largest party with 29 seats but did not get the first attempt at forming a government. Eisenkot has 28 days. He has said repeatedly that he will not sit with Netanyahu, although Yashar has said Likud could join "without extremists". Shas (6 seats) recommended Netanyahu, and Zehut recommended him while also questioning the legitimacy of the result and calling for another vote. Al Jazeera reports that Herzog warned against a repeat election.

The procedural fight. The anti-Netanyahu bloc's first move is to take the Speaker's chair from Likud's Amir Ohana. Under a 2020 precedent, a demand signed by at least 61 members forces a vote. Ohana says he will convene one within a week of receiving the signatures. Reporting differs on who would replace him. One account has Yashar filing a majority-signed motion for its own MK Matan Kahana; another says Liberman and Bennett preferred Yair Lapid. The bloc is also preparing a "legislative blitz": six to eight special committees to repeal judicial-overhaul laws and set up a state inquiry into 7 October without needing a government vote.

Analysis. This is the most consequential shift in Israeli politics since 2021, and its foreign-policy implications are real but easy to overstate. Eisenkot is a former chief of staff. He favours security consensus over ideological maximalism, and his priorities are domestic and institutional: the judiciary and an October 7 inquiry. On Gaza he is likely to be more receptive to the US-led "Board of Peace" architecture and Arab participation. On Iran, Carnegie argues that renewed Israel–Iran confrontation remains possible whoever governs. The government's arithmetic is the vulnerability. A majority that includes Arab-led parties is precisely what Netanyahu is attacking as illegitimate, and his speech was a strategy to peel off Haredi and soft-right partners by making the coalition look toxic. Shas's position is pivotal. The Haredi parties' interests (draft exemptions, budgets) fit awkwardly with Yashar's agenda, and Likud is trying to keep them out of Eisenkot's camp. Abroad, Arab capitals and Europe will read an Eisenkot government as an opportunity on Gaza reconstruction and the West Bank. Washington's reported pressure on Netanyahu to advance the Gaza plan after the election suggests the Trump administration is not invested in his survival.

What to watch. The Speaker vote, which tests whether 61 signatures can be produced and held. Whether Shas or United Torah Judaism engage with Eisenkot. Any Likud move to legally challenge Arab-party participation. The street mood after the Lod "sovereignty march" brawl (see below).

2. US–Iran: Washington says it is done militarily, Tehran says there are no talks, and Hormuz stays shut.

What happened. On Monday 9 November Trump met Qatar's prime minister and foreign minister, Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, at the White House for about 45 minutes. Afterwards he said he did not believe further military action against Iran was necessary for now and that the war could end "fairly soon". He also suggested Qatar and other Gulf states would prefer the "status quo", including the US naval blockade. Rubio said he "wouldn't say we're on the verge of a breakthrough". Qatar is circulating a framework document that both sides are amending. On 10 November Trump described the war as militarily over while keeping the option of another intervention, and Hegseth said US forces remain "locked and loaded". In Tehran, security chief Mohsen Rezaei denied that talks are taking place and said the US must first meet Iran's "seven conditions". The foreign ministry called reports of a planned Vance–Rezaei meeting in Doha "baseless".

Military picture. The pause is not a ceasefire. US forces struck air defences on Qeshm Island on 2 and 6 November. Trump called these "minor strikes", while the IRGC claimed to have downed US drones. Iranian state media reported further US strikes on Qeshm overnight on 8 to 9 November. ADNOC said one of its vessels was damaged in an attack in UAE waters on 10 November. Iran unveiled what it called a new underground "missile city", and an IRGC official warned of retaliation "beyond the region" if Iran is attacked again. This detail came from summaries only. The Wall Street Journal reportedly said the US moved aircraft out of RAF Mildenhall after an Iranian threat; this is single-sourced and unconfirmed here.

Energy. Brent rose about 1.1% to roughly 92.5 dollarsearly on 11 November, up from a two-month low in the previous session, as attacks on Hormuz shipping offset hopes of a truce. In October, 37 ships were attacked in and around the strait, the highest monthly total of the war, and 10 had been attacked so far in November. Tracker data say the strait is effectively closed to commercial traffic: one transit on 1 November against a pre-war baseline of about 85 a day.

Background. The war began on 28 February. A Pakistan-mediated ceasefire and a June memorandum of understanding halted large-scale fighting, but the ceasefire collapsed on 8 July after Iranian attacks on commercial shipping. Since then a US naval blockade of Iranian ports and Iran's closure of Hormuz have produced a costly stalemate. The domestic US turning point was the 3 November midterms. Vance wrote that "the reason we lost is the war". Trump conceded that "a part of it was Iran" but said he would do it again.

Analysis. Washington now has a political incentive to end the war that it did not have a month ago, and Tehran knows it. That explains Iran's maximalist public posture: denying talks, insisting on seven conditions, showing off missile infrastructure, and parliament readying NPT-exit bills (see Iran below). Both sides need a text each can sell as victory, a point Iran International's analysts make. Qatar's role as broker gives it standing in Washington at a time when Gulf neighbours are under fire. Trump's remark that the Gulf prefers the "status quo" with the blockade is revealing: Riyadh and Abu Dhabi may be wary of a deal that frees Iran from the blockade without constraints on its missiles and proxies. The losers in a drawn-out stalemate are the Gulf exporters without bypass routes (Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, Iraq) and Asian importers. China has moved into this space (see Diplomacy).

What to watch. Whether a Vance–Iranian meeting materialises despite the denials. Any US response to the ADNOC incident. Oil reaction to the next attack on Hormuz shipping. Movement in Iran's Majlis on NPT legislation.

3. The Houthi–Saudi war reaches Riyadh's financial district and the Hormuz bypass.

What happened. On Sunday 8 November a Houthi missile attack damaged the Aramco Tower in the King Abdullah Financial District in Riyadh. The Energy Ministry called it an "attempted attack" in which debris caused limited damage and no casualties. Images showed windows blown out across several floors. The Houthis' listed targets included the financial district, Riyadh airport, Aramco storage and Abqaiq. Coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki said two ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh were intercepted. On Monday 9 November the East–West pipeline, which carries crude to the Red Sea and around Hormuz, was attacked and caught fire in a limited way. Operations were unaffected. The Houthis denied attacking it. Early on Tuesday 10 November explosions were heard in Riyadh as another ballistic missile was intercepted.

Saudi and coalition response. Houthi authorities say a strike on al-Shariya village east of Sanaa killed 33 people and wounded 39; another report gives 44 dead. The Houthis blame Saudi airstrikes. Yemen's government says a Houthi ballistic missile hit the site. Riyadh has not commented. On 10 November coalition aircraft reportedly hit a gas facility and a Yemen Petroleum Company building in Sanaa. On the ground, government forces claim advances in a counteroffensive to retake territory lost in September as Houthi cross-border attacks resume.

Background. The 2022 UN-brokered truce unravelled in July, in step with the collapse of the US–Iran ceasefire. Saudi Arabia has since faced near-daily Houthi strikes, including on Riyadh, Khamis Mushait and a power station in Madinah. In October the GCC Ministerial Council held Iran responsible for strikes on the Gulf.

Analysis. Two points matter for the Iran track. First, the East–West pipeline is Saudi Arabia's insurance policy against a closed Hormuz. Attacks on it, even ineffective ones, raise the cost of Riyadh's "status quo" and put pressure on the kingdom to favour a quick US–Iran settlement. Second, a strike on the symbolic heart of Vision 2030, the financial district in Riyadh, threatens the investor-confidence model on which Saudi diversification rests. Egypt's foreign minister urged Iran's foreign minister to halt attacks on Saudi Arabia. That framing treats the Houthis as Iran's responsibility, consistent with the GCC line. The Yemeni government's counteroffensive shows Riyadh using the moment to reshape the ground war, which makes a return to the 2022 truce harder.

What to watch. Whether Aramco or the Energy Ministry revise the damage assessment. Any Saudi strikes on Houthi leadership. Whether Washington folds the Houthi file into its framework talks with Iran.

4. France attributes supermarket sabotage to the IRGC: the war's European front.

What happened. Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot said: "I can confirm that this attack was carried out by the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps." He was referring to the cyberattack of 11 to 12 October that drove refrigeration temperatures to about 50 °C at around 700 food retail sites. France summoned Iran's ambassador "to lodge a very strong protest against this extremely grave act" and will seek further EU sanctions. Paris prosecutors are investigating an IRGC cyber unit, Shahid Kaveh, and are seeking international arrest warrants for its alleged leader and two deputies. Similar incidents hit supermarkets in Sweden, Belgium, Luxembourg, Ireland and Estonia, and France's blood bank was targeted in early November. Paris has not attributed those incidents publicly. Iran rejected the claims as baseless. Separately, Canada reimposed sanctions on 11 Iranian entities linked to the nuclear and missile programmes.

Analysis. This is the first public European attribution of a destructive IRGC cyber operation against civilian critical infrastructure during the war, and the target was chosen for its everyday reach. The EU already listed the IRGC as a terrorist organisation in January and created a freedom-of-navigation sanctions regime in June. Paris will now push for a cyber-sanctions package. For Tehran the operation may serve as horizontal escalation, imposing costs on European publics as the EU tightens the noose. It also undercuts the Europeans who argue for a diplomatic opening. Watch for any coordinated attribution from Sweden, Belgium or Ireland.

5. Gaza's first winter storm and the "ceasefire" that keeps killing.

What happened. Rain flooded about 70,000 tents within 12 hours, according to Gaza's government media office. Civil defence received at least 1,500 emergency calls and warned that more than 2,000 buildings are at risk of collapse. A sand embankment collapsed onto tents at the Ard El-Rebat camp south of Deir al-Balah, killing at least five people, three of them children. Iran's state-aligned Tehran Times put the toll at four, with two missing. "Civil defence does not have a single tarpaulin or a single tent," said its director Raed al-Dahshan. Save the Children said Israel had not allowed enough tents in. Al Jazeera reports that six Palestinians were killed in Israeli attacks in the latest 24-hour reporting cycle and that nearly 1,600 have been killed since the October 2025 ceasefire.

Diplomacy. The Board of Peace's high representative, Nickolay Mladenov, travelled to Israel to seek permission to bring in tents and speed up reconstruction. The UK is reportedly rallying countries behind a statement urging Israel to loosen restrictions before winter. Mladenov has said the Board holds 7 dollarsbillion in reconstruction pledges but that "reconstruction financing will not follow where weapons have not been laid down", and that two more countries are likely to join the International Stabilisation Force (ISF).

Analysis. The shelter dispute is political. Israel treats tents and caravans as "reconstruction", which is barred until Hamas disarms, while Mladenov has argued that they are not. A second winter in tents is the predictable result of making humanitarian shelter conditional on a security process that has stalled. Government formation in Israel could break this logjam. An Eisenkot government would face less coalition pressure from the far right on Gaza inputs, though the military establishment's position on dual-use items would still bind it.

6. Egypt hardens at home and pivots in Libya.

What happened. On Monday 9 November the Official Gazette published a decision to permanently designate the Muslim Brotherhood as a terrorist entity. This ends the series of time-limited, five-year listings and automatically triggers a ban, asset freezes and travel bans. Three individuals were also added to the list. On 7 November Egypt reopened its embassy in Tripoli, closed since 2014. Foreign Minister Badr Abdelatty met Prime Minister Abdul Hamid Dbeibah and raised Cairo's objections to the 2019 Turkey–Libya maritime memorandum.

Analysis. The permanent designation removes a legal lever that could otherwise have featured in any future reconciliation. That suggests the state does not foresee one, even as economic pressure grows. The Tripoli move is more strategic. Analysts read it as a message to the Haftar family, Cairo's long-time client in the east, whose sons are courting France and Niger. It is also an attempt to influence Tripoli's maritime alignment with Ankara. Egypt is hedging across Libya's divide at the moment when US envoy Massad Boulos is pressing for institutional unification.

Iran.

International.

Tehran denies any talks or a Vance–Rezaei meeting and demands its "seven conditions". Canada has reimposed sanctions, and France has attributed the supermarket cyberattack to the IRGC.

Bagheri Kani asked Beijing for a larger role, and a Chinese envoy will visit Tehran.

Local.

NPT exit: the secretary of the Majlis national security commission says withdrawal bills are "ready" pending a "comprehensive will". Ten proposals are registered, and Paydari (Jalili) demanded withdrawal on 24 October. Araghchi reaffirmed NPT membership on 1 November.

Three men were executed in Karaj over the January protests.

A new "missile city" has been unveiled.

Context: The regime is projecting resilience: missiles, NPT brinkmanship and executions. Its read is that US domestic politics now favour Iran's bargaining position.

Turkey.

International.

Egypt is challenging the Turkey–Libya maritime memorandum.

Turkey is keeping troops in Iraq and Syria, according to the defence minister on 20 October. Kuwait and Turkey have pledged a deeper strategic partnership.

Local.

The İmamoğlu trial reached its 107th hearing on 10 November.

The PKK process is stalled: Öcalan has been moved to a new section of İmralı. DEM's Sancar says the process has been paused for about two months, and the PKK's Duran Kalkan accuses Ankara of delay.

October inflation was 29.77%. Karahan's inflation report is due on 12 November.

Context: Erdoğan is running two tracks at once, coercion of the opposition and a stalled Kurdish peace process. Both serve constitutional ambitions, and both are vulnerable to economic disappointment.

Saudi Arabia.

International.

Arab, GCC and Muslim World League condemnation of the Houthi strikes.

China's Middle East envoy is due in Riyadh as part of a shuttle tour.

Local.

The Aramco Tower was damaged and the East–West pipeline hit, and a further missile was intercepted over Riyadh on 10 November.

GCC rail and Riyadh–Doha high-speed rail agreements were signed.

Context: The kingdom is fighting a renewed Yemen war while selling a Vision 2030 business climate. The pipeline attacks expose the fragility of its Hormuz workaround.

United Arab Emirates.

International.

Mladenov used the Hili Forum in Abu Dhabi to preview ISF expansion.

Context: Abu Dhabi has engineered the most war-resilient energy posture in the Gulf, but its shipping remains a target.

Kuwait.

International.

Kuwait and Turkey pledged a deeper strategic partnership.

Kuwait launched its first locally made light ammunition.
