Here is your Middle East briefing for Sunday, 15 November.

Drones over Bahrain, missiles over Jazan: the 'no war, no peace' order frays.

Four things mattered most over the past 24 to 48 hours. First, the US–Iran "no war, no peace" equilibrium came under strain. On Sunday the IRGC claimed a six-drone strike on a US air-defence radar at a Bahraini helicopter base, and Manama issued its first nationwide threat alerts since September. This came days after Trump told advisers he might hold off on new strikes "barring provocations", so it directly tests the president's stated red line. Second, the Houthi–Saudi war is now a front in its own right. On 13 November the Houthis claimed strikes on Riyadh's King Khalid airport, on the East-West Pipeline that carries Saudi Arabia's Hormuz bypass, and on troops in Jazan. Third, Gulf security is being rewired around and beyond Washington: Turkey and Qatar signed a mutual defence pact on top of August's Saudi–Turkey–Pakistan "Mecca agreement", while Syria sends troops to Saudi Arabia. Fourth, two states moved against armed actors they had long tolerated. Iraq's prime minister sacked nine officials over oil smuggling in Kataib Hezbollah's Jurf al-Sakhar stronghold, and Lebanon's army raided a weapons depot in Beirut's southern suburbs. In Israel, Netanyahu's appeal for a "unity government" opens the endgame of coalition-building under Gadi Eisenkot's mandate. In Gaza, Israel killed Hamas's Rafah brigade commander while winter floods affect more than 220,000 people.

Top stories.

1. Iran claims a drone strike on a US base in Bahrain, testing Trump's "no provocations" pause.

What happened. On Sunday 15 November, Bahrain's interior ministry issued two alerts of potential threats. They were its first since September, and it told residents to stay calm and move to the nearest safe place. The IRGC said it had launched six drones at a US air-defence radar at the Al-Riffa helicopter base. It called the attack a "second round" of retaliation for "hostile US actions" and said the Strait of Hormuz "remained closed" (Middle East Eye live blog; Ynet; Russian state outlet Sputnik). Sabereen News, an Iran-aligned channel, reported explosions across the kingdom. There was no immediate US or Bahraini confirmation of any damage. Earlier, on Friday, the US military said it had intercepted two Iranian attack drones heading for Bahrain and rejected Tehran's claim that an American early-warning radar site had been destroyed.

At sea, Iran's navy and the IRGC Navy said on 13 November that oil tankers struck mines and caught fire while trying to cross the strait "without coordination with Tehran". Counts differ between two and four vessels, and none has been publicly identified. The IMO now counts at least 25 seafarers killed around Hormuz since the war began on 28 February. The latest victim was from the tanker Velos Sapphire, hit on 5 November (Marine Insight; Russian state agency TASS).

Background. Three facts set the context. The war began with US–Israeli strikes on 28 February that killed Ali Khamenei; his son Mojtaba was named supreme leader on 9 March and has not appeared in public since. The June Islamabad memorandum collapsed in July, and Washington reinstated its naval blockade of Iranian ports on 14 July. And Iran's new Persian Gulf Strait Authority has been issuing transit permits, in some cases for fees, which Argus notes is hard to square with UNCLOS. On 9 November, after meeting Qatar's prime minister, Trump called the war "effectively over" militarily and said renewed strikes were "quite unlikely", while keeping the blockade: "We're just keeping it the status quo". Reuters-sourced reporting since then says he told aides he may hold off as long as there are no new provocations from Tehran or the Houthis. Rubio argues that economic pain makes major strikes unnecessary.

Analysis. Sunday's claim matters less for any physical damage than for what it signals. Tehran is probing how far "provocation" stretches. It chose a target that is a US asset on Gulf Arab soil rather than a Gulf state's own infrastructure. That calibration lets it claim retaliation while avoiding a direct break with Riyadh or Doha, which are both mediating. The IRGC's "second round" wording implies it is answering something Washington did; CENTCOM has not said what. For Washington, the policy rests on the bet that the blockade does the work. US Treasury Secretary Bessent forecasts a contraction of more than 25% in Iran's economy in the 12 months to March 2027, and the rial hit roughly 2.2 million to the dollar in September. That bet also lets Trump bank a "the war is over" narrative after the 3 November midterms. Iranian leaders reportedly read the midterm results as proof that pressure on the US administration costs it politically. That reading gives the IRGC a reason to keep low-cost attacks going. Israel is the loser if the pause holds. Axios has reported repeated lobbying by Mossad chief Roman Gofman for renewed strikes. And the IAEA still cannot say where Iran's 440.9 kg of 60% uranium is.

What to watch. Watch whether CENTCOM characterises Sunday's incident as a "provocation". Watch Iran's response to Qatar's 48-hour, two-stage plan, which puts IAEA access to bombed sites on day one; Tehran's own sequencing has the blockade lifted first. And watch the 19 November Trump–Xi–Putin meeting in Shenzhen, where Iran is on the agenda.

2. The Houthis take the war to Riyadh's airport and Saudi Arabia's Hormuz bypass.

What happened. On Friday 13 November, Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said one drone hit King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh and two drones hit Pumping Station No. 3 on the East-West Pipeline east of the capital. He said both operations "achieved their objectives" but gave no evidence (House of Saud; Chinese state agency Xinhua; Middle East Eye). The group also claimed two ballistic missiles on "gatherings of Saudi forces" in Al-Tuwal, Jazan province, hours after Saudi civil defence had issued an all-clear. It said dozens were killed or wounded, including Sudanese nationals. It further claimed a strike on weapons depots at the Ras Abbas camp in Aden. Riyadh has not confirmed any of these claims. The coalition said on Saturday it intercepted a Houthi missile fired towards Riyadh province, and Iran's Mehr agency reported Saudi strikes on Sanaa the same day; neither report has been independently confirmed. Inside Yemen, the Giants Brigades said they repelled a Houthi attack in Lahj, and the army said two Houthi ballistic missiles were fired at Aden. Anadolu, a Turkish state agency, puts the number displaced since July's escalation at more than 340,000.

Background. The Houthis entered the regional war late, and by September they had taken the Bab el-Mandeb coastline from the Saudi-backed government. On 2 November Reuters reported them within 80 km of Aden, their closest since 2015. The government's "Dawn of Yemen" counter-offensive claims gains around Dhubab, western Taiz and al-Jawf. The Sanaa strikes that the Houthis say killed 11, later 27, people on 7 November marked an escalation of Saudi air operations.

Analysis. The pipeline claim is the strategic one. Riyadh has rerouted about 4 million b/d through the East-West line to Yanbu to avoid Hormuz. A Houthi campaign against it threatens the kingdom's main hedge against Iranian control of the strait, so Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb have become one theatre. That also explains Riyadh's turn to unconventional manpower. Syria has confirmed sending military advisers, with 600 special forces due, while denying any deployment inside Yemen. The claimed Sudanese casualties in Jazan point to the continued use of Sudanese manpower as well. Trump's hold-off condition explicitly names the Houthis, so the Houthis may now be the likeliest trigger for renewed US action, and that gives Tehran a deniable lever over Washington's timetable. The Houthis have repeatedly made claims that Riyadh never confirms. Until there is imagery or a Saudi statement, read the pipeline strike as an intention, not an established fact.

What to watch. Watch for any Aramco or Saudi energy ministry statement on Pumping Station 3, the Aden perimeter in Lahj's Al-Madaribah hills, and the arrival of Syrian special forces.

3. Israel's coalition endgame: Netanyahu offers "unity", Eisenkot calls it "another spin"

What happened. On Saturday 14 November Netanyahu urged Gadi Eisenkot to start unity-government talks "without preconditions". He argued that a narrow government relying on Arab parties could not handle the Iranian nuclear threat, and he called for "national reconciliation" after some lawmakers walked out during the anthem. Eisenkot dismissed this as "another spin" and noted that Likud–Yashar talks were already scheduled for the next day. He said anyone joining a government he leads must accept his principles and cut ties with extremists. Channel 13 reported that Eisenkot planned to offer Likud a place in a government under his leadership with no rotation, possibly with a diplomatic role for Netanyahu. Only one outlet carried this. Smotrich backed Netanyahu's call.

Background. The 27 October election left no bloc with a majority: Netanyahu's camp holds 53 seats and Eisenkot's 48. The Joint List and Ra'am together hold 19 seats. Herzog gave Eisenkot the mandate on 8 November after 67 MKs recommended him against 46 for Netanyahu. Herzog cited "mutual vetoes" within and between the blocs, and the 28-day clock can be extended by 14 days.

Analysis. Netanyahu is not really proposing a government he would join as a junior partner. He is trying to delegitimise the only arithmetic that gets Eisenkot to 61: a minority government with outside support from Arab parties. Likud's Miri Regev already told Herzog there should be no government relying on them. By framing the choice as "Iran or the Arab parties", Netanyahu aims to peel off soft members of the anti-Netanyahu camp, or to run out the clock towards another election early next year. Eisenkot's no-rotation counter would turn Likud into a junior partner and put the question of Netanyahu's own role, given his trial, at the centre. For foreign policy, the stakes are large. An Eisenkot-led government would likely be more open to US sequencing on Gaza's "Board of Peace" phase two and to coordinating with Washington's Iran pause. A Netanyahu-dominated arrangement keeps strategic continuity on Iran, Lebanon and Syria.

What to watch. Watch the outcome of the Likud–Yashar meeting, any Ra'am or Joint List statement on outside support, and the 6 December mandate deadline, which falls 28 days after 8 November.

4. Turkey and Qatar sign a mutual defence pact as Gulf security moves beyond Washington.

What happened. Erdoğan and Emir Tamim signed a mutual defence agreement in Istanbul on 13 November at the 12th meeting of the two countries' Supreme Strategic Committee. They pledged a "unified stance" against any external armed attack on either party. The communiqué commits them to consultation on threats, more joint exercises, defence-industrial cooperation and training. It does not say what triggers the clause or what form a joint response would take. The two sides also accused Israel of violating the Gaza peace plan. As of 14 November no State Department, CENTCOM or White House reaction had been identified.

Background. The pact formalises a strategic relationship institutionalised by the December 2014 agreement that created the Supreme Strategic Committee. It follows August's Mecca agreement between Turkey, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, under which an attack on one is an attack on all; Turkey's parliament ratified that deal the week before. On Saturday, Bilal Erdoğan was in Lebanon's Akkar saying the Mecca agreement would "protect the region, including Lebanon".

Analysis. Gulf monarchies are hedging their dependence on US guarantees. Those guarantees did not stop Iranian strikes on Gulf soil this year, and Bloomberg Economics' Ziad Daoud argues the midterms give the Gulf only "a little breathing room" to diversify away from hydrocarbons, from the US and from Hormuz. Ankara is the big winner. Through the Mecca pact and now Doha, Turkey becomes a treaty-bound security provider to both rival poles of Gulf politics. That gives it leverage on Syria, Libya and Gaza's stabilisation force, where it has been kept out. The pact's ambiguity is deliberate: it deters without committing Turkey, a NATO member, to fight Iran. Israel will read it as a Turkish shield for Qatar, the host of Hamas's political bureau.

What to watch. Watch whether the pact goes to Turkey's parliament for ratification, whether Washington comments, and whether the UAE, conspicuously absent from both pacts, responds with arrangements of its own.

5. Gaza: Israel kills Hamas's Rafah commander as floods engulf displacement camps.

What happened. On 14 November Israel and the Shin Bet announced a drone strike in al-Mawasi, Khan Younis, on a "senior Hamas military commander". Hamas confirmed that its Rafah brigade commander, Samir Abu Younis, was killed. Early Sunday another drone strike killed one Palestinian and wounded two in southern Gaza. Since the October 2025 ceasefire, at least 1,643 Palestinians have been killed and 5,964 wounded, and five Israeli soldiers have been killed in the same period. Earlier in the week, Israel struck at Nasser Hospital to target a Hamas commander, according to its military. UNICEF reports more than 220,000 people affected by floods. Three children and their mother were killed when a sand dune collapsed on their tent in Deir al-Balah, and about 4,000 buildings are at risk of collapse. UNICEF's winter appeal is 97.5 million dollars. The UK added 5 million pounds and said more than 150,000 tents and 300,000 tarpaulins are waiting outside Gaza.

Background. UNSC Resolution 2803 created the Board of Peace and an International Stabilization Force mandated to separate Israeli forces from areas controlled by the Palestinian technocratic committee (NCAG). Recruitment has been slow: Kosovo and Albania signed on in September. The order of Hamas disarmament and Israeli withdrawal remains unresolved.

Analysis. Israel's pattern of targeted killings under the ceasefire, now reaching a brigade commander, shows how it reads the truce: an end to major manoeuvre, not to its freedom of action. With a government in transition, the military has wide latitude. Each strike weakens Hamas's command in Rafah, the area most relevant to any ISF deployment. It also hands Hamas an argument against disarmament before Israel withdraws. The humanitarian crisis is now driven more by access and weather than by bombardment, and that makes the Board of Peace accountable for aid delivery. Reports say Israel claims it delivered winter supplies while the Board says it must do more.

What to watch. Watch for any Hamas response, for winter-supply entries, and for whether Israel's next government signals movement on the ISF's phase-two deployment.

6. Iraq and Lebanon test the state's monopoly on force.

What happened. On Saturday 14 November, Iraqi PM Ali al-Zaidi dismissed nine security and oil-sector officials over petroleum smuggling at Jurf al-Sakhar, Kataib Hezbollah's stronghold south of Baghdad, and referred suspects to the judiciary. The statement did not name the militia. Kataib Hezbollah reportedly blocked Zaidi's first committee. A replacement, led by a PMF official after a deal, entered under heavy military escort but was not allowed to interview PMF personnel. A senior politician told Asharq Al-Awsat that the dispute "has become personal". In Lebanon, the army raided a warehouse in Musharrafieh in Beirut's southern suburbs on 13 November and seized a large cache of arms and ammunition. It arrested one person and opened an investigation without naming an owner. PM Nawaf Salam said "the era of impunity is over".

Background. Zaidi took office in May. In November he also cancelled Mosul airport service contracts linked to armed groups and rejected disarmament proposals from the Coordination Framework. Iraq's government says it has identified the launch site of the 8 November drone strike that halted gas production at Khor Mor. In Lebanon, June's US-sponsored framework with Israel tied a gradual Israeli withdrawal to Hezbollah's disarmament. Israel still holds a security zone about 10 km deep, and UNIFIL's mandate ends on 31 December.

Analysis. Both moves are cautious, and both are shaped by Iran's weakness. Zaidi targets the militias' revenue rather than their weapons, and he never names Kataib Hezbollah. Lebanon's army hits an unattributed depot. Asharq Al-Awsat notes that the haul may look more like an arms dealer's stock than a Hezbollah arsenal. Still, a blockaded Tehran has less money to send. Washington and Israel are pressing hard; Israel has reportedly asked Beirut to stop Hezbollah joining any renewed Iran war. Both premiers are trying to show the state's reach before the regional settlement is decided. The risk in both cases is a violent backlash from groups that have said they will not disarm.

What to watch. In Iraq, watch for judicial charges and the response of Kataib Hezbollah and Nujaba. In Lebanon, watch Hezbollah's reaction to the raid and the post-UNIFIL security structure the G7 says must be built quickly.

Libya. Dbeibah told Guterres at COP31 in Antalya that elections must advance; this is his office's account, with no UN readout yet. The UN-mediated "4+4" roadmap promises votes within 24 months under one executive, and Turkey hosted both Dbeibah and Khaled Haftar in the same week.

Tunisia. Saied blamed "remnants of colonialism" for fires and water outages on 10 November and ordered judicial probes. This follows October's largest anti-Saied march in years and the four-year sentence handed to an opposition politician over social media posts.

Kurdistan Region. After meeting Zaidi on 9 November, KRG PM Masrour Barzani said Baghdad had promised to "provide and guarantee salaries". The 2027 draft budget gives the Region 12.68% against the 14.14% the KRG demands.

Libya. The OSINT outlet Eekad tracked a UAE-linked Boeing 747 cargo flight landing at Benghazi's Benina airport on 12 November; reports tie such flights to arms transfers in Africa.

Diplomacy and international relations.

US–China–Russia. The Kremlin and the White House confirmed a first-ever Trump–Xi–Putin trilateral in Shenzhen on 19 November on the sidelines of APEC. Nuclear arms control is the main agenda item, and Iran is on the list, though no agreements are expected.

Qatar mediation. After PM Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman met Trump and Vance on 9 November, Doha cited "progress". Its plan diverges from Iran's sequencing by putting IAEA inspections first.

Saudi–China. Saudi national security adviser Musaed Al-Aiban delivered a written message from MBS to Xi via Wang Yi in early November. The content was not disclosed, and one secondary outlet claims it concerned Hormuz.

G7/Lebanon. At a meeting in Paris on 10 November, G7 foreign ministers agreed on the need to avoid a "security void" when UNIFIL's mandate ends on 31 December. France wants an extension, the US opposes one, and Germany has floated an EU force.

Morocco/Western Sahara. France reaffirmed its "unwavering" support for Moroccan sovereignty on 13 November, and the GCC reaffirmed its backing on 10 November. Hespress counted autonomy references in 33 of 48 substantive interventions in the UN General Assembly debate.

UAE. MBZ received US envoys Witkoff and Kushner for UAE-hosted Russia–Ukraine talks, and met Arab League Secretary-General Nabil Fahmy on regional coordination. These are official readouts.

EU. EU foreign ministers meet on 16 November. High Representative Kallas has not ruled out sanctions on Iran over cyberattacks on European supermarket chains attributed to Tehran.

Egypt. FM Badr Abdelatty was in Kuwait for the 14th joint committee, and in the UAE discussed with his Ukrainian counterpart Egypt hosting a global grain storage hub. Egypt also reopened its embassy in Tripoli.

Economy, energy and trade.

Oil. Brent settled at 95.82 dollarson 12 November, up 3.3% on the reports of attacks on Saudi energy sites. It held near 96 dollarson 13 November as the IEA said supply was outpacing demand for the first time since the war began, and later dipped below 90 dollars. The market is pricing in a long-lasting dual chokepoint with spare capacity elsewhere, not an outright shock.

Shipping/Suez. Egypt's Suez Canal receipts rose 94% year on year to 724 million dollars in October, their best month since late 2023, and Evergreen resumed transits on 8 November. FY2025/26 revenue was 4.67 billion dollars, still far below 2023's 10.25 billion dollars. Paradoxically, Houthi attention has shifted from shipping to Saudi Arabia.

Iran. Gasoline rationing is tightening and Bandar Abbas drivers blocked a road over empty pumps, but the regime "dares not reprice" seven years after the November 2019 fuel riots. Bessent projects a contraction of more than 25%.

Turkey. Annual inflation edged up to 29.77% in October after a near five-year low. The central bank resumed easing with a 100 bp cut to 36%, and the next decision is on 10 December. Energy import costs are the main risk.

Gulf investment and energy. Kuwait Oil Company awarded two offshore drilling rig contracts worth KD96.55m to ADNOC, and India now sources crude from about 43 countries, with the UAE supplying about 15%.

Mauritania. US firm Murphy Oil signed two offshore production-sharing contracts, according to the US embassy, a small Western energy foothold in the Sahel's Atlantic edge.

Libya. UNSMIL chief Hanna Tetteh warned that excessive spending, weak governance and fuel smuggling are eroding purchasing power, as the Central Bank's monetary committee was reconstituted.

Oman. Airport passenger traffic fell 6.7% to 10.43m by September, a measurable cost of the war even for the neutral mediator.

Iran. Iran Human Rights documented at least 691 executions in the first ten months of 2026, 77 of them in October. At least 81 people were executed on political charges, including 32 protesters.

Egypt press freedom. Mada Masr's appeal against the 27 October closure order was heard on 11 November, with no result reported yet. RSF called the closure a "declaration of war against the independent press".

Yemen. More than 340,000 people have been displaced since July as fighting moves towards Aden and Taiz.

UAE. A pile-up of 18 vehicles in Fujairah killed 14 people, 10 Emiratis and 4 Indians, and injured 30, according to Khaleej Times' live coverage. The UAE also announced the Eid Al Etihad (National Day) holidays.

Egypt/Libya football. Mohamed Salah scored twice to reach 70 international goals, overtaking Hossam Hassan's national record. Libya beat Tunisia in an AFCON qualifier.

Turkey/Gaza. At COP31 in Antalya, Erdoğan called the Gaza war an "ecocide" as well as a genocide, folding Palestine into Turkey's climate diplomacy as host.

Oman. The Dhofar Mountains were named among the world's Top 100 Geological Heritage Sites, and Oman's National Day holiday falls on 25 to 26 November.

Israel.

International.

Washington's Iran pause cuts against Israeli preferences. Mossad chief Gofman has lobbied the CIA for renewed strikes, and Netanyahu now uses the Iranian nuclear threat as his main argument for a unity government.

Turkey and Qatar's joint communiqué accused Israel of violating the Gaza peace plan.

Local.

Netanyahu's appeal of 14 November and Eisenkot's rejection set up the Likud–Yashar meeting.

Herzog urged the next government to "intensify" the fight against crime in Arab communities.

The new Knesset convened on 10 November, according to one analysis.

Context: For the first time since 2022, Israel's next government may not be led by Netanyahu. The decisive questions are whether Arab parties' outside support is acceptable to Eisenkot's camp and whether Likud will accept a junior role.

Palestine (Gaza and the West Bank)

International.

Arab and European actors continue to tie the Board of Peace's credibility to aid access; the UK says supplies are being blocked.

ISF deployment remains incomplete; the latest contributors are Kosovo and Albania.

Local.

Israel killed Rafah brigade commander Samir Abu Younis in al-Mawasi on 14 November. Since the ceasefire, 1,643 Palestinians have been killed.

Floods affect more than 220,000 people, and children are among the dead.

Settler attacks during the olive harvest continued in Beit Fajjar, Ya'bad, Deir Jarir and Atara. At al-Auja near Jericho, Palestinian sources say 13 sheep died after settlers sprayed toxic substances (Times of Israel; Arab News; Hamas-aligned Palinfo).

The PA's elections are postponed to September 2027.

Context: A "ceasefire" that permits targeted killings, a stabilisation force short of troops and a PA without elections leave Palestinians with neither security nor representation going into winter.

Lebanon.

International.

The G7 warned against a "security void" after UNIFIL. Lebanon has asked Guterres to reconsider the timetable, France wants an extension and the US opposes one.

Bilal Erdoğan's visit to Akkar on 14 November, where he promoted the Mecca pact and floated twinning Tripoli with Istanbul, signals Turkey's growing interest in northern Lebanon.

Local.

The army's raid in Musharrafieh and Salam's statement that "the era of impunity is over" mark a symbolic step in the state's push to monopolise arms.

Israeli strikes continue in the south. On 5 November three girls and their grandmother were killed near Aynata.

LBCI's evening bulletin on 14 November led with a new electricity plan and Lebanon's insistence on a continued UN role in the south.

Hezbollah's bloc urged the government to rethink negotiations that it says have produced nothing.

Context: Lebanon is stuck between Israel's demand to disarm first and Hezbollah's demand that Israel withdraw first, with its international buffer due to expire in six weeks.

Syria.

International.

Damascus confirmed sending military advisers, with 600 special forces due, to Saudi Arabia, while denying any deployment in Yemen. This follows a Saudi defence agreement and more than 400 million dollars in loans.

Israeli incursions in Quneitra continue. Netanyahu claims he spoke directly with al-Sharaa, and Damascus has not confirmed it.

Local.

State agency SANA reported three people detained in Jabata al-Khashab on 13 November.

Government forces and al-Hijri's National Guard clashed in Suwayda's countryside on 11 November.

A debate over media access to information in Damascus points to early friction over press freedom under the new order.

Context: Al-Sharaa is spending his limited military capital to buy Saudi patronage and money, while Israel's occupation in the southwest and Druze autonomy in Suwayda keep the state's sovereignty incomplete.

Jordan.

International.

Amman's posture remains defined by its rejection of displacement and an "alternative homeland" for Palestinians, and by tighter security cooperation after the Karak attack. The US embassy raised its security posture.

Local.

Little new in the past 48 hours. The latest is King Abdullah's speech from the throne vowing that "terrorist outlaws" would not undermine unity, after the church attack in Karak that killed eight or nine people; three suspects were arrested.

Context: The Karak attack revived concerns about jihadist recruitment in Jordan's tribal south at a time of regional war and economic strain.

Iraq (incl. Kurdistan Region)

International.

Zaidi's confrontation with Kataib Hezbollah is read in Washington as a test of whether Baghdad can contain Iran's networks. FDD describes him as putting "Iranian stooges on notice".

Iraqi Airways resumed Baghdad–Beijing flights via Basra from 14 November.

Local.

Nine officials were dismissed over Jurf al-Sakhar smuggling on 14 November.

The government denied any nationwide closure of its airspace.

Lebanon's General Security denied telling Iraqi officials that the killing of former MP Ahmed al-Alwani was an "honour" case (14 November).

Kurdistan Region: Khor Mor remains offline after the 8 November drone strike, and Baghdad is covering cooking gas supplies. The KRG is recording withheld salaries as debt owed to employees; federal transfers covered only 46 of 144 payrolls between 2014 and 2025.

Context: Iraq's new government is cautiously turning Iran's weakness into state leverage. The Khor Mor strikes show the factions' ability to impose costs on Erbil and on Gulf investors such as Dana Gas.

Iran.

International.

The IRGC claimed a drone strike on a US radar in Bahrain (15 November), and the navy claimed tanker mine strikes. Speaker Ghalibaf says the Hormuz regime will "never return" to pre-war status.

Tehran's seven conditions, passed through Qatar, require that the blockade be lifted, sanctions eased and funds released before the strait reopens within two days. Nuclear talks would come later.

The IAEA still cannot locate about 440.9 kg of 60% enriched uranium.

Local.

Gasoline rationing is tightening and protests broke out in Bandar Abbas. Retirees and teachers protested over pensions in six cities on 1 November.

Executions are at multi-decade highs, with 691 in ten months.

Context: Tehran is betting that the strait gives it more leverage than the blockade gives Washington. Its domestic economy, rationing fuel it cannot afford to reprice, is where that bet is most exposed. Note that Tehran Times, Press TV, Fars and Mehr are state or IRGC-affiliated, and Iran International is opposition-leaning.

Turkey.

International.

The Qatar defence pact (13 November) adds to the Mecca agreement with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, making Ankara a treaty-based security provider in the Gulf.

As COP31 host in Antalya, Erdoğan called for Hormuz to reopen and labelled the Gaza war an "ecocide".

Ankara hosted both Libyan rivals, Dbeibah and Khaled Haftar, in the same week.

Local.

A steel-plant explosion near İzmir on 9 November killed at least 16, and Erdoğan ordered an investigation.

Inflation is stuck near 30% (29.77% in October) and the policy rate is 36%.

The İmamoğlu mass trial at Silivri continues in its second year, and there is a separate espionage case.

Context: Erdoğan is turning the region's security vacuum into Turkish leverage abroad while keeping the main domestic challenger in court.

Saudi Arabia.

International.

MBS sent a written message to Xi via Saudi NSA Al-Aiban, and Riyadh joined regional security officials in Tehran on 26 October to try to head off renewed war.

Riyadh recruited Syrian special forces and signed a defence pact with Damascus.

Local.

The Houthis claimed hits on KKIA, the East-West Pipeline and Jazan troops (13 November), and the coalition says it intercepted a missile fired towards Riyadh. Earlier, an attempted attack on an Aramco building in Riyadh caused limited shrapnel damage, according to the energy ministry.

Qatar Airways resumed daily flights to Riyadh (13 November) and Dammam (14 November), a sign that commercial normality is partially returning despite the strikes.

Context: The kingdom's Hormuz hedge, the pipeline to Yanbu, has become the Houthis' target, turning Yemen from a frozen conflict into Saudi Arabia's most direct war risk.

United Arab Emirates.

International.

Abu Dhabi hosted US-mediated Russia–Ukraine trilateral talks, with MBZ receiving Witkoff and Kushner.

The UAE now supplies about 15% of India's crude imports, gaining market share as Hormuz-dependent suppliers falter.

UAE-linked cargo flights into Benghazi drew OSINT scrutiny again.

Local.

The Fujairah pile-up killed 14, and National Day holidays were set.

November fuel prices fell (Super 98 at Dh4.16).

Context: The UAE is not part of either the Mecca or the Doha pact and is building its standing as a broker on Ukraine and a reliable supplier in the energy market.

Qatar.

International.

Qatar signed the mutual defence pact with Turkey on 13 November.

Its PM remains the main US–Iran mediator, and Qatar's 48-hour, two-stage plan was presented to Trump on 9 November.

Local.

Qatar Airways warned of possible US delays after the 13 November US ground stop and resumed Saudi routes.

The QNL heritage-protection drive marked 14 November.

Context: Doha is taking out insurance in Ankara while acting as Washington's indispensable channel to Tehran, a familiar Qatari hedge raised to treaty level.

Kuwait.

International.

Kuwait hosted Egypt's FM for the 14th joint committee and held a third round of senior officials' talks with Australia.

KOC awarded ADNOC two offshore rig contracts worth KD96.55m.

Local.

Decree-Law No. 114 of 2026 opens state-subsidised mortgages to eligible citizens, an important measure on Kuwait's chronic housing queue.

A crackdown on "commercial concealment" (14 November) and a bid for UNESCO listing for Kuwait Towers.

Context: Kuwait is legislating by decree-law on social files such as housing and keeping a low regional profile, despite Iranian missile and drone attacks on its territory earlier in the war.

Bahrain.

International.

Iran's claimed attack on Al-Riffa base and Manama's first nationwide alerts since September put the host of the US Fifth Fleet back on the front line (15 November).

Local.

The interior ministry's shelter advisory dominated the day.

Routine business news included BDB's Q3 results and new Bahrain Bourse trading sessions.

Context: As the most exposed host of US forces and the smallest GCC state, Bahrain bears the risk of Iran's strategy of hitting US assets on Arab soil.

Oman.

International.

Muscat and Tehran discussed a temporary reopening of Hormuz with a set route and a mine-clearance plan, a parallel track to Qatar's.

Uzbekistan's president visits on 16 November, and Oman established diplomatic relations with Liberia.

Local.

Four royal decrees set up laws for the Oman Financial Centre, and Chamber of Commerce elections are on 23 November.

Airport traffic is down 6.7%.

Context: Oman's neutrality buys it a mediating role on the strait it shares with Iran, but it is not spared the economic costs.

Yemen.

International.

Houthi strikes on Saudi infrastructure (13 November) make Yemen a potential trigger for renewed US action under Trump's "no provocations" condition.

The Yemeni government claims IRGC and Hezbollah advisers are deployed in Houthi areas.

Local.

The Giants Brigades repelled a Houthi attack in Lahj, two ballistic missiles were fired at Aden, and there were airstrikes on Houthi reinforcements in Taiz's Wadi al-Sahya.

The government's "Dawn of Yemen" offensive claims strikes on 875 Houthi sites.

More than 340,000 people have been displaced since July. Houthi-run Saba and Houthi military statements are partisan sources.

Context: The Houthis are fighting a two-front war, for Aden in the south and against Saudi Arabia in the north, while serving as Iran's main deniable means of escalation.
