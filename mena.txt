Here is your Middle East briefing for Monday, 9 November.

Mines in Hormuz, missiles over Riyadh, Eisenkot's mandate: the region's war system widens as Trump hosts Qatar.

Four developments from the weekend matter most. First, the Gulf war system is escalating on two linked fronts at once. The IRGC says it mined and set fire to at least five tankers in the Strait of Hormuz on 7 to 8 November; Washington has answered with "minor" strikes on Qeshm and a new CENTCOM land-interdiction cell, Task Force Iron Gate, which takes the blockade inland. Second, the Saudi–Houthi truce has collapsed into open war. Houthi missile debris hit an Aramco tower in Riyadh's financial district, and Saudi-led strikes near Sanaa killed at least 27 to 33 people, according to Houthi health officials. Third, Israeli politics changed overnight: President Herzog formally handed Gadi Eisenkot the mandate, the first time in years that someone other than Netanyahu has had the first shot at forming a government. Eisenkot has 67 recommendations on paper, but 19 of them come from Arab parties he will not seat in cabinet. Fourth, a Saudi-centred security architecture is taking shape beneath all this: Turkey's ratification of the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement, Egypt's parliamentary mandate to defend Saudi Arabia, Sharaa's defence deals in Riyadh and Egypt's return to Tripoli. Diplomacy hangs over everything. Trump meets Qatar's prime minister today (9 November) on a mediators' "two-day" sequencing plan that Rubio publicly called unrealistic.

Top stories.

1. Hormuz: mines, burning tankers, and a blockade that now reaches overland.

What happened. Iran's Revolutionary Guard said two tankers struck mines in the Strait of Hormuz on Sunday 8 November. That came a day after Tehran claimed five other tankers had "met similar fates". The IRGC Navy separately said three ships were burning in the strait after trying to evade Iran's blockade early on Sunday (The National; TASS, Russian state agency). The IRGC said the ships had used an "unauthorised route" with navigation systems switched off, and warned that vessels cooperating with the "aggressive" US military on such routes would "definitely" hit mines. UK maritime authorities confirmed incidents involving two tankers crossing the strait that morning and said no crew were hurt. Indian wire copy reported US forces disabling a vessel in the Gulf of Oman on the same day.

Washington is not de-escalating. Trump confirmed US strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island, which he called "minor strikes" on a newly built air-defence system: "They built a little bit of an anti-aircraft device, and we knocked it out in about 15 minutes, maybe five". Iranian state media reported two missile strikes in the Suza area of Qeshm and claimed its air defences downed an MQ-9 Reaper; that claim has not been verified. These were the first strikes since the Republicans' setback in the 3 November midterms, and they followed an earlier round on Qeshm on 2 to 3 November. Trump insists "it's not a war". He says fresh large-scale attacks are "highly unlikely" if Tehran agrees a nuclear deal and reopens the strait.

On 7 November CENTCOM commander Adm. Brad Cooper ordered the creation of Task Force Iron Gate. Its job is to disrupt Iran's movement of advanced weapons, dual-use technology and sanctioned goods across its land borders and the Caspian Sea. It is small: about 20 civilian and military staff in Tampa. CENTCOM says the naval blockade has denied Iran more than 25 billion dollars in crude exports. Since July, US forces have redirected 147 commercial vessels and disabled five that refused to comply. Iran's military dismissed the task force as "word play". CENTCOM is also offering up to 20 million dollars, plus possible relocation, for information on Iran's nuclear programme (same source).

Background. This is the ninth month of the US–Israeli war with Iran, which began in late February. A June arrangement, the "Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding", paused major operations, but the interim truce broke down on 8 July when Iran struck commercial ships in Hormuz. Iran's counter-blockade has cut traffic to roughly 20% of pre-war levels. Both sides now treat the strait as a bargaining chip. Iran offers to reopen it in exchange for an end to the US blockade. The US wants the reopening, plus nuclear concessions, before sanctions relief.

Analysis. What we are seeing is mutual coercive blockade, not a return to the high-intensity war of spring. Both sides are calibrating pain to strengthen their position in mediated talks. The IRGC's mine claims work as signals whether or not each one is independently confirmed. One analyst notes that the announcements move Brent whatever the facts turn out to be. Iron Gate matters less for its staff of 20 than for the signal it sends. Washington is extending its maximum-pressure logic to the Caucasus, Central Asian and Caspian corridors, the routes that tie Iran to Russia, and implicitly to Turkey, Iraq, Armenia and Turkmenistan. That creates friction with partners whose border trade with Iran is economically vital, Iraq and Turkey above all. The timing, right after the midterms, suggests the White House wants to show escalation dominance before any deal so that it cannot be portrayed as negotiating from weakness. Asian importers lose most from the stalemate: India, China, Japan and Korea, along with Gulf producers whose exports must transit the strait. The winners are producers with bypass routes, Saudi Arabia's East–West pipeline and the UAE's Fujairah outlet, although the Houthis are now hitting Saudi Arabia directly (see story 3).

What to watch. Whether Lloyd's and the UK Joint Maritime Information Centre confirm the IRGC claims. Whether the US starts striking Iranian mine-laying assets, which Trump has threatened. How Baku, Ashgabat, Yerevan and Ankara respond to Iron Gate. And the oil price when markets open today.

2. Israel: Herzog hands Eisenkot the mandate, and Netanyahu's era is in question.

What happened. On Sunday 8 November President Isaac Herzog formally gave Gadi Eisenkot, the former IDF chief of staff and leader of Yashar, the mandate to form a government after the inconclusive 27 October election. Factions totalling 67 of the incoming Knesset's 120 members recommended him, including 19 from the two Arab-led lists, Ra'am and the Joint List. Only 46 recommended Netanyahu. He has 28 days, extendable by up to 14. Eisenkot says he wants a "national emergency government" and would like to swear in a minority government quickly. Netanyahu has called for a "unity government" with his entire bloc. He warned that Israel "won't survive" a coalition that relies on Arab parties, and Eisenkot called that "pathetic spin".

The arithmetic. Eisenkot has a majority on paper but no coalition. He has ruled out including Arab parties in government. The Joint List has offered support from outside, and Ahmad Tibi reportedly sees an outside-supported minority government as the likely outcome. Avigdor Lieberman is the pivot: he rejects any government that depends on Arab votes. Eisenkot's alternatives are a power-sharing deal with Likud or a deal with the Haredi parties, which want military-service exemptions. One Haredi party remains loyal to Netanyahu, and there are signs others may defect.

Background. Netanyahu is fighting his corruption trial while leading a country at war on several fronts: Iran, Lebanon, Gaza and the West Bank. The election became a referendum on 7 October, on the Haredi draft and on the conduct of the Iran war. Arab parties have openly backed a centrist candidate before: Ra'am joined the Bennett–Lapid government in 2021. This time the Joint List has done so too, which is a notable shift for its Hadash–Ta'al components.

Analysis. For foreign-policy watchers, the important point is that an Eisenkot government would differ from Netanyahu in style and in its relations with Washington and Europe more than in the substance of Israel's security doctrine. Eisenkot is a security hawk who supported the Iran war. He is unlikely to concede on Palestinian statehood with a coalition that includes figures to his right, and certainly not with Lieberman. He is, however, likely to be more amenable to Trump's Gaza plan (story 5), to the Board of Peace's demand to admit the International Stabilization Force and Palestinian technocrats, and to repairing ties with the EU. Trump is reportedly pressing for Gaza movement once the post-election period ends. Netanyahu still controls the transitional government. Several outcomes are plausible: a Likud split, a Haredi defection, or a stalemate that ends in a sixth election in under a decade. The pattern from 2019 to 2022 suggests the last should not be dismissed.

What to watch. Lieberman's red lines. Whether Shas or United Torah Judaism open talks with Eisenkot. Whether any Likud MKs break ranks. How the far right on the street reacts: on 8 November eleven people were arrested after a mass brawl in Lod that followed a far-right "sovereignty march" through an Arab neighbourhood.

3. Saudi–Houthi war: an Aramco tower hit in Riyadh, mass casualties near Sanaa.

What happened. On 8 November the Saudi-led coalition said its air defences intercepted two ballistic missiles fired towards Riyadh. Coalition spokesman Maj Gen Turki al-Maliki said they were "launched by the Houthi terrorist militia". Debris damaged an Aramco administrative tower in the King Abdullah Financial District. The Energy Ministry described the damage as minor and reported no casualties, but CNN-verified footage shows several floors with blown-out windows. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree claimed a series of missile and drone attacks on Saudi oil facilities and King Khalid International Airport. Etihad has suspended Abu Dhabi–Riyadh and Abu Dhabi–Dammam flights until 19 November.

The previous day, 7 November, airstrikes on the village of al-Shariya near Sanaa killed at least 27 people and wounded 36, according to Houthi-run Health Ministry spokesman Anees Alasbahi, who blamed Saudi Arabia. Later Houthi figures put the toll at 33 dead and 39 wounded. The Houthis claim the coalition carried out 105 air and missile strikes across Sanaa and seven other governorates in 24 hours. Yemen's government forces dispute the attribution and accuse the Houthis of firing a ballistic missile at the site. Earlier in the week, a strike on a market in southern Yemen killed at least 30 people, and each side blamed the other. Iran's official news agency condemned the strikes on Yemen and called for an end to "the attacks and the blockade" (IRNA, state media).

Background. The UN-brokered April 2022 truce had broadly held through Gaza and the first months of the Iran war, though the Houthis struck Abha airport in July. The current round dates from about 5 October, after a Saudi strike on Sanaa airport that was reportedly meant to stop an Iranian aircraft landing. Riyadh's response has been to build a coalition. Egypt's parliament authorised President Sisi on 26 October to take "necessary measures" to defend Saudi Arabia, though Sisi says Egypt will not attack Yemen. Turkey ratified the Mecca pact (story 4). Reports persist, denied by Damascus, of Syrian soldiers deployed to Saudi Arabia and Yemen (see Syria below).

Analysis. Saudi Arabia's post-2022 "containment" strategy for Yemen, buying quiet in order to concentrate on Vision 2030, has failed. A debris strike in KAFD hurts investor perceptions far more than any physical damage. It is a direct hit on the image of Riyadh as a safe financial hub at a time when the kingdom is courting foreign capital. The Houthis are acting as Iran's most autonomous lever. They are opening a southern front against a Gulf state that has stayed formally outside the US–Iran war, which raises the cost to Riyadh of tacitly backing the US blockade. The civilian toll near Sanaa will revive Western parliamentary scrutiny of arms sales to Riyadh, especially in the UK and EU. Washington is likely to prioritise Saudi air-defence resupply.

What to watch. Whether Houthi attacks extend to Red Sea shipping or to Jeddah and Yanbu. Further mass-casualty incidents. Any UN Security Council meeting on Yemen. Whether Saudi Arabia formally invokes the Mecca pact.

4. The Mecca pact and a Saudi-centred security order.

What happened. On 5 November Turkey's Grand National Assembly ratified the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan by 279 votes to 119. Opposition parties warned it could drag Turkish soldiers into regional wars. The pact was signed in Mecca on 7 August. It contains a mutual-defence clause (an attack on one is an attack on all), provides for intelligence sharing, joint exercises and defence-industrial cooperation, places its secretariat in Saudi Arabia, and binds members for ten years. Turkish officials say no troops will fight in Yemen. An AKP MP said a separate parliamentary mandate would be needed for any deployment to Saudi Arabia. Al-Monitor reported the vote as a "boost to Saudi Arabia". In the same week Syria's President Ahmed al-Sharaa was in Riyadh on 4 November, where the two governments signed a defence-ministry cooperation agreement and accords on reconstruction, energy and housing amid speculation that Syria will join the pact. Pakistan's cabinet has ratified a separate defence accord with Kuwait (Kuwait Times, date unconfirmed).

Background. The pact builds on the earlier Saudi–Pakistan strategic mutual-defence relationship. The Iran war widened it as Gulf states lost confidence in exclusive reliance on US guarantees.

Analysis. This is the most important structural change in regional security since the Abraham Accords. In effect it is a Sunni-majority collective-defence framework. It includes a nuclear-armed state (Pakistan) and a NATO member (Turkey), and it is anchored in Riyadh rather than Washington. For Ankara it brings Gulf financing and defence-export markets and turns the post-2021 reconciliation with Riyadh into an institution. For NATO it raises awkward questions about Article 5 overlap. For Iran it is containment by regional actors, not by America. For Israel the picture is mixed: the pact is a counterweight to Iran, but it has no Israeli component and includes Turkey, whose relations with Israel are hostile. Egypt's parliamentary mandate (story 3) and Syria's courtship show the architecture widening. Implementation will be slow, and a deployment vote in Ankara will be the real test.

What to watch. Whether Turkey tables a deployment mandate. Whether Syria or Egypt formally accede. How Iran responds diplomatically, and how the UAE positions itself; it is notably absent.

5. US–Iran diplomacy: a "two-day" plan, Rubio's scepticism and today's Trump–Qatar meeting.

What happened. Qatar and other mediators, reportedly including Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Egypt and Turkey, have discussed a two-step sequencing draft with both governments. On day one the US would lift the naval blockade and release some frozen Iranian funds, and Iran would formally invite the IAEA to inspect bombed nuclear sites. On day two the Strait of Hormuz would reopen and nuclear talks would resume, focused on Iran's highly enriched uranium stockpile. Secretary of State Marco Rubio poured cold water on it. A breakthrough was "not right in front of us", he said, and some of Iran's demands were "outrageous", "ridiculous" and "unrealistic". Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi wrote on X that American voters had made their choice in the midterms and the White House now had to make its own. Washington should accept Tehran's offer, including an end to the war "on all fronts", or "choose war" (The Hill; Press TV, Iranian state media). On state TV he said mediators had not travelled at all since the "shock" of the midterms. Axios reports that Trump will meet Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani on Monday 9 November. The WSJ, as relayed by Fox, reports that Trump's advisers are split over resuming strikes or continuing talks.

Analysis. It is still unclear who authored the "two-day" framework; Tehran presents a version of it as its own. Both sides accept sequencing but disagree on the order. Iran wants the blockade lifted and the war ended "on all fronts", which would bind Israel in Lebanon and possibly Yemen. Washington wants verifiable nuclear steps and an open strait first. Araghchi's reading of the midterms is that Trump's domestic position is weaker and the price of a deal should rise. Washington's reading is the opposite: Qeshm and Iron Gate show it will not be hurried. Qatar's leverage rests on its record (the Gaza ceasefire, prisoner exchanges) and on its LNG exports, which also transit Hormuz. China is re-engaging: envoy Zhai June is touring Iran, Oman and Saudi Arabia, and a China–Oman joint statement on 2 November called for a return to the Islamabad MoU.

What to watch. The readout from Trump and Al Thani. Whether Iran issues the IAEA invitation as a goodwill step. Any change in Vance's tone; he has said there is no deadline.

6. Egypt returns to Tripoli after 12 years.

What happened. Egypt reopened its embassy in Tripoli on 7 November after a closure that began in 2014. Foreign Minister Badr Abdelatty inaugurated it and met Prime Minister Abdul Hamid Dbeibah. Abdelatty raised Egypt's objections to the 2019 Turkey–Libya maritime memorandum, which Libya's eastern-based parliament ratified on 20 October after years of opposition. Al Jazeera's analysis calls the move a regional shift: Cairo, long Khalifa Haftar's patron, is hedging towards the west.

Analysis. Three dynamics come together here. First, the eastern parliament's ratification of the Turkish maritime deal shows that Haftar's camp has made its peace with Ankara. That leaves Cairo, which disputes the Turkish–Libyan EEZ line with Greece, outflanked if it stays in the east alone. Second, US-mediated talks reportedly envisage keeping Dbeibah as prime minister while giving eastern figures, possibly Saddam Haftar, more institutional authority. Cairo wants to shape that deal from inside Tripoli. Third, Egypt's priorities in Sudan (the RSF, which Haftar is accused of supplying) and the Nile give it reasons not to depend entirely on Benghazi. Losers: Greece, whose maritime position is weakened by the Turkey–eastern Libya convergence. Winners: Dbeibah, who gains legitimacy despite protests in Tripoli demanding his resignation.

What to watch. The Greek reaction. Any Egyptian–Turkish coordination on Libya. The next round of the power-sharing talks.

Local.

A ruling-camp party is floating a 30-month "transition" that the opposition sees as a third-term manoeuvre. Sit-ins continue.

Context: Mauritania's careful neutrality on Western Sahara is being tested just as gas-era rents raise the stakes of its succession politics.

Israel.

International.

Trump is reportedly pressing Netanyahu to move forward on the Gaza plan now the election is over. The Board of Peace wants Israel to admit the ISF and Palestinian technocrats.

Weekend news roundups also report that Israel deported 14 activists; details were not independently confirmed.

Local.

Herzog gave Eisenkot the mandate with 67 recommendations. Netanyahu has 46 and warns against "reliance on Arab parties".

Eleven people were arrested after a brawl following a far-right march in Lod.

The IDF says it found Hezbollah caches near UN posts in south Lebanon ahead of UNIFIL's year-end departure (same source).

Context: Israel may be at the end of the Netanyahu era. But the pivotal kingmakers (Lieberman, the Haredim) and the war footing mean that any successor government is likely to change Israel's tone more than its strategy.

Palestine (Gaza and the West Bank)

International.

The ISF is still stalled. The Board of Peace says Israeli approvals, including for an ISF base near the Yellow Line, are pending. Israel controls about 63% of Gaza, and Hamas still governs much of the rest and refuses to disarm without a political track.

UNSC Resolution 2803 authorises the Board and the ISF until the end of 2027.

Local.

Floods in Gaza flooded about 70,000 tents and killed five, four of them children. Emergency committees say 200,000 tents are needed. The Government Media Office puts storm damage at 3 million dollars.

In the West Bank: settler incursions into Hebron's Old City, beatings near Salfit, attacks on olive harvesters in Sinjil, a child shot in Qalqilya and arrests in Jenin and Nablus districts (Middle East Eye; Palinfo, Hamas-aligned).

Context: Gaza is frozen in a de facto partition along the Yellow Line, and settler violence in the West Bank is at record levels. An Eisenkot government would be the first test of whether the Trump plan's second phase can start.

Lebanon.

International.

UNIFIL will leave at the end of the year. The Security Council expects a 1701 briefing this month, and Israel and Hezbollah each make their move conditional on the other's: Israeli withdrawal against disarmament.

A statement by five embassies on the Dahr al-Baidar explosives case drew accusations of "interference" from Hezbollah's camp.

Local.

Israeli demolitions in Qantara, shelling of Haddatha, a closure of Deir Mimas and road-building in the Shebaa Valley inside the occupied "buffer zone".

L'Orient's evening recap carried reports that Israel is preparing a "new confrontation" with Hezbollah.

The Lebanese Forces have hardened their rhetoric but keep a dialogue open with Aoun and Salam. Banks are offering large depositors settlement routes for frozen deposits.

Context: Lebanon remains in a no-war, no-peace limbo under the June framework deal, and UNIFIL's departure on 31 December removes the last international buffer.

Syria.

International.

Sharaa's Riyadh visit produced a defence cooperation agreement and reconstruction deals amid speculation that Syria will join the Mecca pact.

AFP, citing Yemeni sources, reported about 3,000 Syrian soldiers deployed in Saudi Arabia and Yemen. Damascus denied any presence "in Yemen".

Sweida is the flashpoint: Syria has moved troops to the western front, and Israel is reportedly flying hardware into the area.

Local.

Presidential decrees created 11 university faculties and appointed an assistant interior minister (SANA, state media).

Chinese firms met Sharaa on reconstruction investment.

Context: Sharaa is trading Syrian manpower and alignment for Saudi money. That deepens Damascus's place in the Sunni bloc but risks drawing a fragile state into Yemen.

Jordan.

International.

Thin window. Jordan's exposure to the Iran war is long-standing: its air defences have intercepted Iranian missiles crossing its airspace.

Local.

Flash-flood and fog warnings were issued as the regional storm passed.

Aqaba port cargo handling rose 46% over ten months, as Red Sea and Hormuz disruption pushes trade towards overland routes (same source).

Context: Jordan is quietly benefiting from the shift of trade to land routes while it absorbs the security spillovers of the Iran and Gaza wars.

Iraq (incl. Kurdistan Region)

International.

PM al-Zaidi met US envoy Tom Barrack on de-escalation. Iron Gate's focus on land routes directly concerns Iraq's border trade with Iran.

Iraq is seeking a higher OPEC+ quota for 2027.

Local.

The PUK is withdrawing from the KRG cabinet over the KDP-convened parliament session.

Khor Mor gas output was halted after a strike. Iraq's media regulator is moving against Korek Telecom.

The 2027 budget session is set for Tuesday. Property sales are falling as the dollar rises.

The government denied a nationwide airspace closure.

Context: Baghdad's balancing between Washington and Tehran is getting harder as the US blockade moves onto land, and the KDP–PUK split threatens to re-divide the Kurdistan Region.

Iran.

International.

The IRGC claims at least five tankers were mined or set on fire. The US struck Qeshm and launched Task Force Iron Gate.

Araghchi: "accept Iran's proposal or choose war". He says mediators have not travelled since the midterms. Iran is seeking leverage through China and Russia.

Local.

Iran International reports three more protesters executed in Karaj and four killed in floods.

The economy is in deep stress: roughly 69% inflation, the rial at record lows and bank failures.

Context: Tehran is betting that coercion in Hormuz and Trump's weaker domestic position will extract a better deal than military parity ever could, while it tightens repression at home.

Turkey.

International.

Parliament ratified the Mecca defence pact with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan by 279 to 119.

Erdoğan attended Azerbaijan's Victory Day. Turkey hosts COP31 in Antalya from today.

Ankara's 2019 maritime deal with Libya now has eastern Libyan backing, which annoys Cairo and Athens.

Local.

Eight people were shot dead in Afyonkarahisar. Families filed criminal complaints over a Cyprus ferry tragedy.

A former SPK deputy chair was remanded in an investment-fund probe. Turkey targets 10 billion dollars in AI-infrastructure investment by 2030.

Ankara presented a 2040 energy plan targeting 175 GW of renewables (Daily Sabah, pro-government).

Context: Turkey is becoming a central node of the Gulf-centred security order while presenting itself to the world as a climate host. The two ambitions will compete for attention over the next fortnight.

Saudi Arabia.

International.

Houthi missile debris damaged an Aramco tower in KAFD. Saudi-led strikes in Yemen intensify.

The Mecca pact gained Turkish ratification, and Riyadh signed defence and reconstruction deals with Syria.

Riyadh is part of the mediators' circle on US–Iran sequencing. China's envoy is due in the kingdom.

Local.

Etihad suspended UAE flights to Riyadh and Dammam until 19 November.

The crown prince received Bahrain's national security adviser on 4 November.

Context: Riyadh has moved from de-escalation to building a coalition, but Houthi strikes on its financial core show how exposed the Vision 2030 model is.

International.

Etihad cut Saudi routes until 19 November. UAE rescuers recently saved 22 crew from a burning cargo ship in the Gulf of Oman.

Dana Gas's Khor Mor field in Iraqi Kurdistan was hit again.

Local.

ADIPEC 2026 reported 67 billion dollars in energy investments. Abu Dhabi Airports reports Q3 growth (Abu Dhabi Media Office, government).

A UAE court held BR Shetty liable in the 5.4 billion dollars NMC case.

Context: The UAE keeps a low profile as the Saudi-led bloc forms. It is absent from the Mecca pact and relies on its Fujairah bypass and its financial sector to ride out the Hormuz crisis.

Bahrain.

International.

Bahrain's national security adviser met the Saudi crown prince in Riyadh, underlining Manama's alignment with the Saudi bloc.

Local.

Context: The war has given Manama grounds to postpone electoral politics. As host of the US Fifth Fleet, it remains the Gulf state most exposed to Iran.

Oman.
