Here is your Middle East briefing for Tuesday, 10 November.

Trump calls the Iran war 'effectively over' as Houthi missiles target Riyadh and Israel's new Knesset convenes.

Three things set the region's agenda on 9 to 10 November. First, after meeting Qatar's prime minister at the White House, Donald Trump said the Iran war is "effectively over" militarily and that renewed US strikes are "quite unlikely". He also boasted of "the greatest military blockade ever", and CENTCOM has just widened that blockade to Iran's land and Caspian routes, so this is coercion held in place rather than de-escalation. Tehran's answer, delivered by Mohsen Rezaei and Masoud Pezeshkian, mixes a reported order from the supreme leader to end the "no war, no peace" limbo with a demand that Washington accept Iran's seven conditions first. Second, the Saudi–Houthi war that reignited in July is now the region's hottest front: missiles were intercepted over Riyadh overnight, the East–West pipeline was hit again, and Riyadh is pulling in Arab military partners (Syria, and potentially Egypt) in a way not seen since 2015. Third, Israel's 26th Knesset is sworn in today while Gadi Eisenkot holds the mandate to form a government. Washington is pressing Netanyahu to let a Palestinian technocratic committee administer Gaza, where the first winter storms have flooded tens of thousands of tents. Underneath all three runs one structural shift: a US administration weakened by its midterm defeat is trying to trade military pressure for a negotiated exit, and the region's middle powers (Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Turkey, Iraq) are each positioning for what follows.

Top stories.

1. Trump calls the Iran war "effectively over" while Washington tightens the blockade.

What happened. On Monday 9 November, Trump met Qatar's prime minister and foreign minister, Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, at the White House. Afterwards he said the war with Iran was militarily "effectively over", spoke of "positive momentum" toward a deal, and said a resumption of US strikes was "quite unlikely", adding that Gulf powers wanted to keep the status quo. The Qatari premier said only that the two sides had "discussed the situation in the region and ways to progress the US-Iran talks." He also said he was keeping force in place: "We have the greatest military blockade ever." The AP account notes that the US had carried out limited strikes on Iran, including on its air defences, at least twice in the previous week. Those were the "defensive" strikes on Qeshm Island on 2 November, the eve of the midterms, and "minor strikes" on air-defence systems that Trump confirmed on 6 November.

Tehran's public response was dismissive. Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, called on the US to "surrender" to Iran's seven conditions and accused Washington of spreading "fake news" about "imaginary talks". Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said there had been no negotiations since what he called the "shock" of the US midterms. President Masoud Pezeshkian said on 9 November that Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei had instructed officials to end the "no war, no peace" stalemate with the US. He also insisted the blockade must be lifted and hostilities ended "on all fronts" before talks resume.

From Israel, the Financial Times reported, via ANI and others, that Netanyahu has been calling Trump "every day" to push for renewed strikes. Axios-sourced reporting says he urged an immediate offensive once the midterm results were in, and that the administration chose to give diplomacy time. A source quoted in the coverage denied there was an "organised" pressure campaign.

Background. The war began with US–Israeli strikes in late February 2026. Ali Khamenei was killed on 28 February, and the Assembly of Experts chose his son Mojtaba as successor in early March. After about 40 days of fighting, Pakistan mediated an MoU that Pezeshkian and Trump signed remotely in June. It never became a settlement. In July Washington imposed a naval blockade on Iran's Gulf and Gulf of Oman ports. On 7 November CENTCOM announced Task Force Iron Gate, a roughly 20-person Tampa-based unit set up under Adm. Brad Cooper to interdict weapons, dual-use technology, sanctioned goods and "illegal revenue streams" crossing Iran's land borders and the Caspian. CENTCOM says the sea blockade has already denied Iran more than 25 dollarsbillion in crude exports. Iran first sent its seven conditions through Qatar in September. Rezaei has named only three publicly: an end to the war on all fronts, release of frozen funds, and an end to the blockade. He has tied reopening the Strait of Hormuz to all seven.

Analysis. Trump's statement reads best as a domestic political move dressed as a strategic one. The midterms were a rebuke over an unpopular war. Rep. Thomas Massie says he will "definitely" reintroduce articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth this week, and a floor vote could put vulnerable Republicans on record. Declaring the war "over" lets the White House reduce the political cost of the conflict without paying for a deal. The blockade stays in place, and Iron Gate extends it to Iran's northern trade with Central Asia, Russia and the Caucasus, the only real economic outlet Tehran has left. In effect Washington is turning a hot war into an open-ended siege. The Gulf "status quo" Trump cited fits reports that Riyadh and Abu Dhabi want the economic pressure kept up without military escalation.

Tehran's signals have two audiences. The "surrender" rhetoric is for domestic and IRGC consumption. The leader's reported directive, relayed only by Pezeshkian, authorises diplomacy without conceding anything up front. Khamenei has not been seen or heard since March and is believed to have been badly wounded, so it remains unclear who actually decides in Tehran. That uncertainty is one reason mediators struggle to read Iranian offers. Qatar comes out stronger: it is again the channel that matters, displacing the Pakistani and Omani tracks of the summer. Israel comes out weaker. Netanyahu's push for escalation is running into a lame-duck president who wants an exit and a domestic coalition crisis that limits his own room.

What to watch. Whether the US eases the blockade in any way (for example, humanitarian carve-outs or a Hormuz corridor) as a first step; Iran's formal reply through Doha; the House vote on Hegseth; more "defensive" US strikes around Qeshm, which would contradict the "over" narrative.

2. The Saudi–Houthi war escalates: missiles over Riyadh, the East–West pipeline hit again, and Arab troops called in.

What happened. Overnight into 10 November, the Saudi-led coalition said its air defences destroyed a ballistic missile and a cruise missile fired at Riyadh after a Civil Defence early-warning alert. It also downed a ballistic missile aimed at the south and a drone over the Eastern Province. No casualties were reported, and the Houthis did not immediately claim the launches. The same reports say the East–West pipeline was attacked again on Monday in the Medina region. A small fire broke out, and an official said "pipeline operations were unaffected". Anadolu counted three missiles rather than two. Al Jazeera separately reported Yemeni government forces claiming new advances as Houthi cross-border attacks resumed.

The other major development is Syrian troops. AFP, citing Yemeni officials, reported that thousands of Syrian soldiers would deploy to Saudi Arabia and Yemen, with some already in place, and a Presidential Leadership Council source put the figure near 3,000. AP described a smaller force of about 600 special forces to help secure the Saudi–Yemeni border. SANA quoted a diplomatic source calling reports of troops sent "at this time" unfounded. Al Jazeera's feature is headlined "Syria confirms sending troops to Saudi Arabia". The most plausible reading of the conflicting accounts is that Syrian forces are going to Saudi territory, not into Yemen. On 5 November Riyadh and Damascus signed a defence cooperation agreement and 403 dollarsmillion in loans, and Syrian officials describe the military side as training and expertise.

Background. A Yemen truce collapsed in July. Since then the UN says more than 1,600 people have been killed and nearly 300,000 displaced, and some outlets put the toll above 1,700. The Houthis have hit Saudi airports, refineries and oil infrastructure. On 13 October they struck an Aramco office tower in Dhahran, and missile debris wounded four people in Riyadh. In September the East–West pipeline, the kingdom's roughly 7 million b/d route around Hormuz, was temporarily shut after attacks. On the ground, the Saudi-backed "Dawn of Yemen" operation has pushed into al-Jawf (the Huwaishan range and positions east of al-Hazm) and Marib's southern belt. These are government claims, including 340 Houthi fighters killed, and have not been independently verified. On 26 October Egypt's parliament authorised Sisi, by a two-thirds vote, to send forces to defend Saudi Arabia, limited to Saudi territory and only on request. Reuters reported no formal Saudi request at that point.

Analysis. This is an important regional realignment. Riyadh is building an Arab coalition to defend its homeland rather than to intervene in Yemen, and it is drawing on states it now underwrites financially. Post-Assad Syria repays Saudi patronage with manpower, which would have been unthinkable two years ago. Egypt offers deterrence but limits its exposure, mindful of its 1960s experience in Yemen. For Damascus the deal is attractive: cash, Gulf legitimacy and military professionalisation. The risk is that it pulls Syria into a sectarian proxy war with an Iranian ally while its own south and coast remain unstable. For the Houthis, hitting the Hormuz bypass undermines the Gulf's main hedge against Iranian pressure on the strait, which is why the pipeline attacks matter beyond the physical damage. The Houthi front also gives Tehran leverage while it says the US–Iran war is over. Iran's demand that hostilities end "on all fronts" almost certainly includes Yemen.

What to watch. Confirmation of the Syrian deployment's size and where it is based; any Saudi request to Egypt; Houthi claims for the Riyadh and Medina strikes; whether UN casualty figures are updated; possible strikes on Red Sea shipping in response to Egyptian involvement.

3. Israel: a new Knesset, Eisenkot's mandate, and US pressure on Gaza.

What happened. The 26th Knesset is sworn in today, 10 November. President Herzog gave the mandate to form a government to Gadi Eisenkot, leader of Yashar, on 8 November after he received 67 recommendations, including all 19 from the two Arab-led parties. Netanyahu received 46, and United Torah Judaism, a traditional ally, declined to recommend him. Eisenkot has 28 days, extendable by 14. He has reportedly agreed with Avigdor Liberman on a candidate for Knesset Speaker to replace Likud's Amir Ohana immediately, which would give the anti-Netanyahu bloc control of the legislative agenda. Netanyahu has proposed a "unity government" under his own leadership and accused the Arab parties of supporting Hamas.

On Gaza, Israel's Channel 12 reported on 9 November that Washington is pressing Netanyahu to hand civil administration to the Palestinian technocratic committee and has told him reconstruction will not begin without progress on disarming Hamas.

Background. In the 27 October election, Netanyahu's bloc won 53 seats, Eisenkot's opposition bloc 48, and Arab-led parties 19 (Joint List 11, Ra'am 8). No bloc reached 61. Netanyahu has called the election "stolen" and made unsubstantiated claims of Arab and foreign interference. Eisenkot says he will not bring the Arab parties into the coalition, though he accepted their recommendations. Manara Magazine summed up the arithmetic as "a majority against Netanyahu, but no coalition to replace him". In Gaza, the Board of Peace's July roadmap envisions Hamas police weapons going to the technocratic National Committee once it is inside Gaza, with heavy weapons and tunnels handled later under an International Stabilization Force. Hamas conditions disarmament on Israel ending strikes and withdrawing, and Israel is not a signatory. Board of Peace envoy Nickolay Mladenov demanded in October that Israel admit the committee and the ISF.

Analysis. Two clocks are now running together. The first is Eisenkot's 28 days. He needs either a Haredi party or tacit outside support from the Arab parties, and each option has costs: the first over conscription, the second because of the political stigma the Israeli right has attached to it since 2021. The second clock is Washington's. A lame-duck Trump who has declared the Iran war over wants a Gaza achievement, and Netanyahu's weakness gives him an opening. Netanyahu's incentive is to stall Gaza implementation while he fights for his political survival, and to present any concession on the technocratic committee as something forced on Israel by an Eisenkot-Arab "Hamas" coalition. The fight over the Speaker matters more than it looks: whoever controls the Knesset agenda controls dissolution bills, the timing of a possible new election, and early legislation on the judiciary and the October 7 inquiry.

What to watch. Today's Speaker vote; whether UTJ or Shas open talks with Eisenkot; any first entry of technocratic committee members into Gaza; the ISF's initial deployment, which Israeli media say was approved at around 250 foreign troops.

4. Gaza's first winter storm: tens of thousands of flooded tents and a dispute over aid.

What happened. Heavy rains over the weekend of 7 to 8 November flooded about 70,000 tents on the first day, according to Gaza's (Hamas-run) government media office. The civil defence spokesperson reported more than 1,500 overnight calls for help. Haaretz, citing UNICEF, reported that floodwater entered the tents of over 19,000 displaced families. The difference from the 70,000 figure reflects different units of count. One report says a rain-soaked sand embankment south of Deir al-Balah collapsed onto tents and killed five people, three of them children. That account could not be independently confirmed in this run. OCHA says aid groups are still blocked from bringing in vacuum trucks, tents, tarpaulins and winter clothing. After Hamas blamed Israel, the IDF said it was allowing tents and winter supplies in.

Background and analysis. This is the second winter since the October 2025 ceasefire, and the reconstruction promised in the Board of Peace plan has not started. Israel and the Board both make rebuilding conditional on Hamas's disarmament. Aid groups said in early November that about 260,000 families (almost 1.5 million people) were vulnerable going into winter. Israeli fire continues around the "Yellow Line", which leaves more than 60% of the Strip under Israeli control. The latest per-incident reports found date from late October. The political point is that "no reconstruction before disarmament" has become humanitarian leverage in practice. Washington's push for the technocratic committee is partly an attempt to separate shelter and basic services from the security sequence.

What to watch. More rain is forecast across the Levant this week. Watch whether the entry of "dual-use" items such as vacuum trucks and heavy equipment is approved, and whether OCHA reports new deaths from exposure or collapsing structures.

5. Sudan: the army arrests Ahmed Haroun as it advances in Kordofan.

What happened. Sudan's army detained Ahmed Haroun, chairman of the former ruling National Congress Party, who is wanted by the ICC for alleged crimes in Darfur, two days after his first public appearance since the war began: a four-hour podcast interview. Two NCP officials confirmed the arrest to Reuters. The party condemned it as "an arbitrary use of power", and a security official said he was held "based on legal procedures". Members of the civilian Somoud coalition allege Haroun had been living in Sudan under Burhan's "knowledge, protection and supervision". On the front, the army and allied forces took Ayal Bakhit on the West–North Kordofan boundary on 8 November and said they shot down a drone east of Wad Banda. Around El Obeid, the army and the RSF dispute control of several villages.

Analysis. Haroun's arrest shows the strain in Burhan's wartime coalition with the Islamist networks of the old regime, which have supplied fighters and mobilisation since 2023. Three motives are plausible and could overlap. Burhan may want to curb an Islamist current that has grown more confident. He may want to show Washington, Cairo and the Gulf, especially an Abu Dhabi that labels the SAF "Islamist", that the army is not an NCP front. And he may be keeping the option of an ICC handover as a bargaining chip. Whatever the motive, the move risks tension with the mustanfareen (mobilised fighters) just as the army needs manpower in Kordofan, now the war's centre of gravity, a year after El Fasher fell to the RSF.

What to watch. Whether the ICC requests Haroun's transfer; reactions from Islamist brigades such as al-Baraa bin Malik; the fighting around El Obeid.

6. Western Sahara after MINURSO: liquidation begins and the frontier tightens.

What happened. After the Security Council failed to renew its mandate, MINURSO expired on 31 October and liquidation began on 1 November. Guterres has appointed Alexander Ivanko, the former Special Representative, to lead the liquidation team. Rabat has told its MINURSO coordination office to facilitate the withdrawal. Polisario denounced the non-renewal. Mauritanian prefects in Chami and Tmeimichatt have told residents to stay away from the border zone.

Analysis. The end of the UN's only physical presence east and west of the berm is a quiet but major change. For Rabat it is a victory: the "referendum" mission that symbolised an unresolved decolonisation question is gone. The UN insists self-determination remains in force, and legal scholars are questioning the consequences. The practical risk is that ceasefire violations along the berm now go unmonitored, which raises the chance of escalation in a low-level conflict that resumed in 2020, with Mauritania exposed along its northern frontier.

Context: Thirteen months after the ceasefire, Gaza is stuck between a security sequence (disarmament first) and a humanitarian emergency (shelter now). The Israeli coalition outcome will largely decide which comes first.

Lebanon.

International.

The US expelled Lebanon's Detroit consul-general over pro-Hezbollah posts.

Beirut is waiting to see how the US and Israeli elections affect the stalled US-brokered direct talks with Israel, which had been expected to stall until after the Israeli vote.

Local.

Israeli strikes and shelling hit Qantara, Kfar Tebnit and Markaba, with injuries reported. The Health Ministry toll stands at 4,390 dead since 2 March.

The Lebanese army arrested six wanted individuals, including Syrians, in a Hezbollah stronghold in the east.

Context: The June framework deal has not ended Israel's "security zone" campaign. Lebanon's state is negotiating from weakness while Israel builds permanent infrastructure in the south.

Syria.

International.

Reports that Syrian troops are deploying to Saudi Arabia (600 to 3,000) follow a 5 November defence pact and 403 million dollars in loans. Damascus denies a deployment into Yemen.

Safadi visited Damascus, and Jordan and Syria signed 10 MoUs and a cooperation agreement.

Local.

The People's Assembly passed a law opening administrative decisions to judicial review, and al-Sharaa created 11 new faculties (SANA, state).

A cold-weather warning was issued, and a Chinese contract for smart meters was signed.

Context: Al-Sharaa's Syria is integrating into the Saudi-led Arab order, offering manpower in return for money and recognition, while its southern border remains a security problem shared with Jordan.

Jordan.

International.

Safadi's Damascus visit and the coordination council's agreements consolidate a pragmatic partnership with the new Syria, while a joint probe examines Jordan's deadly October airstrikes on Daraa and Suwayda.

King Abdullah returned from an official visit to Kazakhstan, part of Amman's search for partners beyond the region.

Local.

Little domestic news was found in the window. The latest notable item was the King opening SOFEX 2026 in Aqaba in late October.

Context: Amman's priority is a stable southern Syria (drugs, IS, Suwayda), which it pursues through both air power and a dense web of economic agreements with Damascus.

Iraq (incl. Kurdistan Region)

International.

Baghdad says it has understandings with Washington allowing oil exports through Hormuz via a lane near Oman, and is negotiating a higher OPEC quota.

Task Force Iron Gate's focus on Iran's land borders directly affects Iraq, Iran's largest overland trade partner. Expect US pressure on border crossings and dollar flows.

Local.

A 10 million b/d target by 2032, including about 1 million b/d from the KRG.

KRG crisis: the PUK has quit the government and threatens parallel institutions, and Masoud Barzani called for dialogue on 9 November. PMs al-Zaidi and Masrour Barzani discussed oil and the 2027 budget.

Context: Iraq is squeezed between US sanctions enforcement and Iranian dependence, while the Kurdistan Region risks a return to the Erbil–Sulaymaniyah split of the 1990s.

Iran.

International.

Trump says the war is "over" but keeps the blockade, and Iron Gate targets land and Caspian trade. Rezaei demands US "surrender" to seven conditions, and Araghchi says there have been no talks since the midterms.

China's Wang Yi met Bagheri Kani on 2 November. Russia's role remains limited.

Local.

Pezeshkian says Mojtaba Khamenei ordered an end to "no war, no peace". The leader has not been seen since March.

Two protesters were sentenced to death on 5 November (HRANA), and the execution campaign continues. NCRI-linked outlets report more but are an opposition source.

Context: A blockaded, leadership-opaque Islamic Republic is betting that US domestic politics will force concessions before economic attrition forces its own.

Turkey.

International.

COP31 runs 9 to 20 November in Antalya, with the leaders' segment on 11 to 12 November. Lula arrives today, and Bangladesh's PM meets Erdoğan in Antalya.

Erdoğan is in Shusha for Azerbaijan's Victory Day, and Ankara reiterates its offer to mediate between the US and Iran.

Local.

After the 9 November cabinet meeting, Erdoğan framed "Terror-Free Türkiye" as an economic leap. The recently passed PKK disbandment law excludes serious crimes and does not free Öcalan.

The İmamoğlu mega-trial (407 defendants) continues in the background.

Context: Ankara is converting the regional upheaval into convening power (COP31, offers of mediation) while combining Kurdish reconciliation with continued pressure on the CHP opposition at home.

Saudi Arabia.

International.

The kingdom is building an Arab defensive coalition, with Syrian troops reported and Egyptian authorisation in place, and gathering international condemnation of the Houthis, including from Canada's Carney.

Riyadh is reportedly among the Gulf voices urging Washington to keep economic pressure on Iran without renewed war.

Local.

Missiles were intercepted over Riyadh and the south and a drone over the Eastern Province. The East–West pipeline caught fire near Medina, with flows reportedly unaffected.

On 4 November MBS received Bahrain's national security adviser in Riyadh, showing close GCC security coordination.

Context: Vision 2030's promise of a safe investment destination is being tested by sustained missile fire on the capital. Riyadh's answer is deterrence through coalition rather than a ground war in Yemen.

United Arab Emirates.

International.

The UAE strongly condemned the Houthi attempt to strike Saudi Arabia.

Khaleej Times headlines a cumulative figure of 3,617 aerial threats intercepted during the regional conflict. This could not be checked against a ministry statement in this run; the last confirmed ministry total, from spring, was about 2,900.

Local.

ADIPEC 2026 is under way in Abu Dhabi.

New fines of up to Dh750,000 for violations relating to non-Muslim places of worship; a five-car limit for residents has been postponed after feedback.

Context: Having left OPEC this year and absorbed thousands of Iranian projectiles, Abu Dhabi favours keeping pressure on Tehran while presenting itself as an energy and investment hub.

Qatar.

International.

PM/FM Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman met Trump at the White House. Trump then declared the Iran war "effectively over". Doha is the channel for Iran's seven conditions.

Qatar is also a guarantor of the Gaza disarmament roadmap alongside Egypt, Turkey and the US.

Local.

No significant domestic news found in the window. Doha's agenda is dominated by mediation.

Context: Qatar has turned its mediation capital into central access to the White House, though earlier reporting described Qatari frustration with both sides.

Kuwait.

International.

Kuwait condemned the Houthi attacks on Riyadh.

Decree 176/2026 approved a labour-recruitment agreement with Tajikistan, part of diversifying sources of migrant labour.

Local.

Military and security chiefs met on preparedness.

The Official Gazette published Decree 175/2026 repealing an earlier decree.

Context: Under Emir Mishal, with parliament suspended, governance is by decree. Kuwait has been in the Iranian line of fire (CENTCOM reported missiles fired toward Kuwait in early November).

Bahrain.

International.

Bahrain's NSA Sheikh Nasser bin Hamad met MBS in Riyadh on 4 November.

A CIA assessment reported in May placed Bahrain, with the UAE, among the Gulf states most supportive of continued pressure on Iran.

Local.

Parliamentary elections are expected around 30 November. The date is not yet confirmed.

Context: Nothing major in the window. Watch the elections as a test of the regime's management of Shia opposition sentiment in wartime.

Oman.

International.

No new Omani initiative in the window. Iraq's reported export lane near Oman shows Muscat's coastline is now part of the Hormuz workaround.

The latest major development was the summer Oman–Iran talks on a temporary Hormuz shipping corridor. Muscat opposes transit fees.

Local.

No significant domestic news found.

Context: Muscat's role as Washington's preferred back channel has been taken over by Doha, and its focus is now on technical arrangements for navigation.

Yemen.

International.

The Houthis are escalating against Saudi Arabia while Riyadh assembles Syrian and, potentially, Egyptian support.

UN figures: 1,600+ dead and about 300,000 displaced since July.

Local.

The government says it advanced in al-Jawf and Marib under "Dawn of Yemen" as Houthi border attacks resumed.

Government claims of hundreds of Houthi fighters killed are unverified.

Context: Yemen has returned to full-scale war, now tied to the US–Iran confrontation. Ending it will probably require a package deal that includes Tehran.

France: Khaled Haftar's talks with French military leadership show Paris's continued engagement with eastern Libya.

China: Dialogue-only rhetoric with Iran; commercial presence in Syria (WASION smart meters).

India: No significant Indian move in the region was found in the window.
