Here is your Middle East briefing for Saturday, 28 November.

Mines in Hormuz, deaths in Kuwait, Baghdad moves on Erbil: the Iran war turns into attrition.

This week (Saturday 21 to Saturday 28 November 2026) made the US–Iran war look less like a frozen conflict and more like a war of attrition at sea. Washington disabled another blockade-runner off Bandar Abbas, the IRGC claimed a string of mine strikes on tankers using "unauthorised" routes through Hormuz, and Donald Trump said the war would end "whenever I want it to be" while hinting at new strikes after the new year. The violence spreading across the Gulf now reaches Europeans directly: two Italian Rheinmetall technicians were killed at a Kuwaiti air-defence site on 26 November, which Rome and Kuwait blame on Iranian drones and Tehran denies. Saudi Arabia's second front got worse, with Houthi strikes on a Riyadh tower, Ras Tanura and the Jazan refinery, coalition airstrikes in Lahj and Hodeidah that killed dozens, and Turkey and Pakistan using their Mecca pact to rally to Riyadh. In Iraq, Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi got a Federal Supreme Court ruling and a Trump endorsement for taking federal control of Kurdistan's borders and oil, which brought thousands of Kurds into the streets. In Israel, Gadi Eisenkot's mandate counts down to 6 December, while the West Bank, Gaza and southern Lebanon show how far the Gaza "ceasefire" and the June Lebanon framework still are from holding.

Top stories of the week.

1. Hormuz becomes a minefield as the blockade grinds on and talks stall.

What happened. On Thursday 26 November (US Thanksgiving), CENTCOM said a US military aircraft struck the stern of the Palau-flagged bulk carrier Pacific Archer in the Gulf of Oman. The ship had left Bandar Abbas and "ignored repeated warnings". Its propulsion was disabled and no crew were hurt. Maritime Executive describes the vessel as Chinese-owned. Iran International (a London-based Persian outlet critical of the Islamic Republic) notes that it was the first ship CENTCOM reported disabling since Iranian officials publicly backed breaking the blockade the previous week. It was the sixth since the blockade resumed in July; the previous one was the cargo ship Huda on 7 November. By CENTCOM's count, 158 commercial vessels have complied with instructions and turned back. Al Jazeera puts it at least the 15th vessel disabled since the blockade first began in April.

Iran's response came at sea. Early in the week the IRGC Navy said three tankers had hit mines in the strait despite "extensive US military air cover and escort". CNBC reported this on 24 November alongside a widening of US Treasury sanctions, and The Hill and Arab News also covered it. On Friday 27 November the IRGC Navy said two tankers had tried to "secretly pass through an unauthorised route north of Ghanam Island": one exploded after striking a mine and the other turned back. The IRGC gave no ship names, casualties or damage, and the claim was carried by Iranian state outlets such as Mehr and relayed by Pakistan Today and Anadolu. None of these incidents has been independently confirmed, and the IRGC has warned that the whole "unauthorised route" is mined.

On the diplomatic side, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi told state media on 26 November that Qatari and Pakistani mediators are still relaying proposals between Tehran and Washington and that "communication has not been interrupted". He said Hormuz can reopen once Iran's demands are met: an end to the naval blockade, removal of unilateral sanctions and release of frozen assets. Iran's ambassador in Islamabad said messages are moving "more slowly" than in earlier rounds and pointed to the June memorandum of understanding as the framework. Speaking to deployed troops from Mar-a-Lago on Thanksgiving, Trump said the war would be over "sort of whenever I want it to be", accused Tehran of stalling, and said he was inclined to resume strikes after the new year if there is no deal. A Pentagon budget official has put the war's cost at 48.3 dollarsbillion as of 13 November.

Background. The war began on 28 February 2026 with US and Israeli strikes that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei. Under pressure from the IRGC, the Assembly of Experts elevated his son Mojtaba, who has rarely been seen in public since. A June US–Iran memorandum briefly reopened Hormuz traffic, but it broke down over freedom of navigation. Washington reinstated the blockade in July and has since built an "Economic Fury" sanctions campaign around Iran's attempts to charge tolls and "insurance" for transit. The current round of escalation began on 17 November, when US strikes on Qeshm Island were followed by Iranian drone attacks on Kuwait and Bahrain. On 19 November the Senate narrowly rejected a House-passed war powers resolution, its first test since Democrats won control of Congress in the midterms.

Analysis. Both sides believe time is on their side. Washington's view is that the blockade is choking Iran's economy: the Washington Post reported on 26 November that Tehran is rolling back bread and gasoline subsidies after nearly nine months of war, and the IMF projects inflation near 69 percent this year. Tehran's view is that mines and deniable strikes can make the blockade costly for everyone else, the Gulf monarchies above all, without giving Washington a clear casus belli. Mines are a useful tool for this because they hold the shipping lanes hostage while leaving attribution vague. Chatham House argued this month that Iran, unlike in 1988, believes it holds the upper hand and knows the Gulf states want the blockade lifted. Crisis Group's Hamidreza Azizi warns that the longer the economic squeeze lasts, the stronger Tehran's incentive to escalate. The mediators also matter. With Oman's neutrality damaged after Iran reportedly hit navigation systems on Oman's Didamar island earlier in November, Qatar and Pakistan are now the channel, and Pakistan sits in a defence pact with Saudi Arabia and Turkey at the same time. That makes Islamabad an unusually central actor in Gulf security.

What to watch. Whether the Pacific Archer's reported Chinese ownership draws a reaction from Beijing; any independent confirmation (from ship-tracking or insurers) of the IRGC's mine claims; Trump's "after the new year" deadline; and whether the Qatari–Pakistani track produces a revised version of the June MoU.

2. Two Italians killed in Kuwait: the war's cost now falls on Europe.

What happened. On 26 November two Italian technicians working for Rheinmetall Italia, the Italian subsidiary of the German defence group, were killed at what Kuwait called a "vital facility", an air-defence surveillance site. Italy's foreign minister said "several drones of Iranian origin targeted Kuwaiti military installations". Kuwait's army first attributed the deaths to falling missile debris, and later accounts described shrapnel from intercepted drones. An Iranian armed forces spokesman denied that any operation had been carried out in the area, Araghchi called the incident "suspicious", and Gulf News reported that Tehran offered to take part in an investigation. Syria's government condemned the drone attacks on Kuwait (SANA, state news agency).

Background. Iran had resumed attacks on Kuwait on 17 to 18 November after two relatively quiet months. Iran's regular army said it had targeted US Patriot batteries and launch platforms at Ali Al Salem Air Base. Italy has been one of the European states most exposed in the Gulf. It has a base in Kuwait that was hit earlier in the war, and Prime Minister Meloni pledged air-defence aid to Gulf states.

Analysis. European defence contractors are now part of how the Gulf defends itself, so European casualties are a predictable result of Iran targeting air-defence infrastructure in the Gulf. Rome attributed the attack quickly and Tehran gave a "debris" defence, which follows the familiar pattern of deniable escalation. Politically, the deaths make it harder for the EU to stay on the cautious line it has held since March, when it rejected regime change while backing the Gulf's right to self-defence. Expect more pressure for European air-defence deliveries and for snapback-style sanctions coordination, and more scrutiny in Italy and Germany of what their contractors are doing in a war zone.

What to watch. Whether Italy summons Iran's ambassador, as it did in March over Cyprus; whether Kuwait or the US publishes forensic attribution; and whether Iran keeps targeting Kuwait, which it has used as a proxy for US basing.

3. Saudi Arabia's southern war deepens: Riyadh, Ras Tanura and Jazan hit, Lahj and Hodeidah bombed.

What happened. On 23 November an airstrike on buses and trucks in Lahj province killed 26 people. Local officials told AFP the dead were 12 Houthi fighters and 14 civilians. Government forces blamed the Houthis, and the Saudi-led coalition did not comment. The Houthis' telecoms ministry said separate strikes on the same day killed 17 in Hodeidah, and the coalition hit Hodeidah several times between 20 and 25 November. On 24 November a drone hit a tower in Riyadh's financial district and injured at least 11 people. The GCC Secretary-General condemned the "treacherous attack" and Qatar called it "a flagrant violation of Saudi sovereignty". On 25 November the Houthis claimed strikes on the Ras Tanura refinery, the kingdom's largest, and on the Red Sea ports of Jeddah and Jazan. They called these retaliation for the Hodeidah strikes. The coalition said it intercepted four drones over Ras Tanura, and satellite images showed smoke, but Riyadh did not confirm damage. On 26 November the Saudi energy ministry said a "hostile projectile" hit Aramco's Jazan refinery, killing a Nepali worker, wounding several others and temporarily halting some operations. The coalition said it destroyed the launcher in Hodeidah. Saudi forces also intercepted two ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh. On 27 November, after a meeting in Islamabad, Pakistan and Turkey, Saudi Arabia's partners in the Mecca defence pact, denounced the "heinous" Houthi attacks. Mauritania also condemned the Riyadh attack.

Background. The truce between the Houthis and the Saudi-backed government collapsed in July 2026, and Yemen became an active front in the wider Iran war. Jazan was already hit in July and August. On 19 November a Houthi missile damaged the PIF tower, Riyadh's tallest building. The attacks have already forced the AFC to postpone the 2027 Asian Cup to December 2027–January 2028 and pushed other flagship events out of the kingdom. The Mecca Defence Alliance was signed on 7 August and contains a mutual-defence clause.

Analysis. The Houthis have shifted from symbolic strikes to targeting Saudi Arabia's economy: refineries, export terminals, Red Sea ports and the financial district that represents Vision 2030. The coalition's answer is a sustained air campaign on Hodeidah and the fronts, which brings the civilian-casualty problems of 2015 to 2022 back. The fiscal cost is real. The Q3 deficit was SR84.3 billion and the nine-month gap SR244.3 billion, against a full-year plan of SR165.4 billion. The Islamabad statement is mostly symbolic, since nobody expects Turkish or Pakistani troops in Yemen, but it shows Riyadh building a security network that does not depend on Washington.

What to watch. Whether Aramco reports lasting damage at Ras Tanura or Jazan; whether there is a ground push on Hodeidah; and whether any Mecca-pact partner moves from statements to deployments, which the October Riyadh meeting said could be "rapidly deployed".

4. Baghdad moves on Erbil: a court ruling, Kurdish protests and Trump's endorsement.

What happened. On 24 November Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi ordered federal control over the Kurdistan Regional Government's border crossings and over oilfields in four provinces outside the Region. On Wednesday 25 November the Federal Supreme Court, responding to a request from Zaidi, ruled that federal authorities can deploy along all international borders and oversee agencies at every crossing, including those in Kurdistan. The ruling requires coordination with regional and provincial governments on customs, two judges dissented, and it is final and binding. On Thursday 26 November thousands of Kurds protested. The same day Iraq closed two Kurdistan crossings, one with Syria and one with Iran. On 27 November Trump posted his support for Zaidi's efforts to "stop the smuggling of Iraqi oil, end corruption, and bring all weapons under the exclusive control of the state". An Uzbek outlet, Tafs, reports that the crisis has pushed the rival KDP and PUK closer together and that Baghdad later agreed to open-ended talks. I could not confirm this elsewhere.

Background. Zaidi, whose rise Trump has claimed credit for, has made "state authority" his political project. In mid-November he deployed the army toward Jurf al-Sakhar, the Kataib Hezbollah stronghold, and set a 20 November deadline for handover. Reuters later reported a compromise under which government forces would enter while the militia redeployed. Baghdad–Erbil relations were already strained over salaries and oil, and Khor Mor gas production had been halted, forcing Baghdad to approve LPG supplies to the Region. US troops were due to complete their withdrawal from federal Iraq by 30 September 2026.

Analysis. This is the most serious centralising push since the 2017 Kirkuk crisis, and the coalition behind it is new. Washington used to protect the KRG's autonomy and now endorses a Baghdad prime minister who is moving against both Iran-aligned militias and the Kurds, framed as anti-smuggling. For Trump, a strong Baghdad that squeezes militia revenue and oil smuggling to Iran fits the "maximum pressure" logic. The Kurds lose the US patron they relied on for three decades. The militias lose revenue streams. Turkey, which buys Kurdish oil and has its own PKK process to manage, has to recalibrate. Analysts quoted by Al Jazeera doubt the ruling can be implemented quickly. Federal forces still cannot easily deploy into Peshmerga-held territory.

What to watch. Whether federal units actually deploy to the Region's crossings beyond the two closed on 26 November; whether the KDP and PUK form a joint front; and how Ankara responds.

5. Israel's interregnum: coalition clock, settler violence and a strike on Hamas's leadership.

What happened. President Herzog has given Gadi Eisenkot until 6 December to form a government. Eisenkot's Yashar and Netanyahu's Likud held several rounds of "unity" talks, including a four-hour session on 22 November, and announced that further meetings would follow, with no date set. Sources told the Jerusalem Post that Netanyahu is considering offering to serve second in a rotation. Eisenkot meanwhile made progress with UTJ. Ynet reports a coalition agreement with Yair Golan's Democrats that gives them four ministries and a seat on the Judicial Selection Committee. On 22 November settlers attacked several villages around Ramallah, burning cars and spraying graffiti, and Eisenkot called it "Jewish terrorism". Before dawn on 27 November an Israeli strike on a Gaza City apartment targeted the family of Izzeldeen al-Hayya, the last surviving son of Hamas leader Khalil al-Hayya. Hamas said they survived, and separate strikes killed four or five Palestinians that day.

Background. In the 27 October election Netanyahu's bloc won 53 seats and Eisenkot's bloc 48. Likud remains the largest party with 27 seats, ahead of Yashar with 21 and Bennett's party with 12. Arab-led parties won a record 19 seats, which makes them indispensable for any non-Netanyahu majority. The Gaza ceasefire of 10 October 2025 still nominally holds. On 17 November the US-led Board of Peace warned the UN Security Council that it faces "imminent collapse": at least 1,600 Palestinians have been killed since it began, and Israeli tanks made their first ground move west of the Yellow Line on 18 November.

Analysis. Israeli politics is effectively frozen. That freezes Lebanon diplomacy too: Lebanese PM Nawaf Salam said formal talks with Israel are on hold because of the election. It does not freeze military operations, which continue in Gaza, Lebanon and Syria under a caretaker government. Eisenkot can only form a government by relying on Arab parties or by striking a deal with Likud, and each option shapes foreign policy differently. An Eisenkot-led government would probably be more open to the Board of Peace architecture and to Saudi-backed Palestinian governance formulas, but its parliamentary base would be fragile. Calling settler violence "Jewish terrorism" is also positioning: he is courting the centre, and possibly Washington and European capitals, while Netanyahu's camp attacks him for depending on Arab votes.

What to watch. Coalition agreements Eisenkot's bloc hopes to submit around 1 to 2 December; the 6 December mandate deadline (if it fails, Herzog can hand the mandate to someone else, and new elections must follow within 90 days if no one succeeds); and the Board of Peace's next briefing at the Security Council.

6. Turkey's double game: courting Haftar while the PKK process stalls.

What happened. On 25 November Erdoğan hosted Khalifa Haftar in Ankara, days after Tripoli's foreign minister met Hakan Fidan in Istanbul. The same day the Haftar-aligned parliament published the 2019 Turkey–Libya maritime memorandum in the official gazette, opening waters off eastern Libya to Turkish exploration. At home, Turkey's National Security Council met on 26 November and did not confirm that the PKK had laid down its arms. Without that confirmation the August framework law on returns and prisoner releases cannot be implemented. Erdoğan called the "Terror-Free Türkiye" process a "historic opportunity" on 25 November (Anadolu, state news agency).

Analysis. Ankara is trying to become the broker in two arenas where it used to be a party. In Libya it has moved from backing Tripoli militarily to courting both sides. US mediators are pushing a formula that would make Saddam Haftar chair of the Presidency Council, and Erdoğan plans a December visit that could bring Dbeibah and Haftar together. Egypt, which reopened its Tripoli embassy on 7 November after a 12-year closure partly to counter Turkish influence, and Greece both reject the maritime deal. On the Kurdish file, Ankara insists on verified disarmament before reform. That sequencing is being tested by Baghdad's move against Erbil, Turkey's main Kurdish partner, and by the SDF integration deadline in Syria.

What to watch. Party nominations, due by 30 November, to the 19-member parliamentary committee monitoring the law (the Good Party and the New Party say they will boycott it, per the ISDP bulletin); and Erdoğan's December trip to Libya.

Economy, energy and trade.

Qatar LNG: Exports reached their highest level since the war began. Ras Laffan averaged about 1.4 cargoes a day in November, up about 25 percent from October, although volumes remain around half of pre-war levels.

Iran: Subsidies on bread and gasoline are being rolled back, and emergency fuel cards were withdrawn province by province from 12 November (NCRI, MEK-affiliated opposition). Al Jazeera warns of winter gas shortages that could trigger blackouts.

US sanctions: On 24 November the Treasury widened its sanctions as the mine claims surfaced, continuing the "Economic Fury" campaign against Iran's Hormuz toll and "insurance" scheme.

Saudi fiscal strain: The nine-month 2026 deficit of SR244.3 billion already exceeds the full-year plan. A 2027 deficit of SR191 billion (3.6 percent of GDP) is forecast, and external debt reached SAR 638 billion at end-Q3.

Iraq oil: Zaidi's federalisation of Kurdish crossings and oilfields is presented as an anti-smuggling measure. It will reshape who controls the Region's export revenues.

Egypt: The IMF reached a staff-level deal on 19 November to unlock about 2.3 dollarsbillion (roughly 1.5 dollarsbillion from the final EFF review and 800 dollarsmillion from the RSF), with Board approval expected in December. Gas cuts to fertiliser and methanol plants deepened to 33 percent as LNG import costs rose, and fertiliser gas prices rose 50 percent.

Algeria: On 24 November Tebboune chaired a session on fertiliser production. Concentrated phosphate will reach the market in the first quarter of 2027, and the stated aim is to end fertiliser imports by 2028 (allAfrica/APS, state agency).

Syria: EgyptAir will resume flights to Syria in December 2026. Riyadh's 403 dollarsmillion loan package from 4 November is the first tranche of 1.5 dollarsbillion pledged.

Gulf aviation: Etihad extended its cancellations of Abu Dhabi–Riyadh and Abu Dhabi–Dammam flights through 30 November.

Humanitarian and human rights.

Gaza: At least 1,600 Palestinians have been killed since the October 2025 ceasefire, according to Gaza health authorities, and further strikes killed four or five people on 27 November. MEI's one-year review finds that technocratic governance, the stabilisation force and reconstruction "have not begun in any meaningful way".

West Bank: Settler attacks on 22 November (villages around Ramallah) and 26 to 27 November (homes of the Tubasi family in Jaloud, which the IDF called "a serious criminal act") come after UN figures showed attacks doubling from 647 in September to 1,369 in October. Yesh Din says 93.6 percent of investigations close without indictment.

Yemen: The 23 November strike in Lahj killed 14 civilians among 26 dead, according to local officials, and it is unclear who carried it out.

Sudan: More than 30 million people need aid and over 9 million are internally displaced. OCHA's nearly 3 dollarsbillion appeal is only about 40 percent funded, and UN agencies warned on 6 November that shelter stocks could run out before winter.

Iran: Repression continues. A UN Secretary-General report on 6 November found it had intensified, and HRANA documented at least 2,003 executions between October 2025 and October 2026 (HRANA, rights group).

Syria: Church leaders condemned attacks on Christians after kidnappings and an assault on a bishop. A 14 November car bomb in Jaramana that killed a father and daughter remains unexplained.

Kuwait/Gulf civilians: The Italian deaths and the Nepali worker killed at Jazan are a reminder that the migrant and expatriate workforces running Gulf infrastructure are taking the human cost of this war.

Society, culture and religion.

Sport and events in Saudi Arabia: The postponement of the Asian Cup, the Saudi International golf event and the Dakar Rally shows the war is undercutting Riyadh's soft-power and tourism strategy.

Jordan: At the opening of parliament the King promised to confront "terrorists" after gunmen killed Christians at a funeral service in a Karak church. The attack has shaken Jordan's self-image as a model of coexistence.

Syria's Christians: Church leaders publicly fear for the community's future, which tests the new authorities' commitment to minority protection.

Israel's Arab citizens: A record 19 Knesset seats for Arab-led parties has made Palestinian citizens of Israel pivotal to coalition-building, and Likud is campaigning on it.

Iran's daily life: Bread queues, fuel-card rationing and blackouts are now ordinary, and state media dispute the official framing of these as targeted measures.

UAE: National Day falls on 2 to 3 December. It will be the second marked under wartime alerts, and Gulf News's daily wartime advisories have become a standard part of residents' lives.

Palestine (Gaza and the West Bank)

International.

On 17 November the Board of Peace told the Security Council that the ceasefire faces "imminent collapse" without an International Stabilization Force, Israeli compliance and Hamas disarmament. It is due to return to the Council at the end of November.

Egypt and Qatar coordinated mediation in Cairo on 26 November.

Local.

Gaza: Hamas says the 27 November strike on the al-Hayya family's apartment was an assassination attempt. Hamas official Basem Naim said the family survived, with injuries.

West Bank: settlers attacked villages around Ramallah on 22 November and homes in Jaloud on 26 to 27 November. Palestinian outlets report attacks in five governorates over a single 24-hour period (PIC, Hamas-affiliated; The Cradle).

Context: A year into the Gaza plan, none of its main pillars has taken shape, and elections have been pushed to 2027. The West Bank is the fastest-deteriorating front.

Lebanon.

International.

France is preparing a Security Council resolution to extend UNIFIL's functions, and Salam says a proposal is underway to keep peacekeepers in the south.

The June 2026 US-brokered framework, which provides for phased Israeli withdrawal in exchange for Hezbollah disarmament, has stalled.

Local.

The IDF struck a Radwan Force training camp in Ain al-Tineh, West Bekaa, on 26 November, after hitting a former Hezbollah missile facility in Janta the previous week. Lebanon's state news agency reported strikes on Haris and Kfar Tebnit.

None of the disarmament pilot areas has yet been certified, and there is friction between the government and the army over pace (ITIC, Israeli intelligence-linked).

Context: Beirut is trying to disarm Hezbollah by consensus while Israel strikes at will. The process risks stalling permanently if UNIFIL's mandate lapses.

Syria.

International.

At an OPCW meeting in The Hague, Syria presented its efforts to deal with the Assad regime's chemical-weapons legacy.

Damascus condemned Iranian drone attacks on Kuwait (SANA, state). It is weighing whether to join the Saudi–Turkish–Pakistani defence pact after signing a defence cooperation agreement and 403 dollarsmillion in loans with Riyadh on 4 November.

Local.

An ISIS attack in Hasakah on 26 November killed two security personnel. Israeli incursions into Quneitra and Daraa continue, with more than 30 recorded in the first half of November.

The SDF integration due by year-end is incomplete. Power-sharing and local participation remain disputed.

Context: Two years after Assad, Sharaa's Syria has firmly joined the Saudi-led order. Its weak points are ISIS, Israeli pressure in the south and the place of minorities.

Jordan.

International.

King Abdullah met UK PM Andy Burnham in London on 26 November and received assurances of "full support".

Uzbek President Mirziyoyev visited Amman on 24 November, with 1.5 dollarsbillion in investment plans announced.

Local.

Water security is now framed as a sovereignty priority as a water-supply arrangement with Israel expires and a planned desalination project faces hurdles.

The Karak church attack remains the security backdrop.

Context: Amman is diversifying its partners and hedging against Israeli "expansionism", which the King calls the region's greatest threat.

Iraq (incl. Kurdistan Region)

International.

Trump endorsed Zaidi on 27 November, backing his campaign against oil smuggling and his aim to put all weapons under state control.

Local.

The Federal Supreme Court ruled on 25 November that Baghdad can deploy federal forces along all borders, including Kurdistan's. Kurds protested on 26 November, and two crossings with Syria and Iran were closed.

Kurdish parties are reportedly closing ranks. The KDP had already said it was ready to meet the PUK after an appeal from President Barzani.

The Jurf al-Sakhar standoff with Kataib Hezbollah appears headed for a negotiated redeployment involving army, federal police and PMF units.

Context: Zaidi is building a stronger centre against both the militias and the KRG, with US backing. It is the boldest state-building push since 2017, and it carries risks on both fronts.

Iran.

International.

The blockade continues (Pacific Archer was disabled on 26 November). The IRGC claims mine strikes in Hormuz, and Araghchi says Qatari and Pakistani mediation continues.

Tehran denies responsibility for the deaths in Kuwait and has offered to help investigate.

Local.

Subsidy rollbacks on bread and gasoline are deepening public frustration. Pezeshkian defended tighter controls by citing large-scale abuse of cheap energy and staples.

A winter gas shortfall threatens blackouts. Executions and security arrests remain at record levels.

Context: Nine months into the war, the Islamic Republic under Mojtaba Khamenei is betting that it can outlast the blockade. Domestic austerity is the main pressure point.

Turkey.

International.

Erdoğan hosted Haftar on 25 November. Ankara's partners in the Mecca pact, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, issued joint condemnation of the Houthi attacks on 27 November.

Local.

The National Security Council did not confirm PKK disarmament on 26 November. Parties must nominate members of the monitoring committee by 30 November, and two opposition parties are boycotting.

The central bank raised its inflation forecasts (29.5 percent for end-2026, 21 percent for 2027), citing the Iran war. November CPI is due in early December.

Context: Turkey is maximising its leverage as a regional broker abroad, from Libya to the Gulf, while at home it combines a Kurdish peace process with judicial pressure on the CHP.

Saudi Arabia.

International.

The kingdom is under sustained Houthi fire (Riyadh on 24 November, Ras Tanura, Jeddah and Jazan on 25 to 26 November) and received solidarity from the Mecca pact, the GCC, Qatar, the UAE, Bahrain, Syria and Mauritania.

Local.

The Jazan refinery partially halted after the 26 November strike killed a worker.

The 2026 fiscal deficit is running far above plan. AGSI estimates it could approach SR300 billion, about 6 percent of GDP.

Context: Vision 2030's events-led diversification is colliding with a two-front war. Riyadh is responding by building alliances (Pakistan, Turkey, Syria) rather than relying only on Washington.

United Arab Emirates.

International.

No major UAE-specific event this week. The UAE joined condemnations of the Houthi attacks on Riyadh. Presidential adviser Anwar Gargash's line remains that the UAE wants diplomacy but is "braced for the worst".

Local.

Etihad's Riyadh and Dammam cancellations run through 30 November. National Day is on 2 to 3 December.

Energy Minister Suhail al-Mazrouei said in early November that the UAE is better prepared for whatever follows and that exports at times outperformed pre-war levels.

Context: The war ended Abu Dhabi's "cautious de-escalation" with Tehran. Its strategy now is resilience through pipelines that bypass Hormuz and quiet diplomacy.

Qatar.

International.

Qatar is co-mediating the US–Iran track with Pakistan. Emir Tamim met Sisi in Cairo on 26 November on Gaza.

Local.

LNG exports hit a wartime high in November, averaging 1.4 cargoes a day from Ras Laffan, but remain well below pre-war volumes, with two trains damaged long-term.

Context: Doha has once again made itself an indispensable intermediary. Its gas economy is recovering slowly and depends on how much Hormuz traffic gets through.

Kuwait.

International.

The deaths of two Italian Rheinmetall technicians on 26 November made Kuwait the week's flashpoint between Europe and Iran.

Local.

Kuwait's defence ministry called the site a "vital facility" and blamed Iran. Iranian attacks resumed on 17 November after two months of relative calm. The Amiri Diwan reported the Amir receiving the Crown Prince, PM and ministers on 22 November.

Context: Because Kuwait hosts major US bases, it is Iran's preferred target for signalling to Washington.

Bahrain.

International.

Bahrain was not specifically targeted this week as far as I found. Between 13 and 18 November it reported four Iranian aerial attacks in about six days, with Iran claiming a strike on a US radar.

Local.

Manama continues to press for full implementation of UNSC Resolution 2817 condemning Iran's attacks, and joined condemnation of the Houthi strikes on Riyadh.

Context: As host of the US Fifth Fleet, Bahrain remains exposed whenever the US and Iran escalate.

Oman.

International.

No major Oman-specific event found for this week. Its mediation role has been eclipsed by Qatar and Pakistan since Araghchi's 17 November Muscat visit produced no Hormuz agreement.

Local.

No major dated domestic development found in Times of Oman or ONA for 21 to 28 November.

Context: The reported Iranian attack on navigation systems on Oman's Didamar island (11 November) has strained Muscat's traditional neutrality.

Yemen.

International.

The Houthis widened their campaign against Saudi energy and port infrastructure on 24 to 26 November.

Local.

23 November airstrikes killed 26 in Lahj and 17 in Hodeidah. The coalition struck Hodeidah several times between 20 and 25 November.

Government forces keep publishing large operation tallies against the Houthis, such as 715 operations with 144 claimed killed or wounded in one report. These figures are self-reported and unverified.

Context: Since the truce collapsed in July, Yemen has become the Iran war's southern front, and civilians in Hodeidah and on the frontlines are paying for it.
