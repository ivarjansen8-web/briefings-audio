Here is your US briefing for Wednesday, 18 November.

Trump heads to Xi-Putin summit as FDA pick collapses and Nvidia feud splits cabinet.

Compiled from sources dated 15 to 18 November 2026. Items marked (unconfirmed) are single-source or early reports.

1. Top stories.

Trump is in Asia for a Thursday 19 November trilateral with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen, after arriving in the Philippines. The White House says the agenda is nuclear arms control and the Iran war, while Trump himself says he will "talk about my favorite subject: Ukraine", so aides and the president are sending mixed signals.

The White House pulled Heidi Overton's FDA nomination on 17 November, a day before a committee vote she was set to lose. Republicans hold only a one-vote edge on the HELP panel, and Chairman Bill Cassidy disputed the White House's account that she lacked the votes, pointing instead to abortion-related disagreements; acting commissioner is Kyle Diamantas.

A Bessent-Lutnick feud over Nvidia and China export controls has spilled into public view. Bessent called for a "dramatic shakeup" at Nvidia after CEO Jensen Huang's resignation and rebuked Commerce for not blacklisting Megaspeed; Trump denies he will fire Lutnick and is publicly backing Huang's return.

The US has massed three carriers and about 53,000 troops in the Gulf while Trump says restarting the Iran war is "highly unlikely" but possible. Trump has also said "we don't need a deal with Iran" as the naval blockade continues (unconfirmed on the troop figures: single Indian outlet).

2. White House and the administration.

Trump is pressing ahead with White House grounds renovations costing millions despite the GOP's midterm losses. Critics frame it as tone-deaf after what The New Republic called an "election bloodbath"; the Washington Post carries the factual account.

Post-midterm feuding is hitting the cabinet, with officials reportedly trying to push colleagues "under the bus". The Bessent-Lutnick row is the most visible case, and Trump brushed it off by saying one wants to be friendly with China and the other wants a "blowup".

Trump tightened AI-chip export rules to close a China loophole, as reported 16 November. The rule landed amid the Nvidia scandal and signals that the Treasury hawks have the upper hand for now.

An armed man was arrested near the White House for allegedly threatening Trump (unconfirmed: single aggregated report). Details on charges were not available in the sources found.

3. Congress (House, Senate, key votes, investigations)

The Senate confirmed Anna St. John to the Fifth Circuit 52 to 46 on 17 November. The vote came while the chamber continued work on H.R. 2347, the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act; the official roll call was not independently checked.

The Senate advanced the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act again on 16 November, 70 to 27, after a 73 to 25 vote on 9 November. Amendment votes precede final passage, and 5 Calls notes several "yes" voters remain uncommitted; the House must still act.

White House counsel Will Scharf warned incoming Oversight chair Robert Garcia that aggressive probes could "cross the line into blatant criminality". Garcia called it intimidation, and legal experts say the letter carries little practical force; Democrats take control in January.

The House voted 211 to 204 on 10 November to send Thomas Massie's Hegseth impeachment articles to Judiciary, effectively burying them. Eleven Democrats were absent, and Democrats say they will revive the push in January.

The House is back in session on 18 November at 10:00 after a quiet 17 November.

4. Courts and the Supreme Court.

The Supreme Court agreed on 13 November to hear Mullin v. RAICES, the challenge to Trump's border asylum suspension. Two lower courts had already ruled the policy illegal, so this is a major immigration test for the Court's current term.

The Court turned away the administration's petition in the Ninth Circuit DEI research-funding case on 16 November, with no noted dissent. It also declined a Second Amendment challenge to Maryland's "sensitive places" gun limits.

The Court is weighing a dispute over mandatory no-bond detention of long-term unlawful residents, on which eleven of twelve circuits have ruled. Other pending items include transgender servicemembers, the E. Jean Carroll award and acting US attorneys; December arguments begin 30 November.

Prosecutors sought LinkedIn data on New York Times reporters in a leak probe, per a filing unsealed 16 November. An appeals court had already blocked the order, which raises press-freedom concerns.

Judge Chutkan granted a TRO on 13 November in the voter-list litigation brought by DC and Virginia.

5. Parties, elections and polls.

Trump's approval sits at roughly 32 to 38% after the 3 November midterms. Reuters/Ipsos (to 16 November) has 32% approve and 66% disapprove, Economist/YouGov 36/62, and averages near 36 to 38%.

Republican approval of Trump has slipped to 74% in CNN polling, down from 88% in February 2025. The erosion within his own base is the main driver of the Overton and other Senate GOP rebellions.

Republicans reportedly won fewer than half of Trump's Gen Z voters in the midterms (J.L. Partners). This is a partisan-leaning pollster's finding and should be read with caution.

Democrats are positioning to take House committee gavels in January, including Garcia at Oversight and Bennie Thompson at Homeland Security. Both have vowed investigations of the administration.

6. Foreign policy (Middle East focus)

The US-Iran ceasefire dating from 7 April holds uneasily while a US naval blockade and the new CENTCOM Task Force Iron Gate squeeze Iranian trade. Iron Gate, set up 7 November, targets land and Caspian routes; Tehran calls it "so-called" and CBS reports Iranian and Russian officials discussing workarounds.

Hormuz traffic is reportedly about 20% of pre-war levels, with Iran saying two tankers hit mines. Iran says the strait stays closed until the US makes concessions, which keeps energy prices and inflation elevated.

Rubio says there is "no need" to restart combat operations in Iran. That contrasts with Trump's warning that he could strike, and with Israeli suggestions that the mission is "not complete".

Israel's defence minister says Israel will keep its security zones in Gaza, Lebanon and Syria. Mossad chief Roman Gofman was in Washington last week meeting CIA Director Ratcliffe on Iran.

The Shenzhen trilateral with Xi and Putin is the week's key diplomatic event, alongside APEC. Analysts expect no breakthrough; a possible Trump-Kim meeting was also floated.

7. Defence and the Pentagon.

Hegseth visits Bogota on 18 November to hand over Colombia's first C-130 Hercules, with two more reportedly to follow. Regional media say Venezuela may follow, which Reuters could not confirm; he was at SOUTHCOM in Miami on 17 November.

The Pentagon failed its ninth consecutive financial audit, per the Inspector General. Hegseth has set 2028 for a clean opinion, while the Daily Caller (right-leaning) highlights a 1.5 dollarstrillion budget request and RedState spins the same news as a milestone.

SOCOM gave a 5.4 dollarsmillion no-bid award to a contractor weeks before it settled fraud claims for 7.75 dollarsmillion. SOCOM says the firm was the only supplier able to meet the requirement.

The Pentagon called former spokesman John Ullyot a "crazy ex" after he alleged a makeup studio push tied to Hegseth's wife. CBS reported early proposals cost about 40,000 dollarsbefore being scaled back.

8. Intelligence and OSINT.

A senior US Army officer, reportedly Lt. Col. Brandon Tyner, was arrested in England on 10 November on suspicion of aiding Russian intelligence and is now in US military custody. FBI Director Patel called it "of the utmost concern", and a NATO official said it is unclear what he could access; the name comes from a WSJ report (unconfirmed elsewhere).

House Democrats, led by Intelligence ranking member Jim Himes, plan to investigate Trump's handling of the spy agencies, including contractor ties and covert efforts overseas. The probes begin once Democrats take the majority in January.

Jay Clayton is described as director of national intelligence after Tulsi Gabbard's June resignation (unconfirmed: label from a single Fox Business item). He said there is no coordinated cyberattack behind widespread tech outages, with FBI and NSA directing systems offline for fixes.

OSINT (open-source, unverified): Bellingcat reports, using Planet Labs imagery, that at least 46 of 54 villages inside the IDF "Yellow Line" in southern Lebanon are heavily damaged or flattened. Planet recently restricted imagery in the region, limiting replication.

OSINT (open-source, unverified): Bellingcat says public online maps expose sensitive details of US bases in the Middle East targeted by Iran. ISW's 15 November assessment saw no evidence of Russian advances north of Sumy despite milblogger claims.

9. Economy, trade and tariffs.

US import prices rose 1.1% in October, above the 0.7% forecast, after a similar rise in September. The figure excludes tariffs and points to broadening inflation beyond energy.

Markets price a 25 basis point Fed hike in December and another in January, from a 3.75 to 4.00% range. St. Louis Fed's Alberto Musalem says "several" hikes are likely by spring 2027.

NY Fed's John Williams says more hikes may be needed but a large step is not necessary, and that tariff effects have largely been absorbed (unconfirmed: secondary aggregator).

Retail sales rose 0.2% in October and the 10-year Treasury yield was about 5.11%. The yield level is notable for borrowing costs and the deficit.

A New York Fed analysis estimates the 2025 tariffs added 2.9 points to goods-price inflation by February 2026.

10. Immigration and homeland security.

DHS says ICE carried out a record 483,200 deportations and more than 491,000 arrests in FY2026. The prior deportation record was 442,640, and DHS cites nearly 1.2 million removals since January 2025.

Syracuse's TRAC disputes the claim, saying case-level removals fell, with the gap down to counting voluntary departures. Outlets differ here, and the DHS framing is stronger in pro-administration media.

Incoming Homeland Security chair Bennie Thompson vows to investigate "weaponising DHS against Americans".

The Supreme Court's asylum and detention cases will shape enforcement through 2027.
