Saturday, 21 November 2026 — Daily Government Briefing.

United Nations.

The Security Council, under Latvia's November presidency, is again focused on Ukraine. France called for a Council meeting on Friday after a wave of Russian missile and drone strikes killed eight people across Ukraine and damaged the Austrian and Dutch embassies in Kyiv. Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot said "Russia will have to answer for this." The strikes followed Putin's vow of a "painful" response to Ukraine's attack on the Crimea bridge. A high-level Council meeting on the war, chaired by Latvian President Edgars Rinkēvičs, was already set for Monday, 23 November. Earlier this month the Council renewed mandates without opposition: on 12 November it adopted the Central African Republic (MINUSCA) and Yemen texts 15 to 0, and on 13 November the Sudan/South Sudan (UNISFA) resolution 13 to 0, with China and Russia abstaining. Separately, the UN's International Court of Justice found Myanmar responsible for genocide against the Rohingya. The EU welcomed the ruling and Myanmar's government called it "unfair."

European Union.

On Ukraine, the Council mobilised another 1 eurosbillion in military aid through the European Peace Facility. Commission President von der Leyen joined about 40 delegations at Thursday's Coalition of the Willing summit in Berlin. There, Europe confirmed a joint negotiating team made up of France, Germany, the UK, Italy and Poland, plus the EU Council and Commission and NATO, to claim a seat in US–Russia talks. Merz wants all 27 member states to help cover Ukraine's 70 eurosbillion funding gap for next year, but Bulgaria, Hungary, the Czech Republic and Slovakia are reportedly opting out of the next round. On legislation, Parliament and Council reached a deal revising the EU's sustainability-disclosure rules for the financial sector. The Commission partially approved Hungary's request for post-Covid recovery funds. New rules on e-declaration of posted workers and on cross-border protection of vulnerable adults were also cleared. Trade ministers met in Brussels on Friday, and the Irish presidency plans a first "safety by design" compromise on the Kids Act in December.

US House of Representatives.

The House is out for Thanksgiving and returns Monday, 30 November, after a lame-duck week undermined by poor attendance. On Wednesday, GOP leaders pulled a procedural rule vote on two labor-related bills because 14 Republicans were missing. Six of them had lost their re-election bids. Thursday's session ran without 32 members (21 Republicans, 10 Democrats and one independent), and Friday's planned votes were cancelled. On Friday, Speaker Mike Johnson sent a "Dear Colleague" letter: "barring any serious medical issue, we need every Republican to be here." About 13 legislative days remain to pass a funding bill before the 11 December shutdown deadline, the annual defense authorization (NDAA), a farm bill, and the Senate-passed permitting overhaul. Judiciary Chairman Jim Jordan is set to lead the GOP as minority leader in the next Congress.

US Senate.

The week's main result was Thursday night's 62 to 33 passage of the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA, S. 5653). It is a roughly 500-page permitting overhaul for pipelines, transmission lines, mining and data centers, written by Sens. Capito, Heinrich, Lee and Whitehouse and negotiated with the White House. Fourteen Democrats and one independent voted yes. Critics, including Sen. Merkley and tribal advocates, object to weaker National Historic Preservation Act consultation. The bill now goes to the House, and its future there is uncertain. On Friday the Senate kept confirming judges: it voted to confirm Richard Bennett (S.D. Texas) and to invoke cloture on Courtney Coker (N.D. Texas), with cloture also filed on Kyle Reardon (D. Alaska) and Daniel Ballou (E.D. Ky.). Looking to January, Senate Democrats will hold a 56 to 44 majority and pick their leader by secret ballot on 1 December. A Washington Post count shows 16 senators backing Chuck Schumer, 6 backing Chris Van Hollen and 30 uncommitted.

US Supreme Court.

The Court is between argument sittings. The justices met in private conference on Friday, 20 November, and the resulting orders are expected Monday. Petitions up for consideration include disputes over transgender servicemembers, E. Jean Carroll's jury award against Trump, and the legality of acting US attorneys. The 16 November order list turned away DEI-related cases and a Second Amendment challenge to gun bans in "sensitive places." The emergency docket is still busy with administration requests, including a renewed bid to proceed with the White House ballroom project and a climate-grant dispute. After Thanksgiving, arguments resume on 30 November with Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia and Wassily (green cards after asylum ends). The sitting also includes Viramontes v. Cook County, Jouppi v. Alaska and DHS v. D.V.D. on third-country removals.

White House.

Back from Asia, President Trump gave a long press conference on Friday. He accused Iran of negotiating in bad faith, threatened military action over a rumored "dirty bomb" plot against Washington, and claimed the US Navy blockade gives America "total control" of the Strait of Hormuz. He announced plans to penalize US companies that build AI data centers abroad. He played down a wave of banking, tech and service outages as "separate events" and said he would seek 50 dollarsbillion from Congress for a new air-traffic-control system. He also defended his relationship with Putin and lamented Republican midterm losses. All of this followed Thursday's first-ever US–China–Russia leaders' meeting in Shenzhen at APEC: Trump spent 42 minutes with Putin, about an hour with Xi, and 90 minutes in the three-way session. Trump also dialed into the Berlin Coalition of the Willing meeting. During the trip he drew attention for calling Philippine President Marcos "prime minister" and claiming Manila would buy "100%" of its farm and energy goods from the US, which Marcos later clarified. Today's public schedule lists only executive time (8:00 a.m. ET) and an in-town pool call (9:00 a.m. ET).

International Politics Analysis.

The Shenzhen trilateral has reshaped Ukraine diplomacy, and Europe is pushing to get back into it. The Trump–Xi–Putin declaration largely sidestepped Ukraine. After the summit, Putin invited European leaders to Moscow. On Friday, an adviser to Macron said Europe would go if Russia confirms Europeans have a "full role" in the next round of talks with the US and Ukraine. In Berlin, envoy Jared Kushner said Trump was open to giving Ukraine a security guarantee similar to NATO's Article 5. France replied that such a guarantee would amount to NATO membership. Meanwhile, events on the ground are raising tensions. During Friday's overnight barrage, two Russian cruise missiles and a jet-powered drone came down in southeastern Poland, about 75 km from an unprecedented European exercise rehearsing a postwar mission in Ukraine. Defense Minister Kosiniak-Kamysz called it a deliberate provocation, Warsaw requested more NATO air defenses under Operation Eastern Sentry, and NATO chief Mark Rutte condemned "grave violations" of Polish airspace. Denmark expelled Russia's defense attaché over an alleged plot to kill a Ukrainian rapper and suspended non-essential visas for Russians.

In the Middle East, the US–Iran standoff over Hormuz has not moved. Foreign Minister Araghchi said on 21 November that Tehran could reopen the strait "on the second day" if Washington meets its commitments "on the first day." Parliament speaker Ghalibaf says the strait will "never return" to its pre-war status and that Iran is waiting for a US answer to its seven conditions. Qatar and Pakistan remain the main mediators. German Chancellor Merz used a Berlin visit by Qatar's emir to praise Doha's role and call for a "360-degree partnership." The Yemen front has escalated: the Saudi-led coalition struck Hodeidah port, and the Houthis claimed retaliatory strikes on southern Saudi Arabia, while missiles fired at Riyadh were intercepted.

In Israel, power is shifting. President Herzog gave the mandate to form a government to former army chief Gadi Eisenkot, who has 67 Knesset endorsements against Netanyahu's 46. Eisenkot is now weighing three routes: haredi support, Arab-party backing, or a unity deal. Avigdor Liberman's refusal to serve with Netanyahu in any form is the main obstacle, and Netanyahu stays caretaker prime minister until a new government is sworn in. Elsewhere, Britain and the EU are building on their reset one year after it began. In Asia, the US–China relationship is heading toward a third Trump–Xi meeting at the Miami G20 in mid-December. Analysts say the Shenzhen meeting brought more photos and process than substance.
