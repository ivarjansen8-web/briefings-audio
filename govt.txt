Daily Government Briefing — Thursday, 19 November 2026.

United Nations.

The race to succeed António Guterres, whose term ends on 31 December, dominates the Security Council's week. The Council's ninth straw poll for the next Secretary-General has reportedly been pushed from today to Friday, 20 November, and will use colour-coded ballots that show how the five permanent members vote. In the eighth poll on 5 November, Costa Rica's Rebeca Grynspan led with 12 "encourage" votes and 3 "discourage" votes, ahead of Rafael Grossi and Carolyn Rodrigues-Birkett, but a single permanent-member "discourage" can still block her. Latvia holds the Council presidency this month, with Yemen sanctions renewals and a high-level debate on emerging technologies on the programme. Guterres himself was in Manila for the ASEAN summit this week, where he pressed for Security Council reform and offered UN support for the Trump–Xi–Putin nuclear talks. On 16 November he also urged more than 120 states to negotiate a legally binding instrument banning "killer robots."

European Union.

Yesterday the Commission proposed a new 45 eurosbillion loan for Ukraine, backed by joint EU borrowing: 20 eurosbillion in financial aid and 25 eurosbillion for weapons such as drones, missiles and air-defence interceptors. The loan would cover part of a roughly 70 eurosbillion funding gap for 2027 and would be repaid only after Russia pays reparations. Ursula von der Leyen presented it alongside Commissioners Dombrovskis and Kubilius and said she was "very confident" all 27 member states would back it, even though Hungary, Czechia and Slovakia opted out of the earlier 90 eurosbillion loan. The proposal now needs approval from Parliament and the Council. Today, EU affairs ministers meet in Brussels for the General Affairs Council. They will discuss the Commission's 2027 work programme and the omnibus simplification packages, and hold country-specific rule-of-law discussions on Cyprus, Lithuania, Luxembourg and Hungary. The 31st accession conference with Montenegro is also scheduled today, alongside an Education and Youth Council and an informal meeting of consumer-protection ministers in Dublin. Earlier this week (16 November), EU foreign ministers accused Russia of stalling peace talks while it strikes Ukraine's power grid.

US House of Representatives.

The lame-duck House is mostly reorganizing for the 120th Congress. On Wednesday, House Democrats nominated Hakeem Jeffries for Speaker by a 251 to 11 recorded secret-ballot vote, which puts him on track to become the first Black Speaker when the full House votes on 3 January. Katherine Clark is the majority-leader nominee, Pete Aguilar the whip nominee, and Ted Lieu was elected the next caucus chair. Seven incoming members, five of them backed by the DSA, said publicly that they voted no. On the floor, legislative activity is thin. Rep. Thomas Massie accused Speaker Mike Johnson of cancelling "another day" of session this week, after the House voted on 10 November to table Massie's eight-article impeachment resolution against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. House Democrats plan to introduce an immigration bill today that would put a 10-year expiry on final removal orders for some immigrants without criminal records; it is not expected to move before January.

US Senate.

The Senate cleared the last 60-vote hurdle for the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA, H.R. 2347), the most sweeping permitting overhaul in decades. On Wednesday, it waived Sen. Jeff Merkley's budget point of order 70 to 26, adopted Sen. Shelley Moore Capito's substitute amendment 69 to 27, and invoked cloture on the bill 65 to 31. The bill would narrow reviews under NEPA, the National Historic Preservation Act, the Clean Water Act and the Endangered Species Act, and make data-center developers liable for the grid costs they create. Final passage needs only a simple majority and could come within about 30 hours of post-cloture time. Minority Leader Chuck Schumer was one of 14 Democrats who voted for cloture, and environmental groups are now calling on him to step down; he also faces a leadership challenge from Sen. Chris Van Hollen. The bill's prospects in the House are less certain, because Jeffries has raised objections on environmental and tribal-sovereignty grounds. Also on Wednesday, the HELP Committee held a vote on nominees including Heidi Overton for FDA Commissioner and James O'Neill for NSF Director, and the Judiciary Committee held a nominations hearing.

US Supreme Court.

The Court is between argument sittings, so no oral arguments or merits opinions are scheduled today; no merits decisions have been issued yet this term. On Wednesday, the Trump administration filed an emergency application asking the justices to block a lower-court ruling that keeps Temporary Protected Status in place for Venezuelan nationals. Monday's order list turned away several DEI-related disputes and a Second Amendment challenge to gun bans in "sensitive places." That came after last week's decision to hear a challenge to the administration's suspension of asylum. The next private conference is on 4 December. It will include high-profile petitions on the military's ban on transgender servicemembers (United States v. Talbott), Trump's appeal of the 83.3 dollarsmillion E. Jean Carroll verdict, and the legality of acting U.S. attorney appointments in Nevada and New York.

White House.

President Trump is in Shenzhen, China, for the APEC summit, the final leg of a three-day Asia trip that began with the ASEAN summit and a Quad leaders' meeting in Manila. Xi Jinping greeted him on the tarmac Wednesday. Today Trump addressed the APEC plenary, held a bilateral meeting and working lunch with Xi at Kylin Villa, and met Vladimir Putin separately; Trump called Putin "my friend" before the meeting, which lasted about 40 minutes to an hour. The Kremlin said afterward that Putin was in an "excellent mood," but no substance has been disclosed. The day ends with the first-ever trilateral meeting of the U.S., Chinese and Russian leaders. Trump has named nuclear arms reduction as its centrepiece, since New START lapsed in February, and has said the Iran war, trade and Ukraine are also on the table. Officials on all sides have played down expectations of concrete agreements. Trump has also floated a possible meeting with Kim Jong Un. Elon Musk is travelling with the delegation, and commercial deals in aviation, defence, energy and technology were expected to be announced along the way.

What's Coming Up.

The UN Security Council's colour-coded ninth straw poll on the next Secretary-General is set for Friday, 20 November, the same day as an EU–UK summit and an EU Culture and Sport Council. The Senate's final-passage vote on BAAJA could come as early as late Thursday or Friday. The bill would then go to a House where Republican Energy and Commerce chair Brett Guthrie wants changes, before both chambers break for Thanksgiving on 26 November. The European Parliament returns to Strasbourg on 23 to 26 November for a session with 28 votes and 15 debates; the agenda includes a debate on restoring the rule of law in Hungary after its 2026 elections, EU–Syria relations, and an amending budget for 2026. The Supreme Court's December sitting opens on 30 November with Crowther v. Board of Regents and Wassily v. Blanche, followed by the 4 December conference. Looming over everything is the 11 December government-funding deadline, for which no lame-duck deal has yet been reached.

International Politics Analysis.

The Shenzhen trilateral is the diplomatic event of the week, and the main question is whether it produces anything beyond symbolism. Analysts quoted by Al Jazeera and CNBC expect little more than a framework for future talks. Trump is the leader most eager for a presentable "win" on nuclear arms reduction now that New START has expired. Putin's priority is ending Russia's isolation and protecting his position on Ukraine. Xi is reluctant to be drawn into what Beijing sees as a European war, and China is rapidly expanding its own arsenal. The meeting also shows that Washington's political footing has shifted. Trump arrives after a midterm defeat that handed Democrats both chambers of Congress and was widely tied to the cost of the Iran war, and he faces criticism at home over Nvidia chip exports to China. Holding the Quad leaders' meeting in Manila just before Shenzhen, the first of his second term, was meant to reassure U.S. allies before the session with Beijing and Moscow.

On Ukraine, diplomacy is speeding up but has produced no breakthrough. The Abu Dhabi round on 10 to 11 November, the first direct Russia–Ukraine talks in nine months, yielded only a large prisoner exchange; Kyiv rejected a proposal to surrender all of Donbas. On 16 November, a full European negotiating team (France, Germany, the UK, Italy, Poland, the Commission, the European Council and NATO) joined U.S. envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner and Ukrainian officials for the first time. They agreed to meet next with the "Coalition of the Willing" in Berlin. Moscow has not accepted European participation, and the Kremlin has ruled out a temporary ceasefire. Strikes continue: Russia has hit bridges in Kyiv and the power grid, and Ukraine and Russia exchanged strikes overnight even as Putin met Trump. The EU's new 45 eurosbillion loan shows that Europe is planning for the war to continue into 2027.

The Gulf is the most dangerous front. After U.S. strikes on an Iranian island, Iran attacked Bahrain and a Kuwaiti base used by U.S. forces on 18 November, and a UAE state-oil vessel was hit in the Strait of Hormuz. Washington has reissued security warnings for Americans in the region. Meanwhile, the Saudi-led coalition struck about 60 targets in Sanaa, Hodeidah, Amran and Saada; the Houthis say the strikes killed at least 17 people, and the Houthis fired cruise missiles at Riyadh. This follows a Saudi-backed withdrawal that tightened Houthi control of the Bab el-Mandeb strait. Yemen's government has formally designated the Houthis a terrorist organisation, and Western officials say Riyadh is preparing for the war to last well into 2027. U.S.–Iran talks, which Vice President JD Vance has been involved in through indirect channels, remain stuck on sequencing: whether to deal first with Hormuz and sanctions relief or with Tehran's nuclear programme. Washington hopes Beijing will privately press Iran to compromise, which is one reason Iran is on the Shenzhen agenda.
